SACTCheck | Your STRIDE treatment
Tremelimumab (Imjudo) and durvalumab (Imfinzi) for advanced liver cancer
Irish NCCP regimen 00926 · Patient film transcript · November 2026

0:00   Your STRIDE treatment
If your treatment card says STRIDE, your first visit is different from the visits that follow. This film is for people starting tremelimumab and durvalumab for hepatocellular carcinoma, a cancer that starts in the liver. The aim is to control the cancer and help people live longer when surgery to remove it is not an option.

0:17   Why two medicines?
STRIDE stands for Single Tremelimumab, Regular Interval Durvalumab. Both medicines help immune cells attack cancer, but they act at different points. Tremelimumab, also called Imjudo, blocks a brake called CTLA-4 as immune cells become activated. Durvalumab, or Imfinzi, blocks a signal called PD-L1 that can switch off the attack. Tremelimumab is given once at the start. Durvalumab continues every four weeks.

0:45   The HIMALAYA trial
The study behind this treatment is called HIMALAYA. It was an international phase three trial in people with liver cancer who had not yet received drug treatment for advanced disease. Patients were assigned to treatment groups by chance. At three years, about thirty-one in every hundred were alive with STRIDE, compared with twenty in every hundred with sorafenib, a tablet treatment. These are group results. Your team can explain what they mean for you.

1:10   Your first visit
On day one, you receive both medicines through a drip into a vein. First comes tremelimumab over one hour. After that drip finishes, your team waits one to two hours before starting durvalumab. The durvalumab drip takes another hour. That makes about three to four hours from the start of the first drip to the end of the second. Allow extra time at the hospital for blood results, assessment, preparation and any further observation.

1:35   Four weeks later
Four weeks later, the next cycle begins. This time you receive just durvalumab, through a drip lasting about an hour. The plan repeats every four weeks while the treatment is helping and side effects are manageable. There is no fixed number of cycles. A visit may move if you are unwell or need more tests, so use the date your day ward gives you.

1:55   Checks before each cycle
Your team checks your blood count, kidneys, liver, blood sugar and thyroid before each cycle. They also check for hepatitis B and C before you start. With liver cancer, it matters how your blood results compare with your own starting levels. At each visit, mention any new symptom, even if it has settled. It helps the team decide whether it is safe to give the next dose.

2:16   If you feel unwell during a drip
A reaction can happen during a drip or shortly afterwards. In HIMALAYA, ten of the three hundred and eighty-eight people who received the combination had an infusion reaction. That is about three in a hundred. Tell the nurse immediately if you feel hot, shivery, itchy or dizzy, or if your throat feels tight or breathing becomes difficult. Staff can pause the infusion, check you and treat the reaction.

2:39   Changes to report at home
At home, you may notice tiredness, itchy skin, a rash, loose stools, or aching muscles and joints. These medicines can also make the immune system inflame healthy organs. A small change can be the first sign. Ring your oncology team promptly about new symptoms. If you develop a fever or suddenly feel unwell, ring the twenty-four-hour number straight away so the team can also check for infection.

3:03   Liver and bowel symptoms
Liver inflammation can first show up on a blood test. Ring urgently if the whites of your eyes or your skin turn yellow, your urine becomes dark, or your belly becomes more swollen. Your team will check for inflammation as well as other liver problems. For the bowel, ring about new diarrhoea, even if mild. Tell the team how often you are going and whether there is pain, blood or mucus.

3:26   Breathing, skin and kidneys
A new cough or shortness of breath may be a sign of lung inflammation. Ring your oncology team urgently, even between visits. Tell them about a new rash. Blisters, peeling skin or painful mouth sores need urgent assessment. The kidneys can also become inflamed. Passing much less urine than usual or developing new swelling is another reason to ring.

3:46   Hormones and other warning signs
Immunotherapy can affect glands that make hormones. Thyroid changes can cause tiredness or feeling unusually cold. Severe weakness, dizziness, vomiting or a persistent headache can mean that cortisol, a hormone your body needs, is low. Marked thirst and passing urine much more often can signal diabetes. Ring urgently about these symptoms, a racing heartbeat or new muscle weakness. Your team can arrange the right tests.

4:11   What your team can do
Reporting symptoms early gives your team time to find the cause. They may pause the next dose and use steroids to calm inflammation. Sometimes treatment needs to stop. A hormone problem may need hormone replacement, occasionally for life. If you are prescribed steroids, follow the plan your team gives you, including how to reduce them. Side effects can first appear months after your last infusion.

4:34   Keep your 24-hour number handy
Before you leave the day ward, save your hospital’s twenty-four-hour oncology number. Carry your immunotherapy alert card and show it if you see a GP or an emergency team. If you feel unwell and cannot reach oncology, seek urgent medical assessment. For severe breathing difficulty, severe chest pain or collapse, call one one two for an ambulance. You can ring for advice even after your treatment has finished.

On screen in the final chapter: In Ireland, call 112 or 999 for an ambulance in an emergency.
