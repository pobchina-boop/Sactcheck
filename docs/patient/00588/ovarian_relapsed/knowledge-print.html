Follow your own temperature and contact plan; do not wait for the next appointment. Severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, collapse, seizures or stroke symptoms: call 112/999.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

Understand your treatment

How your medicine works

Help prolong cancer control after platinum chemotherapy has worked again following a relapse, where a qualifying BRCA change is confirmed.

Targeted therapy: PARP inhibitor

Olaparib

Lynparza
Olaparib is a PARP inhibitor: it blocks proteins that help repair damaged deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA) and can trap them on the DNA. Cancer cells with a harmful BRCA1 or BRCA2 change already have difficulty using an important repair pathway. Blocking PARP can leave more damage than these cells can repair. Normal blood-forming cells can also be affected, which explains why blood counts and symptom checks remain important during targeted treatment.

What to watch for
Agree a written anti-sickness plan with your team, including what is prescribed regularly, what is for rescue, and when to call if it is not working. NCCP classifies oral olaparib as moderate-to-high sickness risk, but a fixed intravenous chemotherapy anti-sickness cycle does not apply. Some people need prescribed support and others do not need routine prevention. A stomach-acid medicine may be considered for symptoms under local policy.

Why this treatment is used

The treatment setting

NCCP requires BRCA-mutated disease, at least two platinum courses, a response lasting more than six months after the penultimate platinum regimen, and a complete or partial response to the latest course. The team checks fitness, the pretreatment CA-125 result/trend and no previous PARP-inhibitor treatment.

What your test result means

The Irish monotherapy pathway requires a qualifying BRCA mutation, inherited or tumour-derived. Prior PARP treatment is an exclusion in this NCCP regimen. BRCA1 and BRCA2 help repair damaged DNA. A germline change is present in the body’s cells and may have implications for relatives. A tumour-only change does not by itself show that it is inherited. Ask which test you had, what the result means and whether genetic counselling is appropriate. A variant of uncertain significance is not automatically a treatment-qualifying result.

SOLO2: maintenance after platinum-sensitive relapse

SOLO2 compared olaparib tablets with placebo in 295 people with BRCA-mutated, platinum-sensitive relapsed disease after at least two platinum courses.

The primary analysis found median time without progression or death of 19.1 months with olaparib versus 5.5 months with placebo. In the final overall-survival analysis, the difference did not meet the prespecified conventional statistical-significance threshold. A median is a group midpoint, not a deadline for you.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Before the first dose

Confirm a qualifying BRCA result, response to the latest platinum, earlier platinum-treatment history and CA-125 trend. Check recovery, full blood count, kidney/liver tests, medicines and pregnancy testing where relevant.

STEP 2

How maintenance fits after chemotherapy

This is maintenance after a later platinum response, not a repeat course after olaparib failure. NCCP specifies completing the latest platinum within the preceding eight weeks. Your team confirms the start date once blood counts and other effects have recovered sufficiently.

STEP 3

A practical home routine

Swallow the prescribed tablets whole with or without food, once in the morning and once in the evening. Do not chew, crush, dissolve or divide them. The usual starting dose is two 150 mg tablets each time (300 mg twice daily); your own dose may be lower. Check both tablet strength and tablet count after every prescription change. If you miss a dose, take the next normal dose at its usual time; do not double up. Do not substitute olaparib capsules for tablets. Avoid grapefruit juice and check other grapefruit or bitter-orange products with your pharmacist. Keep enough supply for weekends, holidays and the next review. Olaparib itself needs no infusion pump or port.

STEP 4

The written support plan

Agree a written anti-sickness plan with your team, including what is prescribed regularly, what is for rescue, and when to call if it is not working. NCCP classifies oral olaparib as moderate-to-high sickness risk, but a fixed intravenous chemotherapy anti-sickness cycle does not apply. Some people need prescribed support and others do not need routine prevention. A stomach-acid medicine may be considered for symptoms under local policy. Give the pharmacist a complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal products. Some antibiotics, antifungals, seizure medicines and St John’s wort can change how much olaparib reaches your body. Even anti-sickness treatment needs checking: aprepitant can increase olaparib exposure. Do not start a borrowed support medicine, change the olaparib dose or restart a paused course yourself.

STEP 5

If treatment is paused

Ask why it is paused, which tests are next and when the team will contact you. Only the prescriber can confirm the restart dose. Do not take catch-up tablets.

STEP 6

Reviewing benefit and the next step

Treatment continues while the cancer remains controlled and side effects remain acceptable. There is no automatic two-year endpoint for this relapsed-maintenance pathway; the first-line ovarian two-year rule does not apply. The team reviews symptoms, scans and any relevant CA-125 trend together. CA-125 is not a standalone instruction to change tablets. The consultant sets the response-assessment timetable and continuation decisions.

STEP 8

Before leaving the clinic

Confirm the next tests, review, tablet supply and verified daytime/out-of-hours contact numbers. Ask what to do if a result or prescription has not arrived.

Checks and cancer review

Blood safety tests, symptom reviews and cancer assessments answer different questions.

Ongoing safety

Full blood count, kidney and liver tests every four weeks for the first 12 months, then as clinically indicated. Review symptoms, food/fluid intake, medicines and tablet supply; abnormal results or new symptoms can require earlier checks. Blood counts check red cells that carry oxygen, white cells that help fight infection and platelets that help stop bleeding. Low counts can lead to a pause, a lower prescribed dose, a transfusion or further tests. Persistent or unexplained changes sometimes need specialist bone-marrow tests to investigate myelodysplastic syndrome or acute myeloid leukaemia (MDS/AML). These are serious conditions; their risk differs by treatment setting and previous therapy. A treatment pause is not itself a diagnosis.

Other tests

There is no universal olaparib-only electrocardiogram (ECG), echo or bone-density timetable in this regimen. Tests for previous treatments, existing disease or new symptoms remain part of the individual plan.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Blood counts and extra support

Blood counts check red cells that carry oxygen, white cells that help fight infection and platelets that help stop bleeding. Low counts can lead to a pause, a lower prescribed dose, a transfusion or further tests. Persistent or unexplained changes sometimes need specialist bone-marrow tests to investigate myelodysplastic syndrome or acute myeloid leukaemia (MDS/AML). These are serious conditions; their risk differs by treatment setting and previous therapy. A treatment pause is not itself a diagnosis. There is no automatic white-cell injection, antibiotic or steroid course for everyone.

Eating, drinking and daily life

Ask for help early if nausea, mouth soreness, taste changes or poor appetite affect eating. Keep the team informed about weight loss or difficulty drinking. Fatigue may need a dose or blood-count review. Do not drive or use machinery if dizzy, weak or very tired.

A treatment pause

A prescribed pause can give blood counts or symptoms time to improve. It does not mean treatment has failed. Keep the agreed tests and ask exactly when the team will review restarting. Never take catch-up tablets.

Support for your wider treatment

Keep pain, nutrition, bowel and symptom-support plans under review. Several previous treatments can affect energy and blood-count recovery, so describe what is new or changing. Persistent blood abnormalities may need specialist assessment rather than repeated routine dose changes.

Fertility, contraception and breastfeeding

Olaparib can harm an unborn baby. Discuss fertility and pregnancy prevention before treatment. If you could become pregnant, use two reliable complementary contraception methods during treatment and for six months after the last dose, following your team’s advice. A non-hormonal method may be needed because olaparib can reduce the effect of some hormonal contraceptives; hormone-sensitive cancer also affects the choice. Pregnancy testing is needed before treatment and may be repeated. Do not breastfeed during olaparib or for one month after the last dose. Tell the team immediately if pregnancy is possible.

Daily treatment and the full course

The reference month helps organise tablets and appointments; the treatment endpoint is a separate decision.

A pause or a different dose

Kidney function, side effects and interacting medicines can change the prescription. A lower dose or pause is an individual safety decision. Do not copy someone else’s dose or restart because a calendar date has passed.

Prescribed support and daily life

Use the medicine plan supplied by your own oncology team.

Symptoms that need action

A new symptom can have several causes; contact the team so it can be assessed.

Lungs and blood clots

New or worsening cough, breathlessness, chest pain or a painful swollen limb can signal infection, lung inflammation or a clot. Do not assume these symptoms are routine fatigue.

Liver and blood changes

Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, unexplained severe fatigue, infections, bruising or bleeding need prompt advice. Persistent symptoms still matter during a treatment break or after treatment ends.

Evidence and your choices

Trial results describe groups and should support a discussion about your priorities.

What the comparison cannot tell us

The study supports disease-control benefit in this selected relapsed setting. Its findings cannot be substituted for first-line results. Later therapy, including PARP treatment in some placebo patients, affects interpretation of survival; longer follow-up also matters for marrow safety.

Your priorities

Ask about expected benefit, side effects, alternatives including symptom-focused care, and choosing to decline or stop treatment. Decisions can be revisited as your health or priorities change.

Additional phase-specific support

  • NCCP classifies continuous oral olaparib as moderate-to-high sickness potential and asks the team to consider anti-sickness medicine and a proton pump inhibitor under local policy. The Lynparza SmPC states that routine antiemetic prophylaxis is not required. Agree an individual prevention/rescue plan; this is not a mandatory multidrug prescription.
  • Continuous oral treatment has no Day 1–4 intravenous-chemotherapy antiemetic cycle. Confirm every selected medicine, dose, frequency and duration. There is no automatic daily steroid prescription.
  • Aprepitant is a moderate CYP3A inhibitor in the NCCP regimen and can increase olaparib exposure. The prescriber/pharmacist must review interactions before choosing anti-sickness medicines; do not import another regimen’s antiemetic bundle.
  • Consider a proton pump inhibitor for appropriate symptoms or local indications. It is not a compulsory medicine for everyone taking olaparib.
  • Low blood counts may require an olaparib pause, a reviewed dose change, transfusion or investigation. Routine granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (white-cell growth support) (G-CSF), antibiotics, anticoagulation or steroids are not automatically prescribed solely because olaparib is being taken.
  • Check all prescription, over-the-counter and herbal products. Avoid grapefruit juice. Strong/moderate CYP3A inhibitors or inducers and other transporter interactions need pharmacy review; the example list is not a complete interaction check.
  • Agree what to do if nausea persists, fluids or tablets cannot be retained, or diarrhoea affects intake. Contact the treatment team promptly instead of independently changing the olaparib or supportive-medicine doses.
  • Relapsed BRCA-mutated ovarian maintenance: continue until progression or unacceptable toxicity; no automatic two-year cap.

Before signing: verify allergies, current medicines, interactions, renal / hepatic function, QT risk, sedation, prior response, total steroid exposure and the rescue-medicine duration. Do not duplicate medicines from another phase.
