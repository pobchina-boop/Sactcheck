Understand your treatment

How the medicines work

For unresectable or metastatic gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST), imatinib aims to control disease and symptoms. After removal of higher-risk GIST, adjuvant treatment aims to reduce the chance of recurrence. These are different treatment goals and must be clearly recorded.

Oral targeted therapy: tyrosine kinase inhibitor

Imatinib

Glivec
Blocks selected growth-signal proteins

Imatinib blocks certain kinase proteins, including KIT and platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha, that can drive susceptible gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) cells. The exact tumour mutation affects sensitivity; a KIT-positive label alone does not replace specialist molecular review. Normal tissues can also be affected.

What to watch for
Report rapid weight gain, swelling, new breathlessness, fever, unusual bleeding, severe rash or yellow eyes promptly. Keep blood tests even if you feel well.

Your treatment

The schedule

The usual National Cancer Control Programme starting dose is 400 mg by mouth every day with food. For progression in advanced disease, a specialist may consider 800 mg daily as 400 mg twice daily. Adjuvant treatment after resection is described as up to 3 years in this National Cancer Control Programme document; your specialist confirms duration using current risk and evidence. The 28-day graphic is a reference view, not a treatment break or fixed course.

Taking the medicine

Take the prescribed dose with a meal and a large glass of water. Take doses consistently. If swallowing is difficult, ask the pharmacy about the exact supplied product before changing how it is taken.

Other treatment and follow-up

gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) is a distinct tumour type. A specialist team reviews pathology, KIT/platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha and other relevant molecular findings, prior treatment, symptoms and scans. Surgery may be appropriate for resectable disease or selected situations. Radiotherapy has a selective role, rather than being a routine partner to these tablets. Nutrition, symptom control and supportive or palliative care can be used alongside active cancer treatment.

SSGXVIII/AIO: three versus one year of adjuvant imatinib

An early study in advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) demonstrated tumour responses and disease control with imatinib; it was not an adjuvant study. Separately, in high-risk GIST after surgery, three years of adjuvant imatinib improved recurrence-free and overall survival compared with one year at long follow-up. Keep these treatment settings distinct.

The National Cancer Control Programme document describes up to three years after surgery. Later IMADGIST evidence supports disease-free survival benefit from extending treatment in selected high-risk patients, so three years should not be labelled universally optimal or imposed as an automatic stop. Advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST) requires its own evidence and continuation discussion.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Before starting

Confirm whether treatment is for advanced disease or after surgery, and the exact daily dose. Review all prescribed, over-the-counter and herbal products; avoid grapefruit and St John’s wort. Never increase the dose after a scan or a missed dose without the team.

STEP 3

Missed or vomited doses

If you miss an imatinib dose, take it when remembered unless it is nearly time for the next dose; then skip it and return to the usual schedule. Never double a dose. Ask the team what to do after vomiting; do not take a replacement dose without advice.

STEP 4

Supply and handling

Keep tablets or capsules in their supplied packaging, away from children, and follow the pharmacy storage instructions. Do not share medicines or alter the dose yourself. Ask the pharmacy before crushing, opening or dispersing a medicine; formulation-specific instructions differ. Return unused treatment to the pharmacy.

STEP 5

Monitoring and next review

Full blood count every 2 weeks for the first 12 weeks, then monthly. Kidney and liver tests monthly. Review weight, swelling, breathlessness, rash, bowel symptoms and interactions; additional tests depend on findings. Imaging follows the individual disease or recurrence-monitoring plan.

Tests and reviews

Use the test-specific cards in the monitoring section.

Your testing appointments

Confirm where and when tests are done and who reviews the results before treatment continues.

Safety and benefit

Normal safety tests do not establish that treatment is working. Your specialist sets the separate scan and symptom-review plan.

Questions worth asking

What is the treatment trying to do?

For unresectable or metastatic gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST), imatinib aims to control disease and symptoms. After removal of higher-risk GIST, adjuvant treatment aims to reduce the chance of recurrence. These are different treatment goals and must be clearly recorded. GIST is a distinct tumour type. A specialist team reviews pathology, KIT/platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha and other relevant molecular findings, prior treatment, symptoms and scans. Surgery may be appropriate for resectable disease or selected situations. Radiotherapy has a selective role, rather than being a routine partner to these tablets. Nutrition, symptom control and supportive or palliative care can be used alongside active cancer treatment.

Does a delay mean treatment has failed?

No. Blood counts, organ function and symptoms can make a delay or dose change safer. Your team decides whether and when to give the next dose.

Who do I contact if unwell?

Use your verified oncology daytime or out-of-hours number. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Severe symptoms require 112/999.

Why do I need checks if I feel well?

High blood pressure, thyroid changes, liver injury or low blood counts may be detected before you notice symptoms. The required checks differ by medicine; keep your individual schedule.
