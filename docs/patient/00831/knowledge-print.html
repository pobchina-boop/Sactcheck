When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call your oncology team immediately for new breathlessness, persistent diarrhoea, blood or black stools, yellow eyes, dark urine, severe tiredness or dizziness, thirst and frequent urination, unusual weakness, reduced urine or a spreading rash. Tell every healthcare professional that you receive immunotherapy, even after it stops. Vomiting blood, heavy bleeding, sudden severe abdominal or chest pain, stroke symptoms, seizure, collapse or major breathing difficulty need 112 / 999.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Two medicines with different effects and safety checks

This combination aims to control hepatocellular carcinoma, a cancer that starts in the liver, when it is advanced or cannot be removed and no previous systemic treatment has been given. Atezolizumab helps immune cells recognise cancer; bevacizumab blocks a signal that supports blood-vessel growth.

PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor

Atezolizumab

Tecentriq; intravenous (IV) infusion or thigh injection
Helps immune cells recognise and attack cancer

Blocking PD-L1 can release a brake on the immune response. This may control cancer but can also cause inflammation in healthy organs during treatment or after it stops.

PD-L1 blockade↓Immune response increases↓Cancer and healthy tissues may be affected

What to watch for
Carry the immunotherapy alert card. Report new bowel, breathing, skin, hormone, heart, liver, kidney or neurological symptoms promptly.

Anti-VEGF antibody

Bevacizumab

Avastin or prescribed biosimilar; IV infusion
Blocks a signal for blood-vessel growth

Bevacizumab binds VEGF, reducing signals that help tumours develop blood vessels. It can also affect normal blood vessels, blood pressure, kidney filtration and wound healing.

VEGF signal blocked↓Tumour vessel growth reduced↓Blood vessels and healing need monitoring

What to watch for
Report bleeding, severe headache, chest or abdominal pain, leg swelling, reduced urine or poor wound healing. Tell the team before surgery or dental procedures.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Treatment day

IV atezolizumab is usually infused over 60 minutes initially and may take 30 minutes later if tolerated. The alternative 1875 mg thigh injection takes about seven minutes. Bevacizumab is still an IV infusion: usually 90 minutes first, then 60 and later 30 minutes if tolerated. Checks, preparation and observation make the whole visit longer. Report any reaction immediately.

Day 2–20: Between visits

Continue prescribed support, note symptoms and carry the immune alert card. Immune and blood-vessel effects can happen between appointments. Use the urgent contact plan; do not wait for the next cycle.

Day 21: Review before another cycle

Review symptoms, blood and urine results, blood pressure, liver health and benefit. Thyroid tests are scheduled every six weeks. Confirm each medicine and any changes.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Varices and bleeding

Screening and any required variceal treatment must be reviewed before starting. Call urgently for black stools or blood; vomiting blood or heavy bleeding needs emergency help.

Blood pressure and urine

Blood pressure and urine-protein checks are part of each cycle. Continue prescribed blood-pressure medicines and ask before changing them. A positive urine dipstick may need a timed urine collection.

Immune alert card

Carry the supplied immunotherapy alert card during treatment and afterwards. New symptoms can need urgent treatment; do not wait for routine blood tests.

Support medicines

Both medicines have minimal sickness risk and routine preventive anti-sickness medicines or premedication are not usually required. The team individualises symptom treatment; do not self-treat new significant diarrhoea without advice.

Surgery, dental work and healing

Tell oncology before planned procedures and tell your dentist about bevacizumab. Wounds must heal and the team must agree an interruption/restart plan.

Pregnancy prevention and breastfeeding

Discuss fertility and pregnancy prevention. Atezolizumab requires effective contraception for five months after the last dose and bevacizumab for six months; use the longer interval when both apply. Do not breastfeed during treatment or for at least six months after bevacizumab.

Treatment reference · 01

Understanding the combination

Confirm the treatment aim and source eligibility.

Treatment aim

This combination aims to control hepatocellular carcinoma, a cancer that starts in the liver, when it is advanced or cannot be removed and no previous systemic treatment has been given. Atezolizumab helps immune cells recognise cancer; bevacizumab blocks a signal that supports blood-vessel growth.

Liver health matters

The team checks liver function, cirrhosis, viral-hepatitis management and any ascites or confusion. The pivotal study mainly supports patients with Child-Pugh A liver function; poorer liver function needs specialist discussion.

Two medicine decisions

There is no fixed total number of cycles. Clinical benefit, liver function, bleeding risk, immune effects and your preferences guide treatment. One medicine may be paused or stopped while the other is considered separately by the team.

Treatment reference · 02

Preparing for the first treatment

Bleeding-risk review is a separate safety step.

Varices

Screening and any required variceal treatment must be reviewed before starting. Call urgently for black stools or blood; vomiting blood or heavy bleeding needs emergency help.

Baseline checks

Blood count, kidneys, liver, glucose, thyroid function, urine protein, blood pressure and cardiac history are reviewed. Other tests depend on symptoms, earlier treatment and medicines.

Consent and route

Discuss expected benefit and uncertainty, alternatives including not proceeding, liver disease and variceal bleeding, immune toxicity that may be delayed or need lifelong hormone replacement, vascular/kidney/bowel/healing risks, fertility and pregnancy prevention, IV versus thigh administration and your right to reconsider.

Treatment reference · 03

Your treatment visit

The medicines follow a defined sequence.

IV or thigh atezolizumab

IV atezolizumab is usually infused over 60 minutes initially and may take 30 minutes later if tolerated. The alternative 1875 mg thigh injection takes about seven minutes. Bevacizumab is still an IV infusion: usually 90 minutes first, then 60 and later 30 minutes if tolerated. Checks, preparation and observation make the whole visit longer.

During administration

Tell the nurse immediately about rash, swelling, breathlessness, chest tightness, dizziness or pain at the injection or infusion site. Do not wait until the visit ends.

Before every cycle

Blood count, kidney/liver profile, glucose and urine protein are checked before every cycle. Blood pressure is checked before each cycle and after treatment. Thyroid tests are scheduled every six weeks.

Treatment reference · 05

Follow-up after treatment

Continue the contact and alert-card plan.

Delayed immune effects

No. They can begin or persist afterwards. Keep the immunotherapy alert card and report new symptoms, including to doctors outside oncology.

Continuing support

The team follows ongoing liver, kidney, blood-pressure and hormonal problems and agrees medicine stop dates and next cancer reviews.

Pregnancy prevention

Discuss fertility and pregnancy prevention. Atezolizumab requires effective contraception for five months after the last dose and bevacizumab for six months; use the longer interval when both apply. Do not breastfeed during treatment or for at least six months after bevacizumab.

Evidence in previously untreated unresectable HCC

IMbrave150 compared atezolizumab plus bevacizumab with sorafenib in selected people with HCC and preserved liver function.

The combination improved survival and time before cancer progressed in the study population. This does not predict an individual outcome or establish the same benefit for more severe liver dysfunction.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Does having more side effects mean treatment is working?

No. Side effects do not reliably show whether the cancer is responding. Report them so they can be treated. Your team assesses benefit using the tests and review plan appropriate to your cancer.

Why might a dose be delayed or reduced?

Your team may allow recovery, investigate a symptom or reduce the chance of lasting harm. Ask which drug changes, why, and when review is planned. A delay does not itself mean treatment has failed.

What should I say when calling?

Give the regimen name, last treatment date, symptoms and when they started. Explain whether you can eat, drink, pass urine and manage usual activities. Give your temperature if available, but do not delay calling to collect details.

What belongs in the consent conversation?

The individual aim and likely benefit, uncertainty, reasonable alternatives including not proceeding, common and serious risks, lasting effects, fertility and practical concerns. You can ask for an interpreter, more time or another explanation and can reconsider your decision.

How many cycles should I use this passport for?

The passport provides reusable recording pages, not a prescribed course length. Your oncology team sets the duration and can provide extra pages.

Can I use this page to decide if blood results are safe?

No. Treatment decisions require the clinical protocol, symptoms, previous toxicity, organ function and the prescriber’s review. The patient page explains the checks and does not authorise treatment.

Why do I need a check for varices?

Liver disease can cause enlarged veins in the food pipe or stomach that may bleed. Bevacizumab can increase bleeding risk. The team checks for varices and treats any relevant risk before starting; having the test alone does not mean the risk has been controlled.

Does an abnormal liver test always mean immune hepatitis?

No. Liver cancer, underlying liver disease, infection, blocked bile flow and medicines can all affect results. The team compares results with your baseline and investigates the cause before deciding how each medicine changes.

Can one medicine be held while the other continues?

Sometimes. The reason for holding treatment, your general condition and the source rules for each medicine determine the plan. Never assume that stopping one means the other is automatically safe to continue.

Do immune side effects stop when treatment stops?

No. They can begin or persist afterwards. Keep the immunotherapy alert card and report new symptoms, including to doctors outside oncology.

Does the thigh injection replace every drip?

No. Atezolizumab may be given into the thigh, but bevacizumab remains an infusion into a vein. The two atezolizumab formulations have different doses and must be prescribed correctly.
