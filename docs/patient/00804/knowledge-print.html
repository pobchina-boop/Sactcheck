Understand your treatment

How your medicines work

Lomustine chemotherapy and bevacizumab targeted therapy are used together to help control recurrent glioblastoma. Your team discusses likely benefits, risks, symptoms and quality-of-life goals.

Oral chemotherapy capsule

Lomustine

CCNU
Damages deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA)

Lomustine is an oral nitrosourea chemotherapy that can reach the brain. It damages DNA and interferes with cell division. Its effects on healthy blood-forming cells can appear several weeks after the dose and can build up over successive cycles. This explains the long gap between doses and the need for continuing blood tests.

One scheduled dose↓DNA damaged↓Delayed blood-count effects possible

What to watch for
This is one dose on Day 1, not a daily medicine. If an extra dose is taken, get urgent advice even if you feel well. Keep blood tests after the dose and report fever, bruising, bleeding, new cough or breathlessness.

Targeted antibody given into a vein

Bevacizumab

Avastin / prescribed biosimilar
Blocks the blood-vessel growth signal VEGF

Bevacizumab binds VEGF, a signal involved in making and maintaining blood vessels. Blocking this signal can change the blood supply and leakiness of tumour vessels. It can also affect normal vessels, which helps explain high blood pressure, protein in the urine, bleeding, clots and slower wound healing. A scan change does not by itself prove that the tumour has stopped growing.

VEGF signal blocked↓Tumour vessels affected↓Response and safety reviewed

What to watch for
Report bleeding, severe abdominal pain, new chest or breathing symptoms, sudden neurological changes, headaches with visual changes, and any operation or wound problem. Keep blood-pressure and urine checks.

Complete timing and symptom notes

Day 1: Two medicines, different routes

Lomustine is a single oral dose; bevacizumab is an infusion. Keep the anti-sickness plan for lomustine. Confirm cycle number and the exact capsule supply before taking it.

Day 2–42: Separate infusion visits and continuing checks

Return for bevacizumab on Day 22. No extra lomustine is scheduled. Continue blood-pressure, urine and blood checks. Report symptoms and planned surgery; each medicine has its own safety decision.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Taking the treatment

Take lomustine only once on the authorised Day 1. The pharmacist must confirm the total dose, capsule strengths, how to take it and anti-sickness timing. Do not open or crush capsules or take another dose to replace a missed or vomited one without advice. Bevacizumab is given into a vein. The usual first infusion lasts 90 minutes; later infusions may take 60 and then 30 minutes if tolerated. Checks and observation add time. Report flushing, rash, breathing difficulty, dizziness or other new symptoms immediately.

STEP 2

Missed, vomited or held medicine

If a capsule dose is missed or vomited, call the oncology team or pharmacist before taking more. Do not double up or use leftover capsules. An infusion or capsule held for toxicity needs a new team decision.

STEP 3

Medicine and supplement review

Bring all prescription, over-the-counter and herbal medicines, vitamins and supplements to review. Include steroids, seizure medicines, anticoagulants and any recent antibiotics or antifungals. Tell the team before starting or stopping a medicine.

STEP 4

Surgery, procedures and wounds

Tell all treating teams that you receive bevacizumab. Major surgery needs an appropriate treatment interval and a fully healed wound before restarting; minor and dental procedures also need individual advice. Do not decide timing yourself or rely on the wallet stamp.

STEP 5

Capsule supply and handling

Lomustine is supplied as an unlicensed medicine in this NCCP protocol. Confirm pharmacy supply in advance. Keep the labelled pack away from children and do not open or crush capsules. Return unused chemotherapy to the pharmacy; contact staff if swallowing or handling is difficult.

STEP 6

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and fertility

Discuss fertility preservation and contraception before starting. Treatment can harm a pregnancy. Do not breastfeed during treatment. Ask for the written medicine-specific precautions and the required periods after stopping; these may differ between products.

STEP 7

Appointments and transport

Before starting: Blood count, kidney/liver tests, glucose, urine protein, blood pressure and cardiac history/examination; NCCP lung testing for patients at high risk. Current lomustine SmPC additionally advises baseline lung-function assessment. During treatment: Regular blood count, kidney/liver tests and urine protein; blood pressure before each bevacizumab dose and after treatment. Current lomustine SmPC advises weekly blood counts for at least six weeks after a dose and continuing lung assessment. MRI and neurological review follow your individual treatment plan. Echocardiography is considered only when indicated, including previous anthracycline treatment or chest-wall radiotherapy. If taking warfarin, international normalised ratio (blood-clotting test) (INR) is checked weekly until stable and before subsequent cycles.

STEP 8

Help with the daily plan

Agree help with prescriptions, travel, calls and symptom notes if fatigue, memory, sight or weakness makes these difficult. Your preferences guide who is involved. Keep the printed plan and real urgent contacts together.

STEP 9

Off-label treatment and infusion decisions

Bevacizumab for glioblastoma is outside its licensed indication in Ireland. The team should explain the reason for using it, expected benefit, uncertainty and alternatives as part of consent. Any rapid infusion outside the standard schedule has a separate local consent and governance process.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Lomustine nausea prevention

Lomustine has moderate-to-high oral emetic potential. Take the prescribed prevention and rescue plan around the single dose. Contact the team if nausea or vomiting prevents fluids or essential medicines; do not automatically repeat a vomited chemotherapy dose.

Bevacizumab support is separate

Bevacizumab alone has minimal emetic potential. Routine anti-sickness drugs or steroid premedication are not automatically required for every infusion; the team may prescribe specific medicines after a previous reaction or for another reason.

Blood pressure, urine and wound care

Keep prescribed blood-pressure medicine and urine checks. Follow the team’s plan for any home measurements. Report wounds and upcoming surgery or dental treatment early, so timing and healing can be assessed before another infusion.

Separate steroid, seizure and bowel plans

Keep prescribed steroids and seizure medicines on their own schedules. Ask about missed or vomited doses. Agree a bowel plan if other medicines cause constipation and check interactions before adding a medicine.

Your schedule

Each 42-day cycle has lomustine capsules taken once on Day 1 and bevacizumab infusions on Days 1 and 22. Up to six cycles are planned, with review before each treatment.

Evidence and uncertainty

Adding bevacizumab delayed measured progression in the trial but did not improve overall survival. Treatment goals and burdens need an individual discussion. The trial used bevacizumab 10 mg/kg every two weeks, not this NCCP 7.5 mg/kg every three weeks schedule. No trial survival or response number is assigned to this dose schedule. Trial lomustine dose escalation and treatment duration do not replace the current NCCP regimen. Historical pathology, patient selection and later treatments limit individual prediction.

Consent and decisions

Discuss the exact diagnosis, treatment goal, evidence limits, alternatives and burdens; medicine-specific risks, monitoring, fertility and pregnancy precautions; any unlicensed supply or off-label use; and practical support. Keep steroid and seizure plans separate from anticancer treatment.

Treatment reference · 04

Understanding your particular glioma

Your diagnosis includes the tumour’s cells, molecular findings and behaviour, not just the word brain cancer.

The name and location

A glioma is a tumour arising in the brain or spinal cord. Astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma and glioblastoma are different types. Cancer that travels to the brain from another organ is a brain metastasis and follows a different pathway.

Grade is not the same as stage

Glioma reports combine the tumour type, grade and molecular findings. Grade is not a measure of spread around the body: a grade 4 brain tumour does not mean body-wide metastases. Ask your specialist to explain older terms in your records.

isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and 1p/19q

Changes in the IDH genes and loss of chromosome regions 1p and 19q help classify certain gliomas. The pattern of results matters. These are usually tumour-sample tests; they do not by themselves show that a change runs in the family.

O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation

The MGMT gene makes a protein that repairs a particular type of DNA damage. Methylation in its control region can reduce that repair and inform discussion of temozolomide benefit. This is a tumour-tissue result, not a blood marker or a personal survival percentage.

A useful diagnosis record

Keep the exact pathology diagnosis, sample date, grade and available molecular results together. Label a result as pending or not tested when appropriate. Ask whether further pathology review would affect the plan.

Treatment reference · 05

Tests and scans: what each one tells you

Safety blood tests, tumour tissue tests and MRI answer different questions.

Safety blood tests

A full blood count measures red cells, white cells and platelets. Kidney and liver tests assess other aspects of treatment safety. Follow the blood-test schedule even when you feel well or the scan is reassuring.

MRI and neurological review

MRI gives detailed pictures of the brain. The team also checks symptoms, movement, speech, thinking and daily function. Before scanning, tell staff about implants, metal, kidney problems or previous contrast reactions, and ask for help if the scan worries you.

Changes on a scan need interpretation

Treatment-related changes can resemble tumour growth on MRI; this is called pseudoprogression. The specialist interprets the scan with symptoms and may need another scan or further assessment. Report worsening symptoms promptly rather than assuming a scan change is harmless.

An individual follow-up timetable

Record the next MRI and the contact who will explain it. Your diagnosis, treatment phase and previous results determine the interval. Other scans or blood markers are ordered for a specific reason, not automatically before every cycle.

Keep results with decisions

Mark tests as booked, done and reviewed so the next action is clear. Contact the named service about a missing result. Do not change chemotherapy or steroids in response to a report without instructions.

Treatment reference · 06

Brain symptoms, medicines and when to call

Symptoms may reflect the tumour, swelling, treatment, another medicine or a separate illness.

Know your usual symptoms

Before treatment, note your usual walking, strength, speech, sight, memory, sleep and seizure pattern. Record new changes and their timing. A trusted person can help if you want them involved.

Urgent neurological changes

Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice, including on treatment rest days. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. Follow the emergency instructions in any established seizure rescue plan.

Steroids need a separate written plan

Steroids may reduce swelling around a brain tumour. If needed, ask for a written dose, review date and reduction plan; do not stop suddenly or adjust the dose yourself. Report infection symptoms, marked thirst, weakness or troublesome sleep or mood changes.

Seizure medicines and rescue instructions

Take prescribed seizure medicine on its own schedule. Ask what to do after a missed or vomited dose and how to manage a breakthrough seizure. Do not switch or stop it yourself. Anyone giving a rescue medicine needs the personal instructions and training.

Safety at home and outside

Discuss driving, work, bathing, swimming, heights and machinery with your team, taking account of symptoms and seizures. Individual medical and licensing advice is needed. Occupational therapy can help make daily routines safer.

Treatment reference · 07

Your overall treatment plan and choices

Treatment should account for the tumour and the life you want to lead.

Understand the sequence

Place biopsy or surgery, radiotherapy, medicines and follow-up on one plan. Concurrent means treatments overlap; adjuvant means treatment follows the main treatment. A new phase can require different doses, tests and support medicines.

Agree what treatment is trying to achieve

Ask what treatment can reasonably achieve for your tumour and which effects matter most to you. Discuss benefits, burdens, alternatives and the reasons for a pause or stop.

Put evidence in context

A study result applies to a particular group, diagnosis and schedule. Older studies may use different tumour names. Ask which evidence fits your plan; an average cannot forecast your response or lifespan.

Discuss options available to you

Ask about suitable trials, another specialist opinion and other treatment or symptom-care options. Your multidisciplinary team explains what is appropriate and available for you.

Make room for your priorities

Bring up thinking, independence, family time, work and the side effects you most want to avoid. Ask for time, written notes or a supporter if that helps you weigh the choices.

Treatment reference · 08

Living well, rehabilitation and support

Practical and symptom support can begin alongside anticancer treatment.

Thinking, communication and daily tasks

Tell the team about changes in memory, language, attention, sight or planning. Rehabilitation can involve physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy or psychology. Ask for practical help with routines and medicines.

Food, nausea, bowels and energy

Use your prescribed nausea and bowel plan and report problems early. Ask for dietitian help if intake or weight falls. Adapt movement to your balance and strength, and ask the team to assess fatigue that disrupts daily life.

Emotional support and carers

Tell the team about changes in mood, behaviour or coping. Patients and carers may each need psychological or practical support. Agree who can receive information and ask for help before caring becomes overwhelming.

Work, transport and finances

Tell the nurse or social worker about travel, employment, finances and caring responsibilities. Arrange practical help with medicines or appointments if symptoms make those tasks difficult.

Supportive care and future wishes

Supportive and palliative care can help with symptoms and quality of life while active treatment continues. You can discuss future care preferences and who you want involved in decisions at a time that suits you. Ask the team for advice from your team and documentation rather than using legal or hospice rules from a US booklet.

Information that works on paper

Keep the latest paper schedule, medicine list, results plan and emergency contacts together. The treatment card helps others identify your regimen. It supports, but does not replace, the prescription and emergency instructions.
