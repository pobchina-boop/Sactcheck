Understand your treatment

Your medicines and what they do

This weekly paclitaxel protocol is used in specified advanced breast, ovarian, small-cell lung and bladder-cancer settings. The aim is usually disease control and symptom relief. Evidence and alternatives differ between cancers; confirm the indication with your oncologist.

Taxane chemotherapy

Paclitaxel

Taxol (conventional paclitaxel)
Locks the cell division machinery

Cells use tiny structures called microtubules to separate their chromosomes when dividing. Paclitaxel stabilises these structures so they cannot rearrange normally, interrupting cell division. It can also affect nerves and other healthy tissues.

Microtubules form↓Structures remain locked↓Division is interrupted

What to watch for
Report tingling or numbness before the next weekly dose, especially if it affects walking or hand use. Tell the nurse immediately about flushing, chest or throat tightness, itching, dizziness or breathing changes during infusion.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: First visit of this cycle

Review symptoms, blood results and premedication before the infusion. Tell the nurse straight away about itching, flushing, throat or chest tightness, back pain, dizziness or breathing change. Check transport if premedication makes you drowsy and confirm the next weekly visit.

Day 2–7: Between weekly doses

Aches, fatigue, nausea or bowel symptoms may occur. Tingling may be mild at first but matters before the next dose. Record what symptoms stop you doing. Contact the team for new or worsening numbness, balance trouble, marked weakness or uncontrolled symptoms. Fever or sudden illness needs immediate contact.

Day 8: Second weekly review

This dose requires its own symptom and blood-test review. Describe the worst symptoms since the previous visit, not only how you feel today. Report sensory changes before the infusion and confirm your premedication instructions.

Day 9–14: Keep tracking function

Repeated treatment can increase nerve symptoms and fatigue. Check practical tasks such as buttons, writing and walking. Use the symptom passport to show changes. Seek early advice rather than waiting until numbness is severe.

Day 15: Third weekly review

The team decides whether treatment can proceed using the current assessment. Confirm whether Day 22 is planned for you; do not infer this from a different person’s calendar.

Day 16–21: After the third visit

Symptoms may settle or continue. An interval between appointments does not rule out infection or a treatment complication. Use the urgent contact plan if unwell and bring your record to the next review.

Day 22: Day 22: follow the prescribed schedule

A fourth dose is part of the four-visit plan. In the consultant-selected three-visit plan there is no Day 22 infusion. The calendar here is educational. Use your confirmed appointment and prescription; call if the schedule is unclear.

Day 23–28: Complete the cycle record

Record symptoms and functional changes, including those that improve before the next appointment. Treatment continuation depends on benefit, toxicity and your priorities. Check the next cycle date and review plan. Do not treat a gap in visits as a guarantee of recovery.

Practical treatment guide

Your practical treatment plan

Continue the agreed weekly pattern while helpful and tolerated

STEP 1

Weekly visits

Each planned infusion needs blood results, symptom review, preparation, premedication and observation. The one-hour infusion is not the total visit length.

STEP 2

Access and transport

Use a peripheral cannula or existing access selected with the team. A port is not automatic. Sedating antihistamines may affect driving; arrange transport as advised.

STEP 3

At home

Confirm whether Day 22 is prescribed and who to contact about missing results or appointments. Keep the medicine list and symptom record; report nerve symptoms early.

Schedule confirmation

Default Days 1, 8, 15 and 22 every 28 days. The consultant may choose Days 1, 8 and 15 only; confirm the selected plan. Each dose needs its own review.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Premedication for infusion reactions

Steroid and antihistamine premedication reduce the chance or severity of reactions. The team may adjust premedication after tolerated doses. Do not omit or change it yourself; tell the nurse about missed doses.

Aches, nausea and bowel support

Agree a pain-relief and anti-sickness plan. Ask before taking new medicines. Uncontrolled pain or poor intake needs review. Some supportive medicines cause constipation or drowsiness.

Nerve symptoms and fall prevention

Use secure footwear and take care with balance, hot surfaces and sharp objects if sensation is reduced. Tell the team early about functional changes; persistent neuropathy may influence dosing.

Hair, nails and skin

Ask about hair-loss support and whether scalp cooling is appropriate and available. Protect sensitive nails and skin. Report painful nail changes, spreading rash, blistering or infection.

Review the full medicine plan

First-dose NCCP suggested premedication: dexamethasone 8 mg IV, chlorphenamine 10 mg IV and famotidine 20 mg IV 30 minutes before paclitaxel. From Day 8, dexamethasone may be omitted in the source suggested table; add or modify for increased risk or previous reaction under consultant guidance. Famotidine omission is also a consultant/local-policy decision requiring close observation. The conventional paclitaxel product information has a different generic premedication regimen. Reconcile the exact product, weekly NCCP pathway and local policy; never self-omit premedication. Low emetic risk; keep the antiemetic plan separate from reaction premedication. Consider analgesia for myalgia/arthralgia and early action on neuropathy.

Treatment reference · 01

Which cancer setting does this page cover?

The regimen number matters as well as the drug name.

Advanced breast cancer

The NCCP indication concerns metastatic breast cancer after unsuitable or unsuccessful anthracycline-based treatment. Tumour biology and previous treatments influence options. This page does not describe every early-breast-cancer paclitaxel regimen.

Sources: nccp

Ovarian, small-cell lung and bladder cancer

These are specific later-line settings in this shared protocol. Evidence is less robust than for the breast-cancer weekly comparison. Ask about current alternatives and the implications of use outside the licensed indication where applicable.

Sources: nccp

Paclitaxel formulations and combinations

Conventional paclitaxel and nab-paclitaxel have different formulations and administration requirements. This is a single-agent paclitaxel guide. If another anti-cancer medicine is added, obtain the matching combination guide.

Sources: nccp

What matters to you?

Discuss symptom control, time in hospital, hair loss, neuropathy risk and the activities you most want to preserve. Your priorities can change. Supportive care is available alongside active treatment.

Sources: nccp

Before each weekly visit

Use the time between appointments to make review more informative.

Write down change from your baseline

Record tingling, numbness, pain, balance, bowel symptoms and fatigue. Tell the team about fever or illness when it occurs; the record is not a reason to wait.

Sources: patient

Check medicines and transport

Know whether premedication is taken at home or given in hospital. Bring your medicine list and tell the team about a previous infusion reaction. Sedating antihistamines can affect driving.

Sources: patient

Know the agreed schedule

Check the planned treatment days and blood-test arrangements. A three-visit and four-visit schedule must be distinguished in your own prescription. The number of visits may change after clinical review.

Treatment reference · 03

What the checks are for

Symptoms, blood results and the treatment aim answer different questions.

Blood counts

Neutrophils protect against infection, haemoglobin carries oxygen and platelets help clotting. A normal previous result or feeling well does not prove the current counts are safe. Fever or sudden illness needs immediate advice.

Sources: hse

Kidney and liver function

Organ function can change drug exposure. Vomiting or diarrhoea can cause dehydration and worsen kidney function. Report reduced urine, poor intake, jaundice and new medicines. The clinical assessment combines results with symptoms.

Sources: nccp

Describe symptoms in practical terms

Tell the team about the worst symptoms since the last visit, even if improved today. Examples include extra stools above your usual pattern, whether mouth pain affects drinking, and whether numbness affects buttons or balance.

Sources: patient

Assessing cancer control

The review plan may use scans, symptoms and ability to manage daily life. Stable disease can be worthwhile. Ask what benefit would justify continuing and what alternatives would be considered if treatment stops helping.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 04

What to watch for

Some effects appear early; others build over repeated doses.

Neuropathy and lasting effects

Paclitaxel can affect sensation and sometimes strength. Report numbness, burning pain, weakness or difficulty with everyday tasks before the next dose. Symptoms may take time to improve and can persist after treatment.

Sources: patient

Aches and fatigue

Muscle or joint pain can occur after treatment. Use the agreed analgesia plan and describe how pain affects sleep and function. New extreme weakness, fainting or breathlessness should be assessed rather than attributed automatically to treatment.

Sources: patient

Hair, skin and nails

Hair loss is common with paclitaxel; it may include body hair. Nail changes and skin symptoms can occur. Ask about practical support and report painful, infected or rapidly worsening changes.

Sources: patient

Mouth, bowels and infection

Gentle mouth care and early reporting help when soreness affects eating. Report persistent diarrhoea or vomiting. Low blood counts can make infection dangerous; a normal temperature does not make sudden illness safe.

Sources: hse

Infusion and breathing symptoms

Reactions can occur during infusion despite premedication. Tell the nurse immediately. New cough or breathlessness after leaving also needs assessment; the cause may be infection, a clot, treatment or cancer.

Sources: patient

Treatment reference · 05

Living with treatment

Plan around the activities and people that matter to you.

Food, drink and practical help

Use foods and drinks you can manage. Tell the team about weight loss, poor intake or difficulty shopping and cooking; ask for dietitian or practical support. A fixed daily fluid target is not appropriate for everyone, particularly with heart or kidney problems.

Sources: patient

Activity, rest and driving

Balance rest with manageable activity. Record what fatigue stops you doing. Do not drive when dizzy, unwell or affected by sedating medicines. Ask about work adjustments and transport for treatment days.

Sources: patient

Medicines, vaccines and supplements

Bring a complete medicine list, including over-the-counter products and supplements. Ask the oncology pharmacist before changes. Discuss vaccination timing. Do not stop prescribed long-term medicines on your own.

Sources: hse

Fertility, contraception and breastfeeding

Discuss fertility preservation before treatment when relevant. Ask for a written contraception and breastfeeding plan covering each medicine and the period after treatment. Tell the team promptly about a possible pregnancy.

Sources: hse

Support for you and your family

Ask about psycho-oncology, social work, counselling and symptom support. Take information in manageable amounts. A family member or trusted person can help record questions if you wish. Discuss how treatment fits your priorities.

Sources: patient

Treatment reference · 06

Useful treatment terms

Short explanations for the clinic conversation.

Taxane and microtubule

A taxane is a chemotherapy drug family. Microtubules are small cell structures involved in division and other functions. Effects on these structures help explain both anti-cancer activity and nerve toxicity.

Sources: nccp

Premedication and neuropathy

Premedication is medicine given before an infusion to reduce reactions. Neuropathy means nerve dysfunction. These are separate issues and both need review during weekly therapy.

Sources: nccp

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Does having more side effects mean treatment is working?

No. Side effects do not reliably show whether the cancer is responding. Report them so they can be treated. Your team assesses benefit using the tests and review plan appropriate to your cancer.

Why might a dose be delayed or reduced?

Your team may allow recovery, investigate a symptom or reduce the chance of lasting harm. Ask which drug changes, why, and when review is planned. A delay does not itself mean treatment has failed.

What should I say when calling?

Give the regimen name, last treatment date, symptoms and when they started. Explain whether you can eat, drink, pass urine and manage usual activities. Give your temperature if available, but do not delay calling to collect details.

What belongs in the consent conversation?

The individual aim and likely benefit, uncertainty, reasonable alternatives including not proceeding, common and serious risks, lasting effects, fertility and practical concerns. You can ask for an interpreter, more time or another explanation and can reconsider your decision.

How many cycles should I use this passport for?

The passport provides reusable recording pages, not a prescribed course length. Your oncology team sets the duration and can provide extra pages.

Can I use this page to decide if blood results are safe?

No. Treatment decisions require the clinical protocol, symptoms, previous toxicity, organ function and the prescriber’s review. The patient page explains the checks and does not authorise treatment.
