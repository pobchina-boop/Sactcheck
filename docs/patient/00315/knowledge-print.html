When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call oncology immediately for fever, shivering, sudden illness, unusual bleeding, persistent vomiting, severe diarrhoea, severe mouth pain or inability to drink. Chest pain during or after fluorouracil, sudden confusion, or unexpectedly early severe symptoms needs urgent assessment even if dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (a medicine-processing enzyme) (DPD) testing was normal. Report reduced urine, hearing change, ringing in the ears or new numbness promptly. Severe chest pain, severe breathing difficulty, collapse or a seizure is an emergency: call 112 or 999.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

Understand your treatment

What each part of treatment does

This chemotherapy is given before definitive treatment to shrink or control the head and neck cancer. The team explains how induction fits your overall treatment aim and why this particular schedule is appropriate.

Taxane chemotherapy

Docetaxel

Disrupts the cell’s division machinery

Docetaxel stabilises tiny structures called microtubules. Cells need these to separate their chromosomes during division; disrupting the process can stop cancer cells multiplying. Healthy dividing cells are also affected.

Microtubules stabilised↓Division disrupted↓Cell growth affected

What to watch for
Take prescribed steroid premedication. Report fever, lasting numbness, swelling, sore nails or infusion symptoms.

Platinum chemotherapy

Cisplatin

Cisplatin
Damages cancer-cell deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA)

Cisplatin links strands of DNA, the instructions inside cells, making it harder for cancer cells to repair damage and divide. Healthy cells may also be affected. Kidney function, hearing, blood counts and nerve symptoms help the team decide whether the next dose is safe.

DNA damage↓Cell repair disrupted↓Cancer growth reduced

What to watch for
Report reduced urine, persistent vomiting, tinnitus, hearing change or new tingling.

Antimetabolite chemotherapy

Fluorouracil

5-FU
Disrupts production of DNA and RNA

Fluorouracil, often shortened to 5-FU, interferes with the building blocks used to make DNA and RNA, which cells need to grow. It runs through a vein over the prescribed hours. Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) is an enzyme that breaks it down. Testing for DPD deficiency before treatment reduces risk but cannot rule out every severe reaction.

Cell building blocks disrupted↓Cells cannot divide normally↓Pump provides prescribed exposure

What to watch for
Report severe diarrhoea, mouth sores, chest pain, sudden confusion, sore palms or soles, or unexpectedly early severe symptoms.

Understanding your diagnosis

Keep your diagnosis, treatment name and agreed goal with your records.

Site, type and stage

Ask where the cancer started, what the biopsy shows and how far it has spread. Site, stage, previous treatment and general health affect the available options.

Which pathway applies?

Induction treatment for stage III or intravenous (IV) non-metastatic squamous cell cancer of the head and neck, within a planned course of definitive treatment.

Tumour tests and medicine safety tests

Your team explains any HPV/p16, Epstein–Barr virus or other tumour tests appropriate to your cancer site. Not every site needs the same tests. These are different from blood, kidney and other medicine safety checks.

Understanding the evidence

The NCCP regimen combines evidence and specialist guidance for a defined treatment setting.

A treatment name such as TPF or chemoradiation does not make different dose schedules, patient groups or sequences interchangeable. Ask which research and clinical factors support your exact plan. No single study percentage is presented as your personal chance of benefit.

Your treatment, step by step

Your port and treatment pump

From preparing the line to coming home after disconnection. Your team arranges the appointments and shows you what to do.

1 · Before treatment

Confirm access and training

Your team chooses the venous access device and teaches its care, including a port or peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) if used.

2 · Before Day 1

Write down the infusion option

Confirm whether your prescription uses one continuous 96-hour fluorouracil infusion or four daily 22-hour infusions; the unit writes down connection, change and disconnection times.

3 · Treatment days

Staff connect and change infusions

Only trained staff connect, program or change the infusion. Do not calculate or alter the rate.

4 · While connected

Watch the line and device

Call for leakage, pain, swelling, damage, disconnection, unexpected completion or a pump that appears not to run. Follow the unit’s device-specific emergency instructions.

5 · At prescribed completion

Attend for disconnection

The actual connection time and selected infusion duration determine completion. Do not disconnect or reconnect the line yourself.

6 · Between cycles

Keep the access-care plan

Keep dressing, flushing and review appointments; report redness, pain, fever or swelling.

Port or PICC: what is the difference?

A port is a small chamber beneath the skin, usually on the chest, attached to a tube ending in a large vein. A special needle goes through the skin into the port when it is used. A PICC enters a vein in the upper arm; a short section remains outside under a dressing. Both can deliver a home infusion. Your team helps choose the device and teaches its care.

Before insertion and during recovery

Tell the team about allergies, blood thinners, infections and any other cancer medicines. Follow the instructions about eating, drinking and transport; do not stop medicines yourself. Port placement uses local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation or general anaesthetic. The team explains the procedure and risks, including infection, bleeding, clots and insertion complications. Ask how to manage discomfort, protect the wound and resume activity. Report increasing pain, redness, swelling, discharge or fever.

Connection: what the nurse does

The nurse checks the line and treatment, secures the connection and dressing, and starts the pump. Before leaving, you should have the disconnection location and time, contact numbers including out of hours, and the written spill instructions. Ask to be shown what normal pump progress looks like. You do not need to calculate a rate.

Carrying it, sleeping and washing

Use the supplied carrier and the position recommended for your pump. Keep tubing free from pulling and kinks. Ask how to place the pump safely for sleep and how to protect the line and dressing when washing. Do not immerse it or swim. Avoid direct heat and cold. An elastomeric pump has a balloon that shrinks slowly and may have no alarm; an electronic pump has different instructions. Follow the instructions for the device you actually receive.

Disconnection is not port removal

Keep the appointment even if the pump looks empty, unless the team changes the plan. Staff check the pump and line and arrange safe disposal. After flushing and other line care as required, the port needle may be removed and a small dressing applied. The implanted port stays in place until a separate removal procedure is arranged. A PICC remains in place and needs scheduled dressing and flushing care.

Leakage, disconnection or unexpected pump progress

Call the team immediately if the pump leaks, is damaged, finishes unexpectedly early, appears not to run, or the line disconnects. Follow the emergency or spill steps you have been specifically taught, including use of a clamp if instructed. Do not reconnect, change settings or disconnect the pump yourself. Keep other people away from leaked medicine. If medicine touches skin, rinse it off and wash with soap and water, then seek advice.

Pain, swelling or feeling unwell

Contact oncology immediately for pain or swelling around the line, a wet/loose dressing, redness, fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell. A painful swollen arm or neck needs urgent advice. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty or collapse: call 112 / 999. Do not wait for pump disconnection or the next clinic visit.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Steroid preparation for docetaxel

The source premedication is dexamethasone 8 mg by mouth twice daily for three days, starting the day before docetaxel, with at least three doses taken before infusion. It helps reduce allergy and fluid retention. Follow your prescribed plan. If doses are missed or you cannot swallow them, contact the unit before attending; the clinician decides the alternative.

One anti-sickness and steroid plan

Cisplatin has a high risk of nausea and vomiting. The team provides preventive and rescue anti-sickness treatment. Have all steroid doses reconciled on one list so the docetaxel premedication and anti-sickness prescription do not lead to accidental duplication.

Growth-factor support

Growth-factor support (G-CSF) is required with every cycle of this regimen. Your team prescribes the product, dose and injection dates. Confirm who gives it and whom to contact if a dose is missed; it reduces risk but does not remove the need to call for fever or sudden illness.

Fluids and kidney protection

Cisplatin requires prescribed fluids before and after treatment. Oral hydration is encouraged for the following 24 hours, adapted to your heart, kidney and swallowing needs. Ask for a practical individual fluid plan and call for much less urine, persistent vomiting or inability to drink.

Antibiotics when prescribed

An antibiotic may be added by the treating team according to the regimen and your situation. It is not a medicine to start from an old supply. Confirm the name, start and stop dates and important interactions; infection symptoms still need urgent contact.

Food, mouth and swallowing support

Use the mouth-care and pain plan. Report ulcers, infection, poor intake or weight loss early. Dietitian and speech and language support can tailor intake and swallowing advice; arrange dental assessment before planned head and neck radiation.

Fertility, pregnancy and other medicines

Discuss fertility, pregnancy, breastfeeding and the medicine-specific duration of contraception before treatment. Bring medicines, supplements and allergies and check before adding over-the-counter or herbal products.

Between visits: symptoms and medicines

Use the written home plan and contact route.

Symptoms have no safe-day guarantee

Effects may begin during treatment or develop later, and some build up over repeated doses. Call for a new, severe or worsening symptom even when a calendar calls the day a recovery day.

One medicine list

Keep prevention medicines, rescue medicines and regular medicines together on one reconciled written list. Record what you take and whether it helps. Do not double a missed dose or reuse an old prescription unless your treating team instructs you.

Mouth, teeth and jaw care

Dental preparation and gentle daily care reduce avoidable problems.

Before treatment

Arrange dental assessment before head and neck radiation. The dental and oncology teams decide what treatment is needed and how much healing time is required; do not schedule extractions independently.

During treatment

Follow the prescribed brushing, fluoride, rinse and pain-relief advice. Report ulcers, infection, new bleeding, difficulty wearing dentures or worsening jaw opening. Avoid starting a new mouthwash without checking if the mouth is sore.

After head and neck radiation

Dry mouth can increase dental problems and some radiation effects last. Keep regular dental care and tell any future dentist that you have had head and neck radiation before invasive treatment. Your radiation/dental team plans any procedure.

Eating, drinking and maintaining strength

The aim is adequate intake and comfort during treatment.

Recognising an intake problem

Record weight and report food or drink becoming harder to manage. Pain, taste change, dry mouth or nausea can each need a different response; a dietitian can adapt the plan to you.

If ordinary food is not enough

The team may suggest food fortification, supplements or tube feeding when intake or swallowing safety requires it. A tube is a support option, not a judgement about how well you have tried to eat. Training and follow-up are needed.

Fluid needs are individual

Ask for a realistic drinking plan that accounts for kidney/heart problems and any feeding tube. Persistent vomiting, dizziness, markedly reduced urine or inability to drink needs urgent contact.

Swallowing, voice and communication

A speech and language therapist can help protect function.

Swallowing assessment

Report coughing or choking with food or drink, a wet-sounding voice after swallowing, food sticking or repeated chest infections. The team may need to assess aspiration risk and safe textures.

Exercises and rehabilitation

Do swallowing or jaw exercises only as taught for your situation. Treatment and recovery can change what is comfortable and safe. Ask for reassessment when symptoms change.

Communication needs

Tell the team about changes in speech, voice or hearing. Ask for written instructions, communication aids, an interpreter or help involving a support person.

Daily life and emotional support

Practical adjustments can make the plan easier to follow.

Energy and activity

Balance rest with manageable activity according to your ability and team advice. Ask for help if weakness, falls, sleep difficulty or pain interferes with daily life.

Smoking and alcohol

Ask for support to stop smoking and discuss alcohol with the team. Mouth irritation, medicine interactions and head and neck recovery can change what is safe for you.

Emotional and practical concerns

Tell the team about anxiety, appearance or intimacy concerns, finances, travel and caring responsibilities. Ask which local supports are available; resources can be added to your record when confirmed.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Are all TPF or TCF schedules the same?

No. This is NCCP 00315: docetaxel 75, cisplatin 75 and fluorouracil 1000 mg/m², using the exact days and infusion times in this guide. There are 3 planned induction cycles. The name alone is not enough to identify the prescription.

When does the fluorouracil finish?

Confirm whether your prescription uses one continuous 96-hour fluorouracil infusion or four daily 22-hour infusions; the unit writes down connection, change and disconnection times. Do not disconnect or change the infusion yourself.

What if I miss the steroid tablets?

Call the treatment unit before docetaxel. The clinician decides whether an alternative premedication or a change to the appointment is needed.

Does a normal DPD test mean fluorouracil cannot cause serious effects?

No. Testing reduces risk but cannot exclude every severe reaction. Unexpectedly early severe diarrhoea, mouth pain, illness, chest pain or confusion still requires urgent assessment.

Try explaining the plan back: Can I explain which treatment phase I am in and how many induction cycles are planned? Do I know when to start steroid tablets and what to do if a dose is missed?
