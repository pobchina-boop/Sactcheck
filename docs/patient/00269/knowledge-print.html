When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Seek prompt advice for fever, shivering, severe diarrhoea, painful mouth ulcers, inability to drink, new chest symptoms or a painful/leaking line. Do not wait for the next cycle.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

How the medicines work

Treatment to control cancer and symptoms, with regular review of benefit and side effects.

Antimetabolite chemotherapy

Fluorouracil (5-FU)

Short intravenous (IV) injection in FEC
Disrupts deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA) and RNA production

Fluorouracil interferes with the materials cells need to grow and divide. dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (a medicine-processing enzyme) (DPD) testing helps identify people at increased risk of severe toxicity. In FEC it is given on treatment day; it is not a take-home pump infusion.

Cell building blocks disrupted↓DNA / RNA affected↓Growth reduced

What to watch for
Report severe diarrhoea, mouth ulcers or inability to drink promptly. New chest pain needs urgent assessment.

Sources: nccp

Anthracycline chemotherapy

Epirubicin

Interferes with DNA handling

Epirubicin interferes with enzymes and DNA processes needed for cell division. It can also affect heart muscle, so the team considers previous exposure and cardiac risk.

DNA handling disrupted↓Cell damage↓Division reduced

What to watch for
Report pain or burning at the infusion site immediately. New breathlessness or chest symptoms need prompt review. Red urine for a short time can be a drug-colour effect; pain, clots or persistent bleeding still need advice.

Sources: nccp

Alkylating chemotherapy

Cyclophosphamide

Damages DNA needed for growth

Cyclophosphamide is activated in the body and damages DNA. This helps stop dividing cancer cells but can also affect blood-cell production and reproductive tissues.

Drug activated↓DNA damage↓Cell division affected

What to watch for
Use the prescribed anti-sickness and hydration plan. Report fever, painful urination or blood in the urine.

Sources: nccp

Practical treatment guide

Your practical treatment plan

Fluorouracil, epirubicin and cyclophosphamide on Day 1 every 21 days while beneficial and tolerated, within the lifetime anthracycline limit. Radiotherapy must not overlap FEC.

STEP 1

Before the first visit

Blood tests, relevant cardiac assessment, medicines review, fertility discussion and the consent conversation come first.

STEP 2

Plan venous access

A cannula, peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) or implanted port may be used. If a port is chosen, a trained team inserts it in a separate procedure with local anaesthetic and sometimes sedation; the team explains fasting, blood-thinner and wound instructions.

STEP 3

Infusion day

Staff check the line, give prescribed premedicines and treatment, and observe for reactions. Tell them immediately about burning, pain, rash or breathlessness.

STEP 4

Home and next review

This regimen does not routinely need a take-home infusion pump. Staff disconnect the infusion; a port is de-accessed when appropriate. Keep line-care and next-visit instructions.

Port insertion and removal

The line team explains placement, dressing care and when normal activities can resume. A port is accessed with a special needle during treatment. Removal of the implanted device is a separate planned procedure when no longer needed; do not remove or adjust it yourself.

PICC care and urgent line symptoms

A PICC usually needs regular dressing and flushing appointments even between treatments. Fever, redness, swelling, pain, leakage or damage requires prompt advice. Your hospital supplies the exact care instructions.

Visit time and transport

Allow time for blood review, preparation, treatment and observation. Your unit confirms the full visit duration. Arrange transport or an escort where advised.

Phase-specific visit pattern

These medicines are given on Day 1 in the unit. No take-home infusion pump is part of this regimen; port needle removal after a visit is different from removal of the implanted port.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

A clear anti-sickness plan

Take the prescribed prevention medicines and keep the rescue instructions. Contact the team if nausea or vomiting prevents drinking.

Infection and growth-factor plan

The team decides whether growth-factor injections are needed and gives the exact days. Fever or suddenly feeling unwell needs immediate advice.

Hydration and urinary symptoms

Agree fluid intake with your team, especially if you have a heart or kidney fluid restriction. Report painful urination or blood in the urine.

Before treatment

Discuss fertility, contraception, other medicines and line access. Tell staff immediately about burning or pain at the infusion site.

DPD testing before fluorouracil

Confirm the DPD result before the first treatment. Testing reduces uncertainty but does not rule out all serious side effects.

Treatment reference · 03

Surgery, radiotherapy and later treatment

In metastatic disease, surgery and radiotherapy are individual decisions for symptoms or selected local-control needs; they are not automatic steps after every course. Concurrent radiotherapy during FEC is contraindicated in the NCCP source; agree a sequential plan.

Hormone-receptor-positive disease

Endocrine treatment is considered when appropriate for the cancer biology and setting. It has its own schedule and monitoring; it is not automatically part of this chemotherapy prescription.

Sources: early · metastatic

Bone health

DEXA is linked to an aromatase inhibitor, ovarian suppression, treatment-induced menopause or other bone risk—not automatically to every chemotherapy course. The endocrine team confirms baseline testing and risk-based repeat scans.

Sources: bone

Markers and follow-up

Tumour markers can support selected metastatic assessments but must not alone determine progression or treatment changes.
