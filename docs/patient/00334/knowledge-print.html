Understand your treatment

How your treatment works

This treatment combines daily temozolomide with radiotherapy, followed by a break and further temozolomide cycles, to help control newly diagnosed glioblastoma. Your team discusses the expected benefit and how it fits after surgery or biopsy.

Oral chemotherapy capsule

Temozolomide

Temodal / generic
Damages deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA) in tumour cells

Temozolomide crosses into the brain and creates chemical changes in DNA. If the damage cannot be repaired, a cell may stop dividing or die. Healthy blood-forming cells can also be affected, which helps explain blood-count changes, infection and bleeding risks. Nausea prevention and liver monitoring are important too.

Capsule swallowed↓DNA damaged↓Cell growth disrupted

What to watch for
Report fever, bleeding, persistent vomiting, new breathing symptoms or yellow skin/eyes. Keep blood and liver tests even after capsule days finish.

Treatment focused on the brain tumour area

Radiotherapy

External-beam treatment planned by radiation oncology
Damages DNA in the treated area

Radiotherapy directs a planned dose of radiation to the tumour area. Radiation damages cellular DNA; planning aims to limit exposure of nearby healthy brain. The dose and treated area affect both benefit and side effects.

Individual planning scan↓Radiation to planned area↓Tumour-cell DNA damaged

What to watch for
Report new or worsening headache, vomiting, weakness, speech or memory change. The radiotherapy team gives a separate skin-care and symptom plan.

Complete timing and symptom notes

Day 1–5: Five daily capsule doses

Check the prescribed capsule strengths and number each day. Take fasting with the anti-sickness plan. Do not use leftover capsules to change the dose or replace a vomited dose.

Day 6–21: No temozolomide; recovery continues

Rest days allow recovery but blood counts can still fall. Continue other medicines only as individually prescribed. Contact the team for fever, sudden illness, bruising or persistent vomiting.

Day 22–28: Blood count and next-cycle decision

Have the Day 22 blood count and any follow-up blood tests arranged. Before the next cycle, the team reviews counts, kidney/liver tests and symptoms. Do not start the next cycle just because the calendar reaches Day 1.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Taking the treatment

Take the exact temozolomide capsule strengths and number on your pharmacy label, once daily when prescribed, on an empty stomach. Swallow whole with water; do not open, chew or crush. Ask the pharmacist to write the fasting interval and anti-sickness timing. Different phase doses are not interchangeable.

STEP 2

Missed or vomited medicine

If you vomit after temozolomide, do not take another dose that day. For a missed dose, use your pharmacist’s written instructions or call before taking it late; do not double a dose. Never replace a dose held for toxicity.

STEP 3

Medicine and supplement review

Bring an up-to-date list of all medicines, including steroids, seizure medicines, anticoagulants, non-prescription treatments, vitamins and herbs. Ask before starting something new. Keep a record of all dose changes.

STEP 4

Storage and handling

Keep capsules in their original labelled packs, away from children. Do not open, crush or chew. If swallowing or medicine organisation is difficult, contact the team before the next dose and agree safe help. Return unused chemotherapy to the pharmacy.

STEP 5

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and fertility

Treatment can harm a pregnancy and may affect fertility. Discuss fertility preservation, contraception for patients and partners, and the required period after the final dose before starting. Do not breastfeed during treatment. Ask the team for written, medicine-specific advice.

STEP 6

Appointments and transport

Before starting: full blood count, kidney and liver tests, glucose and hepatitis B screening. During concurrent treatment: blood count weekly; kidney and liver tests before week 4. Adjuvant treatment: blood count, kidney and liver tests on Day 1 of every 28-day cycle. EMA additionally advises a Day 22 blood count with follow-up until recovery and liver tests after each cycle. MRI timing is individual.

STEP 7

Help with the daily plan

Agree who can help with medicines, travel and appointments if memory, sight, fatigue or hand strength makes them difficult. Record your preferences and consent for sharing information.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Nausea prevention

Temozolomide has moderate-to-high oral emetic potential. Take the prescribed anti-sickness plan before chemotherapy and use rescue medicine as instructed. Ask what to do if nausea prevents taking medicines or fluids.

PJP infection prevention

PJP-prevention medicine is required during temozolomide with radiotherapy in this NCCP regimen. Your team supplies the prescription and decides how long to continue, including if lymphocyte counts remain low. Do not stop it just because radiotherapy has finished.

Bowels, food and fluids

Anti-sickness medicines and other medicines can worsen constipation. Ask for your bowel plan, and call for persistent vomiting, severe constipation or difficulty drinking. Do not double a dose after vomiting.

Steroid and seizure plans

If prescribed, keep steroids and seizure medicines on separate written schedules. Chemotherapy rest days do not tell you when to stop them. Ask the pharmacist to check interactions and explain any dose changes.

Treatment reference · 01

Your treatment and its purpose

Use the regimen and phase agreed with your team.

Why this treatment?

This treatment combines daily temozolomide with radiotherapy, followed by a break and further temozolomide cycles, to help control newly diagnosed glioblastoma. Your team discusses the expected benefit and how it fits after surgery or biopsy.

What each part does

Temozolomide produces chemical damage in DNA. Cells unable to repair that damage may stop dividing or die. Blood-forming cells can also be affected, which explains the need for blood counts and infection precautions. Radiotherapy damages DNA in the planned treatment area. Its team explains the separate dose, skin care and effects on the brain.

Schedule and planned duration

Take temozolomide daily throughout six weeks of radiotherapy, including weekends as prescribed. After a four-week break, take temozolomide on Days 1–5 of each 28-day cycle for up to six adjuvant cycles.

Evidence and uncertainty

Adding concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide improved survival in the trial population. This is evidence for the combined treatment strategy; it does not establish a separate benefit for every individual component or an individual survival forecast. The trial population and older pathology classification do not replace review of the current integrated diagnosis, fitness, O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) result and patient priorities.

Treatment reference · 04

Understanding your particular glioma

Your diagnosis includes the tumour’s cells, molecular findings and behaviour, not just the word brain cancer.

The name and location

A glioma is a tumour arising in the brain or spinal cord. Astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma and glioblastoma are different types. Cancer that travels to the brain from another organ is a brain metastasis and follows a different pathway.

Grade is not the same as stage

Glioma reports combine the tumour type, grade and molecular findings. Grade is not a measure of spread around the body: a grade 4 brain tumour does not mean body-wide metastases. Ask your specialist to explain older terms in your records.

isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and 1p/19q

Changes in the IDH genes and loss of chromosome regions 1p and 19q help classify certain gliomas. The pattern of results matters. These are usually tumour-sample tests; they do not by themselves show that a change runs in the family.

MGMT promoter methylation

The MGMT gene makes a protein that repairs a particular type of DNA damage. Methylation in its control region can reduce that repair and inform discussion of temozolomide benefit. This is a tumour-tissue result, not a blood marker or a personal survival percentage.

A useful diagnosis record

Keep the exact pathology diagnosis, sample date, grade and available molecular results together. Label a result as pending or not tested when appropriate. Ask whether further pathology review would affect the plan.

Treatment reference · 05

Tests and scans: what each one tells you

Safety blood tests, tumour tissue tests and MRI answer different questions.

Safety blood tests

A full blood count measures red cells, white cells and platelets. Kidney and liver tests assess other aspects of treatment safety. Follow the blood-test schedule even when you feel well or the scan is reassuring.

MRI and neurological review

MRI gives detailed pictures of the brain. The team also checks symptoms, movement, speech, thinking and daily function. Before scanning, tell staff about implants, metal, kidney problems or previous contrast reactions, and ask for help if the scan worries you.

Changes on a scan need interpretation

Treatment-related changes can resemble tumour growth on MRI; this is called pseudoprogression. The specialist interprets the scan with symptoms and may need another scan or further assessment. Report worsening symptoms promptly rather than assuming a scan change is harmless.

An individual follow-up timetable

Record the next MRI and the contact who will explain it. Your diagnosis, treatment phase and previous results determine the interval. Other scans or blood markers are ordered for a specific reason, not automatically before every cycle.

Keep results with decisions

Mark tests as booked, done and reviewed so the next action is clear. Contact the named service about a missing result. Do not change chemotherapy or steroids in response to a report without instructions.

Treatment reference · 06

Brain symptoms, medicines and when to call

Symptoms may reflect the tumour, swelling, treatment, another medicine or a separate illness.

Know your usual symptoms

Before treatment, note your usual walking, strength, speech, sight, memory, sleep and seizure pattern. Record new changes and their timing. A trusted person can help if you want them involved.

Urgent neurological changes

Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice, including on treatment rest days. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. Follow the emergency instructions in any established seizure rescue plan.

Steroids need a separate written plan

Steroids may reduce swelling around a brain tumour. If needed, ask for a written dose, review date and reduction plan; do not stop suddenly or adjust the dose yourself. Report infection symptoms, marked thirst, weakness or troublesome sleep or mood changes.

Seizure medicines and rescue instructions

Take prescribed seizure medicine on its own schedule. Ask what to do after a missed or vomited dose and how to manage a breakthrough seizure. Do not switch or stop it yourself. Anyone giving a rescue medicine needs the personal instructions and training.

Safety at home and outside

Discuss driving, work, bathing, swimming, heights and machinery with your team, taking account of symptoms and seizures. Individual medical and licensing advice is needed. Occupational therapy can help make daily routines safer.

Treatment reference · 07

Your overall treatment plan and choices

Treatment should account for the tumour and the life you want to lead.

Understand the sequence

Place biopsy or surgery, radiotherapy, medicines and follow-up on one plan. Concurrent means treatments overlap; adjuvant means treatment follows the main treatment. A new phase can require different doses, tests and support medicines.

Agree what treatment is trying to achieve

Ask what treatment can reasonably achieve for your tumour and which effects matter most to you. Discuss benefits, burdens, alternatives and the reasons for a pause or stop.

Put evidence in context

A study result applies to a particular group, diagnosis and schedule. Older studies may use different tumour names. Ask which evidence fits your plan; an average cannot forecast your response or lifespan.

Discuss options available to you

Ask about suitable trials, another specialist opinion and other treatment or symptom-care options. Your multidisciplinary team explains what is appropriate and available for you.

Make room for your priorities

Bring up thinking, independence, family time, work and the side effects you most want to avoid. Ask for time, written notes or a supporter if that helps you weigh the choices.

Treatment reference · 08

Living well, rehabilitation and support

Practical and symptom support can begin alongside anticancer treatment.

Thinking, communication and daily tasks

Tell the team about changes in memory, language, attention, sight or planning. Rehabilitation can involve physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy or psychology. Ask for practical help with routines and medicines.

Food, nausea, bowels and energy

Use your prescribed nausea and bowel plan and report problems early. Ask for dietitian help if intake or weight falls. Adapt movement to your balance and strength, and ask the team to assess fatigue that disrupts daily life.

Emotional support and carers

Tell the team about changes in mood, behaviour or coping. Patients and carers may each need psychological or practical support. Agree who can receive information and ask for help before caring becomes overwhelming.

Work, transport and finances

Plan travel, time away from work, caring duties and help with appointments. A nurse or social worker can identify practical support; agree who can help with tablets if needed.

Supportive care and future wishes

Supportive and palliative care can help with symptoms and quality of life while active treatment continues. You can discuss future care preferences and who you want involved in decisions at a time that suits you. Ask the team for advice from your team and documentation rather than using legal or hospice rules from a US booklet.

Information that works on paper

Keep the latest paper schedule, medicine list, results plan and emergency contacts together. The treatment card helps others identify your regimen. It supports, but does not replace, the prescription and emergency instructions.

Questions worth asking

What is my exact diagnosis, grade and relevant molecular result?

Ask for the integrated pathology wording and an explanation of IDH, 1p/19q and MGMT where relevant. The diagnosis and treatment history guide how this regimen applies to you.

Which phase and capsule calendar am I on now?

Take temozolomide daily throughout six weeks of radiotherapy, including weekends as prescribed. After a four-week break, take temozolomide on Days 1–5 of each 28-day cycle for up to six adjuvant cycles.

What is my PJP-prevention plan, including after radiotherapy?

The NCCP concurrent treatment requires PJP prevention. Ask which medicine, when to take it, monitoring and when the team will decide it can stop. Low lymphocyte counts may mean continuing longer.

Which tests must happen before I can take the next dose?

Before starting: full blood count, kidney and liver tests, glucose and hepatitis B screening. During concurrent treatment: blood count weekly; kidney and liver tests before week 4. Who helps with seizures, steroids, memory or daily tasks?

Ask for a named contact, written seizure and steroid plans if prescribed, and rehabilitation or practical support. Agree who can help you organise treatment.

Which symptoms mean contact the team today?

Use your written emergency plan even on days without chemotherapy. Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. Follow any existing seizure rescue plan. Promptly report worsening headache, repeated vomiting, cough, breathlessness, unusual bruising, yellow skin or eyes, or inability to drink. When will we review the benefit and my priorities?

Ask for the next clinical and MRI review, how treatment effects will be distinguished from growth, and what would lead to continuing, changing or stopping treatment.
