Understanding your treatment

In this chapter

Why this treatment is offered, what its medicines do, and how the agreed plan fits your care.

Adjuvant ribociclib (Kisqali) + endocrine therapy
National Cancer Control Programme (NCCP) 00892

How this medicine supports your treatment

For eligible higher-risk HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer, ribociclib is added to endocrine therapy to reduce recurrence risk.

CDK4/6 inhibitor

Ribociclib (Kisqali)

Slows the cell-division checkpoint
CDK4/6 checkpoint↓Checkpoint inhibited↓Cell cycling slows

Ribociclib blocks CDK4 and CDK6, proteins that help cells move through the cycle of division. Endocrine therapy reduces the hormone signal that can drive HR-positive breast cancer.

Keep blood and electrocardiogram (ECG) appointments. Fever, jaundice, fainting, palpitations, severe rash or new breathlessness need prompt advice.

Endocrine partner

Aromatase inhibitor ± ovarian suppression

Reduces oestrogen signalling
Oestrogen pathway↓aromatase inhibitor (AI) or receptor blockade↓Growth signal reduced

The aromatase inhibitor reduces oestrogen production. Before menopause, ovarian suppression is also required; men receiving this AI combination need the appropriate LHRH-agonist plan under product information. The endocrine treatment is taken on a separate schedule and normally continues after ribociclib.

Discuss joint symptoms, hot flushes, sexual symptoms and bone health. Keep ovarian-suppression appointments if prescribed.

Your treatment aim

Who this treatment is for

HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer at high recurrence risk: stage IIA with qualifying nodal/high-risk factors, or stage IIB–III, as specified by NCCP. Current NCCP00892v2 includes selected node-negative eligibility and a conflicting node-negative exclusion; clinical reconciliation remains required.

NCCP 00892 v2 · official regimen

Schedule and duration

The usual adjuvant starting dose is ribociclib 400 mg once daily on Days 1–21, followed by Days 22–28 off, for up to three years. The aromatase inhibitor continues every day on its own plan; ovarian suppression is added when indicated. At completion of the planned three-year ribociclib course, continue the separately agreed endocrine and follow-up plan.

NCCP 00892 v2 · official regimen

What the studies tell us

Ribociclib added to an aromatase inhibitor reduced the chance of invasive cancer returning in the study. Recent longer follow-up also reported a favorable survival finding, but that analysis was descriptive and needs that qualification. Your treatment team can explain how the study matches your recurrence risk.

How to interpret this

Study results describe groups with defined treatment and eligibility. Ask about your own likely benefits, important harms, alternatives and remaining uncertainty.

NATALEE primary report, Slamon et al 2024 · NATALEE prespecified five-year report, Crown et al 2025 · NATALEE six-year descriptive analysis, Slamon et al, Annals of Oncology journal pre-proof, 23 October 2026

NATALEE: primary trial and prespecified six-year descriptive follow-up

Studies describe groups. Ask how closely the treatment and study population match your situation.

Your team can discuss the strength and limitations of the evidence alongside treatment risks and alternatives.

When to get help

Emergency: 112 / 999. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty, collapse or stroke symptoms need emergency help.
Contact your treating team promptly: Report new breathlessness, jaundice, marked bleeding, severe diarrhoea, inability to drink, chest symptoms or a severe rash promptly.

Checks before and during treatment

Baseline tests and ongoing tests have different purposes.

Before the first dose

full blood count (FBC), renal and liver profile; potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphorus. ECG before initiation; QTcF must be below 450 ms. Correct electrolytes and review QT/CYP3A interactions. Confirm stage, recurrence-risk criteria, prior treatment, AI and ovarian-suppression plan; reconcile the NCCP node-negative eligibility/exclusion conflict before selection.

During treatment

FBC, renal and liver profile every two weeks for cycles 1–2, then before cycles 3–6, then as clinically indicated. Electrolytes before each of the first six cycles, then as clinically indicated. Repeat ECG around cycle 1 Day 14, then as clinically indicated. Increase liver monitoring for grade 2 or worse abnormalities; review fever, jaundice, palpitations/fainting, breathlessness and severe skin symptoms. Keep early-breast surveillance and bone/endocrine follow-up distinct from treatment-toxicity checks and from metastatic response imaging.

Living with treatment

Supportive medicines and the endocrine partner have their own plan.

Nausea support

NCCP classifies ribociclib as oral moderate–high emetic risk. Agree preventive or rescue treatment under local policy after QT and CYP3A interaction review; no default QT-prolonging antiemetic is selected by this information card.

Heart rhythm and electrolytes

Attend the baseline and cycle 1 Day-14 ECG and scheduled electrolyte checks. Fainting, palpitations or severe dizziness need urgent assessment.

Blood and liver monitoring

Keep two-weekly blood tests during cycles 1–2 and pre-cycle tests during cycles 3–6. Report fever, jaundice, dark urine or new breathing/skin symptoms promptly.

AI and ovarian-suppression support

The aromatase inhibitor continues during the ribociclib rest week and generally after ribociclib completion. Discuss menopause, joints, sexual health, bone risk and any injection-site symptoms from ovarian suppression.

Completion and follow-up

Ribociclib has a planned maximum three-year course. Confirm the endocrine duration and early-breast surveillance plan separately; new symptoms should be assessed without waiting for a routine appointment.

What if a dose is missed or treatment is held?

Take ribociclib once daily with or without food; swallow whole. Avoid grapefruit. If a dose is missed or vomited, do not take an extra dose; take the next scheduled dose. Confirm any clinical hold and restart with the team. The endocrine partner follows a separate prescription. A hold may affect one medicine rather than the whole combination; confirm each component.

What happens after treatment changes or ends?

At completion of the planned three-year ribociclib course, continue the separately agreed endocrine and follow-up plan. Keep the separately agreed disease follow-up, endocrine and bone-health plan.

Five warning signs: contact your team promptly

These are priority warning signs, not the five most common side effects. Keep using the detailed anatomical map. Do not wait for a diary entry or your next appointment to seek help.

New breathlessness or cough

Call your oncology team urgently for new or worsening shortness of breath, cough or chest pain. Lung inflammation can occur during treatment. Severe breathing difficulty or severe chest pain: call 112 / 999.

Fever, shivering or feeling suddenly unwell

Low white blood cells can make infection serious. Follow your hospital’s temperature threshold and call immediately for fever, shaking chills or sudden illness, even without a fever. Do not wait for the next appointment.

Palpitations, dizziness or fainting

Report a new fast or irregular heartbeat or dizziness urgently. Ribociclib can affect heart rhythm. Fainting, collapse or severe chest pain needs emergency assessment; call 112 / 999.

Yellow skin or eyes, or dark urine

Call your team urgently for yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, pain high on the right side of your tummy, or unusual bleeding. Liver changes may also occur without symptoms, so keep blood-test appointments.

Blistering or rapidly worsening rash

Seek urgent medical assessment for a painful or spreading rash, peeling skin, blisters, or sores involving the mouth, eyes or lips, especially with fever. Do not wait for the next clinic visit.

Everyday effects of ribociclib (Kisqali)

Tiredness

Pace activities and plan short rests. Tell the team if fatigue limits your usual activities or self-care; blood tests may identify a contributing cause.

Nausea, appetite or bowel changes

Try small meals and regular drinks. Use only the anti-sickness or bowel medicines agreed with your team. Call if symptoms persist, intake falls or you cannot keep fluids down.

Milder skin or hair changes

Gentle skin care and moisturiser may help dryness. Report new rashes rather than assuming they are harmless. Hair thinning can occur; ask about practical support.

Changes found on blood tests

White blood cells and liver tests can change before you feel unwell. Attend the planned tests and wait for the team’s advice about the next cycle.

Your anti-hormonal (endocrine) treatment

Ribociclib and endocrine treatment have different jobs. Your prescribed aromatase inhibitor has its own schedule. Ovarian suppression may also be part of your plan. Effects overlap: you do not need to decide which medicine caused a symptom.

Hot flushes and night sweats

Layers, a cooler bedroom and identifying personal triggers may help. Tell the team if flushes disturb sleep or daily life; treatment options should be checked for interactions with Kisqali.

Joint or muscle aches

Gentle movement and regular activity can help stiffness. Discuss persistent pain and suitable pain relief. Report new focal or worsening pain; do not automatically attribute it to hormone treatment.

Mood and sleep changes

Tell the team about ongoing low mood, anxiety or disrupted sleep. These can have several causes, and support is available. Seek urgent help if you feel unsafe or at risk of harming yourself.

Vaginal dryness and sexual wellbeing

Dryness, discomfort or reduced interest in sex can occur. Ask about non-hormonal moisturisers, lubricants and specialist support. Discuss any hormonal product with your oncology team first.

Bone health over time

Aromatase inhibitors and ovarian suppression can affect bone strength. Ask about your bone-density assessment, activity, calcium/vitamin D needs and whether bone-protective treatment is indicated.

Medicines, supplements and food: check before adding anything

Interactions can increase side effects, reduce treatment effectiveness or affect another medicine. This is a set of examples, not a complete interaction checker. Do not start, stop or change a prescribed medicine or your Kisqali dose without your team.

May increase Kisqali exposure and side effects

Some medicines slow the enzyme that clears ribociclib (CYP3A4).

Examples include clarithromycin, itraconazole and ritonavir-containing medicines. Grapefruit and grapefruit juice can also raise exposure.

What to do: Avoid grapefruit. Ask the oncology pharmacist to check any new prescription, including short courses of antibiotics or antivirals.

May reduce Kisqali exposure and effectiveness

Some medicines increase CYP3A4 activity, so the body removes ribociclib more quickly.

Examples include rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin and St John’s wort.

What to do: Avoid St John’s wort. Do not stop prescribed medicines yourself; ask the oncology team to arrange a suitable alternative when needed.

May add to heart-rhythm risk

Other medicines can also lengthen the heart’s QT interval.

Examples include amiodarone, moxifloxacin and ondansetron. Tamoxifen is not a recommended partner with ribociclib.

What to do: Check anti-sickness medicines, antibiotics and all other treatments with your team. An example list cannot establish that an unlisted medicine is safe.

Kisqali can increase other medicine levels

Ribociclib itself inhibits CYP3A4.

Some sedatives, pain medicines and other prescriptions may need an alternative, monitoring or dose adjustment.

What to do: Keep an up-to-date list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal products. Tell other prescribers that you take ribociclib.
