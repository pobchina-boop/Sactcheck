When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call urgently for severe diarrhoea or dehydration, bleeding, new swelling, much less urine, severe skin blistering or symptoms of low blood sugar. Chest pain, severe breathlessness, collapse, seizure or severe headache with vision changes needs emergency assessment.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Your treatment and urgent-contact card is supplied separately and in the wallet-card section of the complete handbook.

How your medicines work

Sunitinib is a daily targeted capsule used to help control a pancreatic NET that cannot be removed or has spread and is progressing. The team reviews control, side effects and your priorities.

Targeted therapy capsule

Sunitinib

Sutent
Blocks several growth and blood-vessel signals

Sunitinib blocks several signalling proteins involved in tumour growth and blood-vessel formation. This can slow a pancreatic NET. Effects on healthy blood vessels and other tissues help explain high blood pressure, bleeding, wound-healing problems, thyroid changes and sore hands or feet.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Capsules and missed doses

The usual starting dose for this pancreatic NET schedule is 37.5 mg once daily. Follow your label, swallow whole with water and take at the same time each day consistently with or without food. If a dose is missed, do not take an additional dose; take the usual prescribed dose the next day.

STEP 2

Interactions

Avoid grapefruit and St John’s wort. Ask the pharmacist to review every medicine and supplement, including blood thinners, antibiotics and medicines affecting heart rhythm.

STEP 3

Surgery and dental work

Tell your team about planned operations, wounds or invasive dental work. A treatment interruption may be needed for major surgery; the oncology and surgical teams agree when to stop and restart. Ask about a dental assessment.

STEP 4

Storage and supply

Keep capsules in their original labelled packaging, away from children, and follow storage instructions. Return unused medicine to the pharmacy. Bring your current prescription and medicine list to review.

STEP 5

Pregnancy, feeding and fertility

Tell the team about pregnancy, planned pregnancy or breastfeeding before treatment. Ask for the contraception and fertility plan specific to sunitinib, including how long precautions continue after stopping.

STEP 6

Tests and visits

Baseline blood count, kidney/liver, thyroid and blood pressure; urine protein assessment and cardiac tests as indicated. Counts, kidney and liver tests before each cycle; BP at each attendance; thyroid every 12 weeks. Heart, urine and glucose monitoring are individualised. Scans and tumour markers follow the consultant plan.

Treatment reference · 01

Your sunitinib treatment

This guide is for the continuous pancreatic NET regimen.

Why sunitinib?

Sunitinib is a daily targeted capsule used to help control a pancreatic NET that cannot be removed or has spread and is progressing. The team reviews control, side effects and your priorities.

How it works

Sunitinib blocks several signalling proteins involved in tumour growth and blood-vessel formation. This can slow a pancreatic NET. Effects on healthy blood vessels and other tissues help explain high blood pressure, bleeding, wound-healing problems, thyroid changes and sore hands or feet.

What the trial can tell us

Sunitinib delayed progression compared with placebo in this population. The trial stopped early; early survival findings require caution and do not predict one person’s lifespan. The results apply to the pancreatic NET population and continuous dose studied, not every NET site or another sunitinib schedule.

Treatment reference · 04

Understanding your particular NET

Several different features help the team choose treatment.

Where it started and where it is now

A neuroendocrine tumour begins in cells with hormone-making features. Its starting organ and the places it has spread are separate parts of the diagnosis. A pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour differs from pancreatic adenocarcinoma; a deposit in the liver may have come from another organ.

Differentiation, grade and Ki-67

The pathology report describes how organised the cells look (differentiation) and their growth activity (grade). Ki-67 measures cells showing a growth-related protein in the sample. Its percentage describes that sample, not the amount of your body affected or your chance of survival.

Grade is different from stage

Stage describes extent; grade describes tumour biology. Either can vary independently. A well-differentiated grade 3 neuroendocrine tumour and a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma are distinct diagnoses and may need different treatments.

Functioning and nonfunctioning

A functioning tumour produces a hormone syndrome. A nonfunctioning tumour can still cause problems through growth or pressure. Somatostatin receptor uptake on a scan shows a treatment target; it does not, on its own, identify a hormone syndrome.

Your diagnosis record

Ask for one diagnosis summary: starting organ, grade, differentiation, Ki-67 result if reported, sites involved, hormone syndrome and receptor-imaging result. Keep pending results clearly marked.

Treatment reference · 05

Making sense of tests and results

Safety tests, tumour tests and hormone tests answer different questions.

Blood tests for treatment safety

Blood counts assess oxygen-carrying cells, infection-fighting cells and platelets. Kidney and liver results help assess treatment safety. These checks are needed even when you feel well; a reassuring scan answers a different question.

computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and receptor imaging

CT and MRI show anatomy and tumour size. Somatostatin receptor imaging tests whether a particular receptor target is present. An fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scan measures uptake of a glucose-like tracer and is used selectively. Your team chooses the scans that can guide your care.

Hormones and tumour markers are individual

Selected markers may help answer a hormone or tumour question. Examples include 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) for serotonin-related symptoms, chromogranin A, or a specific pancreatic hormone. They are not a routine panel for every neuroendocrine tumour. Interpret changes with symptoms and imaging.

Preparing for a test

Use the laboratory instructions for fasting, food restrictions and any timed urine collection. Some medicines affect marker results, including acid-suppressing medicines and chromogranin A. Ask the team what to do; do not stop a prescribed medicine yourself.

Heart and inherited-risk checks

Some hormone syndromes can affect heart valves, so an ultrasound of the heart may be requested. Inherited-risk assessment depends on the tumour and family history; multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 is one example. An inherited-risk test and a test of tumour tissue answer different questions.

Who does what, and when?

For each test, write its name, purpose, booking date and result-review contact. Ask the team to set your next scan or marker date. A schedule for someone after surgery may not apply during your treatment.

Treatment reference · 06

Hormone symptoms and day-to-day changes

A new symptom may come from the NET, treatment or another illness.

Build a picture of what is normal for you

Keep a brief record of your usual bowel pattern, flushing, appetite, weight, pain and energy. Note new changes and when they started. This helps the team assess whether treatment, hormones or another problem needs attention.

Hormone-related symptoms

Hormone symptoms depend on the tumour: examples include flushing and watery diarrhoea, acid-related symptoms, or changes in blood sugar. An insulin-producing tumour can lower blood sugar. Ask which symptoms apply to you and whether you need a written rescue plan.

Hormone treatment is a separate part of the plan

Hormone-control medicines have their own schedule. A pause in anticancer tablets does not automatically mean pausing those medicines. Before peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, the specialist team coordinates any interruption and restart of somatostatin analogue injections.

When a change is urgent

Follow your oncology emergency plan for sudden or severe symptoms. Collapse, severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, confusion or uncontrolled bleeding needs emergency assessment. Severe diarrhoea, persistent vomiting, dehydration, fever or sudden illness needs urgent oncology advice. Do not wait for a scan, an online answer or the next clinic visit.

Procedures and hormone syndromes

Tell teams arranging an operation, anaesthetic or other procedure about a hormone-producing tumour or previous hormone crisis. Your specialist decides whether extra preparation is needed. Carry the agreed emergency instructions and current medicine list.

Treatment reference · 07

Your overall plan and choices

Your priorities belong in the treatment plan.

What are we trying to achieve?

Agree your treatment aim: slowing growth, reducing tumour size, controlling hormone symptoms or preserving daily function. A scan showing no growth may be a useful result even when the tumour has not shrunk.

How treatments fit together

Your plan may use different treatments at different times, including surgery, medicines, receptor-targeted radiation or treatment directed at the liver. Ask the team to mark your own sequence and who coordinates it.

Choosing between options

Ask why the proposed option fits the tumour site, grade, receptor findings, symptoms and your health. Discuss reasonable alternatives, observation when appropriate and suitable trials. Your team explains which options are available to you.

Evidence is about groups

Studies measure different outcomes, such as shrinkage, time before worsening and survival. Ask which outcome matters for your plan and how closely the study group matches your diagnosis. No study average can predict one person’s course.

Review points and shared decisions

Agree when to review the plan and what could lead to continuing, pausing or changing treatment. Include the effects on work, caring duties, travel and the activities that matter to you.

Treatment reference · 08

Eating, energy, practical help and support

Supportive care can be used alongside treatment from the start.

Food, weight and digestion

Report weight loss, persistent diarrhoea, greasy stools or difficulty eating. Different causes need different treatment. Ask for a dietitian and whether reduced digestive-enzyme function needs assessment.

Fluids and safe activity

Use your personal fluid advice, especially with diarrhoea or heart or kidney disease. Adjust activity and rest to your energy. Report dizziness, breathlessness or fatigue that limits daily tasks so treatable causes can be checked.

Emotions, relationships and quality of life

Ask for help with mood, sleep, relationships, intimacy or caring responsibilities when you need it. Symptom and palliative-care teams can support comfort and quality of life while anticancer treatment continues.

Practical help

Ask your specialist nurse or social worker about transport, employment, finances and help at home. Tell them about caring responsibilities and ask which support organisations fit your needs.

A record that works without a phone

Keep a paper schedule, current medicine list and real contact numbers together. The handbook can be read without internet access. Use the written emergency plan whenever you need urgent help.

Questions to take to the next visit

For the next visit, choose the questions that matter most: my exact diagnosis; the aim of treatment; which tests I need and why; who explains results; when to call; and what help is available.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Is my dose the continuous pancreatic NET schedule?

For this NCCP pancreatic NET regimen, the standard starting schedule is 37.5 mg once daily continuously, with 28-day reviews and no planned rest week. Your own prescribed dose may differ.

Who checks my BP and when are my 12-week thyroid tests?

Blood pressure is checked at each attendance and thyroid tests every 12 weeks, with extra tests if indicated. Ask who will book them and where to record readings.

What should I do about diarrhoea or painful hands and feet?

Report these symptoms early and follow the written rescue and hold plan. Painful hands or feet can require interruption even at the first episode; the team confirms dose changes and restarting.

Do my diabetes or blood-thinning medicines need extra monitoring?

Ask the pharmacist to check the full medicine list. Diabetes may require closer glucose checks and changes to diabetes treatment; bleeding risks and interactions need individual review.

What is the plan for dental work or surgery?

Tell the oncology team before arranging procedures. Surgery, wounds and dental work may need a coordinated interruption, with restart decided by the treating teams.

When will we review response and quality of life?

Ask for the planned symptom, scan and treatment-review dates. There is no fixed total number of cycles; continued treatment depends on benefit, tolerability and your priorities.

Try explaining the plan back: Can you explain why this is continuous daily dosing, where to record blood pressure and when painful hands/feet or diarrhoea need a call?
