Understand your treatment

How the medicines work

The aim is to control advanced sarcoma and help symptoms. The strongest trial evidence concerns previously treated liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma; the team explains expected benefit for your particular subtype.

Chemotherapy

Trabectedin

Disrupts genetic instructions and repair

Trabectedin binds to the genetic material, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), and interferes with how cancer cells use and repair it. This can slow tumour growth; normal blood, liver and muscle cells can also be affected.

Individual prescribed course

Trabectedin 1.5 mg/m² is infused over 24 hours starting on day 1 of each 21-day cycle. Treatment continues while benefit and tolerability justify it. The actual dose, start time and any delay require a new safety review.

This is later-line single-agent trabectedin over 24 hours. It is not first-line doxorubicin plus trabectedin with maintenance, and it is not the shorter ovarian-cancer combination infusion. Severe kidney impairment, heart problems or previous serious toxicity require individual oncology/pharmacy review before treatment can continue.

Your treatment, step by step

Your port and treatment pump

From preparing the line to coming home after disconnection. Your team arranges the appointments and shows you what to do.

1 · Before treatment

Choose central venous access

Central access is strongly recommended. The team explains insertion, infection prevention, dressing and flushing.

2 · Before connection

Confirm training and contacts

Obtain spill/leak instructions, pump guidance and verified daytime/out-of-hours contacts.

3 · Day 1

Connect the prescribed infusion

Trained staff connect a 24-hour trabectedin infusion after the required premedication.

4 · During the next 24 hours

Follow the pump and line plan

Do not alter settings or disconnect the pump. Report alarms, leakage, pain, swelling or fever promptly.

5 · After 24 hours from connection

Professional disconnection

Attend at the actual prescribed finish time. An apparently empty pump does not authorise self-disconnection.

6 · After treatment

Continue line care

Pump disconnection does not remove a port or catheter. Agree ongoing care and any separate removal appointment.

Port or peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC): what is the difference?

A port is a small chamber beneath the skin, usually on the chest, attached to a tube ending in a large vein. A special needle goes through the skin into the port when it is used. A PICC enters a vein in the upper arm; a short section remains outside under a dressing. Both can deliver a home infusion. Your team helps choose the device and teaches its care.

Before insertion and during recovery

Tell the team about allergies, blood thinners, infections and any other cancer medicines. Follow the instructions about eating, drinking and transport; do not stop medicines yourself. Port placement uses local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation or general anaesthetic. The team explains the procedure and risks, including infection, bleeding, clots and insertion complications. Ask how to manage discomfort, protect the wound and resume activity. Report increasing pain, redness, swelling, discharge or fever.

Connection: what the nurse does

The nurse checks the line and treatment, secures the connection and dressing, and starts the pump. Before leaving, you should have the disconnection location and time, contact numbers including out of hours, and the written spill instructions. Ask to be shown what normal pump progress looks like. You do not need to calculate a rate.

Carrying it, sleeping and washing

Use the supplied carrier and the position recommended for your pump. Keep tubing free from pulling and kinks. Ask how to place the pump safely for sleep and how to protect the line and dressing when washing. Do not immerse it or swim. Avoid direct heat and cold. An elastomeric pump has a balloon that shrinks slowly and may have no alarm; an electronic pump has different instructions. Follow the instructions for the device you actually receive.

Disconnection is not port removal

Keep the appointment even if the pump looks empty, unless the team changes the plan. Staff check the pump and line and arrange safe disposal. After flushing and other line care as required, the port needle may be removed and a small dressing applied. The implanted port stays in place until a separate removal procedure is arranged. A PICC remains in place and needs scheduled dressing and flushing care.

Leakage, disconnection or unexpected pump progress

Call the team immediately if the pump leaks, is damaged, finishes unexpectedly early, appears not to run, or the line disconnects. Follow the emergency or spill steps you have been specifically taught, including use of a clamp if instructed. Do not reconnect, change settings or disconnect the pump yourself. Keep other people away from leaked medicine. If medicine touches skin, rinse it off and wash with soap and water, then seek advice.

Pain, swelling or feeling unwell

Contact oncology immediately for pain or swelling around the line, a wet/loose dressing, redness, fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell. A painful swollen arm or neck needs urgent advice. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty or collapse: call 112 / 999. Do not wait for pump disconnection or the next clinic visit.

The drawings show general steps, not instructions for inserting or accessing a line. Your hospital confirms its own arrangements and the care instructions for your device.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Before the visit

Review all medicines and supplements, especially strong liver-enzyme inhibitors or inducers, statins and other liver-toxic medicines. Avoid alcohol. Confirm steroid premedication, blood tests, pump training, line care, disconnection and verified contact numbers.

STEP 2

Time in the unit

Central venous access is strongly recommended. Trained staff connect the prescribed 24-hour infusion and confirm its actual disconnection time. Dexamethasone 20 mg is given intravenously 30 minutes beforehand. The full visit includes checks, preparation and line care.

STEP 3

Intravenous access and line care

The team chooses appropriate intravenous access and explains its care. Report pain, burning, redness or swelling during treatment immediately. If a port or peripherally inserted central catheter is used, confirm dressing, flushing, infection precautions and eventual removal arrangements. Confirm spill or leak instructions and who to call if the pump alarms, becomes disconnected or appears to finish early. Never change settings or disconnect it yourself.

STEP 4

Home support

Dexamethasone 20 mg intravenously 30 minutes before each infusion is required for anti-sickness treatment and appears to help reduce liver toxicity. This regimen has moderate nausea/vomiting risk; agree the complete prevention and rescue prescription without duplicate steroids. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, a white-cell growth factor, may be used for low counts under local policy; it is not universally required.

STEP 5

Next appointment

Confirm the next treatment, tests and any scan before leaving.

General chemotherapy risks

Discuss low blood counts with serious infection or sepsis, anaemia and bleeding; nausea or vomiting; mouth soreness, appetite or taste changes, diarrhoea or constipation; fatigue, hair, nail and skin changes; changes in sexual wellbeing; allergic reactions and intravenous-line complications; blood clots, including clots in the legs or lungs; fertility, pregnancy and breastfeeding effects; and the possibility of lasting organ injury, rare later cancers or death from serious treatment complications. Trabectedin versus dacarbazine in previously treated liposarcoma or leiomyosarcoma

A randomised phase III trial in previously treated liposarcoma or leiomyosarcoma found longer progression-free survival with trabectedin than dacarbazine. The final analysis did not demonstrate longer overall survival. This supports a disease-control discussion, not a guarantee that treatment lengthens an individual life.

The trial population had prior anthracycline and another systemic regimen. It does not establish the same benefit for every soft tissue sarcoma subtype. Survival results from first-line doxorubicin/trabectedin induction followed by maintenance belong to a different treatment strategy.

Sources: trial · nccp · final-survival

What is the treatment trying to do?

When do I attend?

Trabectedin 1.5 mg/m² is infused over 24 hours starting on day 1 of each 21-day cycle. Treatment continues while benefit and tolerability justify it. The actual dose, start time and any delay require a new safety review.

Does a delay mean treatment has failed?

No. Blood counts, organ function and symptoms can make a delay or dose change safer. Your team decides whether and when to give the next dose.

Who do I contact if unwell?

Use your verified oncology daytime or out-of-hours number. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Severe symptoms require 112/999.

Can I restart after a delay or safety hold?

Only after the treating team gives an explicit plan. A normal result does not erase a previous severe reaction or permanent-stop decision.

Are safety tests the same as checking benefit?

No. Safety tests look for treatment effects on the body. Scans, symptoms and function help the specialist assess tumour control.
