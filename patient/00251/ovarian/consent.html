Carboplatin every four weeks for ovarian, fallopian tube or peritoneal cancer

Patient nameDate of birthHospital / MRN

Treatment and purpose

After surgery, treatment may reduce the risk of the cancer returning. For advanced or recurrent disease, it aims to control cancer and symptoms. Ask which aim applies to you. Three groups are covered for epithelial ovarian, primary peritoneal or fallopian tube cancer: after surgery when combination chemotherapy is unsuitable; first treatment of stage III–IV disease when surgery is infeasible and combination treatment unsuitable; or recurrence that remains sensitive to platinum.

Treatment schedule. One infusion on day 1 every 28 days (four weeks). Bring your written next appointment date. Your team plans the number of cycles and reviews response, blood results and treatment tolerance.

Regimen-specific side effects

Carboplatin

Effect to discussWhat to look for and when to get help
Low blood counts and infectionCall urgently for fever, shivering or unexpected bleeding. Red cells and platelets can fall as well as white cells.
Nausea and vomitingTake the prescribed anti-sickness medicines. Call if vomiting continues or you cannot keep fluids down.
Allergy, kidneys and hearingTell the nurse immediately about an infusion reaction. Kidney function guides dosing. Report new ringing in the ears or hearing changes.

Treatment discussion and decision

Carboplatin every four weeks for ovarian, fallopian tube or peritoneal cancer

Benefits, alternatives and individual risks

Studies compared carboplatin alone with combinations in people receiving their first treatment and in people whose cancer had returned. Some combinations controlled the cancer longer. Your team weighs this against which treatment you can tolerate. The studies involve different settings and do not compare 21-day with 28-day administration. Neither provides an individual prognosis. EWOC-1 was stopped early in a selected vulnerable population; age alone does not establish that combination treatment is unsuitable.

Discuss why a single medicine fits your situation, the purpose and expected benefit, infection and bleeding, allergy after repeated exposure, kidney function, fertility and alternatives such as combination treatment when appropriate.

Discuss other treatment options, symptom-focused care, fertility, contraception and the effects that matter most to you. You can ask for more time and change your decision.

Options discussed, personal risks and questions

Urgent contact Call the 24-hour oncology line for fever, chills, feeling suddenly unwell, unusual bleeding, persistent vomiting or markedly reduced urine. Tell the nurse immediately about itching, wheeze, throat tightness or faintness during treatment. Emergency services: 112 or 999.
