When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call your oncology team immediately for coughing blood, significant bleeding, severe tummy pain, sudden breathlessness or chest pain, or a new severe headache, confusion, seizure or vision change. Fever or sudden illness also needs urgent advice, especially with chemotherapy.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Three medicines. Different jobs.

To help control advanced non-squamous lung cancer alongside the prescribed chemotherapy, then possibly as continuation treatment.

Anti-VEGF antibody

Bevacizumab

Bevacizumab
Blocks the VEGF growth signal

Bevacizumab binds VEGF, a signal that helps new blood vessels grow. It can slow tumour growth but also affect normal blood vessels and wound healing.

Blocks the VEGF growth signal↓Cancer growth disrupted↓Healthy tissues may also be affected

What to watch for
Report bleeding, severe tummy pain, unusual headache or vision changes urgently. Tell the team about any surgery or wound.

Understanding the evidence

AVAiL showed longer progression-free survival with bevacizumab added to the studied chemotherapy, without a significant overall-survival improvement.

Direct evidence for 7.5 mg/kg with the studied cisplatin/gemcitabine backbone; outcomes must not be borrowed from 15 mg/kg carboplatin/paclitaxel trials or colorectal studies.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Who confirms my next dose?

Your treating team checks the prescription, tests and symptoms. A completed-cycle stamp records past treatment; it does not authorise the next dose.

What if the plan changes?

Ask the team to update your phase, medicines, test appointments and cycle record. Use the updated prescription.

When are my scans?

Your team sets a response and follow-up plan for your cancer and symptoms. This document does not invent a fixed scan or tumour-marker timetable.

Try explaining the plan back: Which medicines are you having now? What changes after induction? What would make you call today? Ask your team to fill any gaps.
