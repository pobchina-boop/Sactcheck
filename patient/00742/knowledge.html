Understand your treatment

How your medicines work

Lomustine chemotherapy and bevacizumab targeted therapy are used together to help control recurrent glioblastoma. Discuss the expected benefit, symptom goals and treatment burden with your team.

Oral chemotherapy capsule

Lomustine

CCNU
Damages DNA

Lomustine is an oral nitrosourea chemotherapy that can reach the brain. It damages DNA and interferes with cell division. Its effects on healthy blood-forming cells can appear several weeks after the dose and can build up over successive cycles. This explains the long gap between doses and the need for continuing blood tests.

One scheduled dose↓DNA damaged↓Delayed blood-count effects possible

What to watch for
This is one dose on Day 1, not a daily medicine. If an extra dose is taken, get urgent advice even if you feel well. Keep blood tests after the dose and report fever, bruising, bleeding, new cough or breathlessness.

Sources: nccp · lomustine-smpc

Targeted antibody given into a vein

Bevacizumab

Avastin / prescribed biosimilar
Blocks the blood-vessel growth signal VEGF

Bevacizumab binds VEGF, a signal involved in making and maintaining blood vessels. Blocking this signal can change the blood supply and leakiness of tumour vessels. It can also affect normal vessels, which helps explain high blood pressure, protein in the urine, bleeding, clots and slower wound healing. A scan change does not by itself prove that the tumour has stopped growing.

VEGF signal blocked↓Tumour vessels affected↓Response and safety reviewed

What to watch for
Report bleeding, severe abdominal pain, new chest or breathing symptoms, sudden neurological changes, headaches with visual changes, and any operation or wound problem. Keep blood-pressure and urine checks.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Two medicines, different routes

Lomustine is a single oral dose; bevacizumab is an infusion. Keep the anti-sickness plan for lomustine. Confirm cycle number and the exact capsule supply before taking it.

Day 2–42: Separate infusion visits and continuing checks

Return for bevacizumab on Day 15 and Day 29. No extra lomustine is scheduled. Continue blood-pressure, urine and blood checks. Report symptoms and planned surgery; each medicine has its own safety decision.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Taking the treatment

Take lomustine only once on the authorised Day 1. The pharmacist must confirm the total dose, capsule strengths, how to take it and anti-sickness timing. Do not open or crush capsules or take another dose to replace a missed or vomited one without advice. Bevacizumab is given into a vein. The usual first infusion lasts 90 minutes; later infusions may take 60 and then 30 minutes if tolerated. Checks and observation add time. Report flushing, rash, breathing difficulty, dizziness or other new symptoms immediately.

STEP 2

Missed, vomited or held medicine

If a capsule dose is missed or vomited, call the oncology team or pharmacist before taking more. Do not double up or use leftover capsules. An infusion or capsule held for toxicity needs a new team decision.

STEP 3

Medicine and supplement review

Bring all prescription, over-the-counter and herbal medicines, vitamins and supplements to review. Include steroids, seizure medicines, anticoagulants and any recent antibiotics or antifungals. Tell the team before starting or stopping a medicine.

STEP 4

Surgery, procedures and wounds

Tell all treating teams that you receive bevacizumab. Major surgery needs an appropriate treatment interval and a fully healed wound before restarting; minor and dental procedures also need individual advice. Do not decide timing yourself or rely on the wallet stamp.

STEP 5

Capsule supply and handling

Lomustine is supplied as an unlicensed medicine in this NCCP protocol. Confirm pharmacy supply in advance. Keep the labelled pack away from children and do not open or crush capsules. Return unused chemotherapy to the pharmacy; contact staff if swallowing or handling is difficult.

STEP 6

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and fertility

Discuss fertility preservation and contraception before starting. Treatment can harm a pregnancy. Do not breastfeed during treatment. Ask for the written medicine-specific precautions and the required periods after stopping; these may differ between products.

STEP 7

Appointments and transport

Before starting: Blood count, kidney/liver tests, glucose, urine protein, blood pressure and cardiac history/examination; NCCP lung testing for patients at high risk. Current lomustine SmPC additionally advises baseline lung-function assessment. During treatment: Regular blood count, kidney/liver tests and urine protein; blood pressure before each bevacizumab dose and after treatment. Current lomustine SmPC advises weekly blood counts for at least six weeks after a dose and continuing lung assessment. MRI and neurological review follow your individual treatment plan. Echocardiography is considered only when indicated, including previous anthracycline treatment or chest-wall radiotherapy. If taking warfarin, INR is checked weekly until stable and before subsequent cycles.

STEP 8

Help with the daily plan

Agree who can help with transport, prescriptions, phone calls and recording symptoms if memory, vision, weakness or fatigue makes them difficult. Your consent and preferences guide what is shared. Keep the printed schedule and actual urgent contacts together.

STEP 9

Off-label treatment and infusion decisions

Bevacizumab for glioblastoma is outside its licensed indication in Ireland. The team should explain the reason for using it, expected benefit, uncertainty and alternatives as part of consent. Any rapid infusion outside the standard schedule has a separate local consent and governance process.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Lomustine nausea prevention

Lomustine has moderate-to-high oral emetic potential. Take the prescribed prevention and rescue plan around the single dose. Contact the team if nausea or vomiting prevents fluids or essential medicines; do not automatically repeat a vomited chemotherapy dose.

Bevacizumab support is separate

Bevacizumab alone has minimal emetic potential. Routine anti-sickness drugs or steroid premedication are not automatically required for every infusion; the team may prescribe specific medicines after a previous reaction or for another reason.

Blood pressure, urine and wound care

Keep prescribed blood-pressure medicine and urine checks. Follow the team’s plan for any home measurements. Report wounds and upcoming surgery or dental treatment early, so timing and healing can be assessed before another infusion.

Separate steroid, seizure and bowel plans

Keep steroids and seizure medicines on their own written schedules if prescribed. Ask what to do after a missed or vomited dose. Other medicines can worsen constipation; agree a bowel plan and have all new medicines checked for interactions.

Evidence and uncertainty

Adding bevacizumab delayed measured progression in the trial but did not improve overall survival. Treatment goals and burdens need an individual discussion. The trial used bevacizumab 10 mg/kg every two weeks, not this NCCP 5 mg/kg every two weeks schedule. No trial survival or response number is assigned to this dose schedule. Trial lomustine dose escalation and treatment duration do not replace the current NCCP regimen. Historical pathology, patient selection and later treatments limit individual prediction.

Treatment reference · 04

Understanding your particular glioma

Your diagnosis includes the tumour’s cells, molecular findings and behaviour, not just the word brain cancer.

The name and location

Gliomas begin in supporting cells of the brain or spinal cord. Astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma and glioblastoma are different diagnoses. A tumour that spread to the brain from another organ is a brain metastasis and is not covered by these glioma regimens. Record the exact integrated diagnosis and where it is located.

Sources: nccn-patient

Grade is not the same as stage

Grade describes features linked to how a tumour behaves. Adult gliomas are usually discussed by their type, grade and molecular findings rather than the stage system used for many other cancers. Grade 4 does not mean cancer has spread through the body. Ask your specialist to explain your own report and any older terminology in the treatment protocol.

Sources: nccn-patient · nci-cns

IDH and 1p/19q

IDH1 or IDH2 changes and loss of parts of chromosomes 1p and 19q help classify particular gliomas. The combination of findings matters: one result should not be read in isolation. These tests are usually performed on tumour tissue. They do not automatically mean a change has been inherited or that relatives need testing.

Sources: nccn-patient

MGMT promoter methylation

MGMT is involved in repairing certain DNA damage. Methylation of its promoter can reduce that repair activity and helps the team discuss the likely benefit of temozolomide in relevant settings. It is not a blood tumour marker, a percentage of tumour left, or an individual survival prediction. Record the reported result and ask how it affects your treatment choice.

Sources: nccn-patient

A useful diagnosis record

Keep the biopsy or operation date, pathology wording, grade, IDH result, 1p/19q result and MGMT result if tested. Mark a result as pending or not applicable rather than assuming it is negative. Ask whether specialist pathology review or further testing would change the plan.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 05

Tests and scans: what each one tells you

Safety blood tests, tumour tissue tests and MRI answer different questions.

Safety blood tests

A full blood count checks infection-fighting white cells, oxygen-carrying red cells and platelets that help stop bleeding. Kidney and liver tests help assess treatment safety. Attend the regimen’s required tests even when you feel well; a reassuring scan does not replace them. The schedule in this guide separates checks before, during and after treatment.

Sources: nccp · nccn-patient

MRI and neurological review

MRI is the main scan used to assess many gliomas. Your team also considers symptoms, strength, movement, speech, memory, daily function and medicines such as steroids. Tell staff before MRI about implants or metal, kidney problems, previous contrast reactions or difficulty lying still. Ask about support if scans make you anxious.

Sources: nccn-patient

Changes on a scan need interpretation

After treatment, inflammation and other treatment effects can resemble tumour growth on MRI. This is sometimes called pseudoprogression. The team may need comparison scans, specialist imaging or further assessment before deciding what a change means. New or worsening symptoms still need prompt review; do not assume they are harmless treatment effects.

Sources: nccn-patient

An individual follow-up timetable

Write the next MRI date, what question it should answer and who will explain the result. Timing depends on your diagnosis, treatment phase and previous findings. General follow-up intervals in an international booklet are not automatically your schedule. These regimens do not require a generic CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or a blood tumour marker before every cycle.

Sources: nccn-patient

Keep results with decisions

Use the passport to distinguish a test being booked, completed and reviewed. Record the agreed next step and whom to call if an appointment or result is missing. Do not alter chemotherapy or steroids yourself in response to a number or scan report.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 06

Brain symptoms, medicines and when to call

Symptoms may reflect the tumour, swelling, treatment, another medicine or a separate illness.

Know your usual symptoms

Write down your usual headache, strength, walking, speech, vision, memory, mood, sleep and seizure pattern before treatment. Note new changes, when they started and how they affect daily tasks. With your permission, a trusted person can help spot changes or record what happened.

Sources: nccn-patient

Urgent neurological changes

Use your oncology unit’s written emergency plan. Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice even on days without chemotherapy. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. If you have an established seizure rescue plan, follow it and its emergency instructions.

Sources: nccp · nccn-patient

Steroids need a separate written plan

Steroids may be prescribed to reduce swelling around the tumour. They are not automatically needed for everyone. Ask for the exact dose, time, review date and any taper plan. Do not stop or change them suddenly without advice. Report sleep or mood changes, increased thirst, infection symptoms or muscle weakness and ask which additional checks or protection you need.

Sources: nccn-patient · nhs-dexamethasone

Seizure medicines and rescue instructions

If prescribed, take seizure medicines on the agreed schedule. Ask what to do after a missed or vomited dose and whom to contact for breakthrough symptoms. Some medicines can affect mood, alertness or interact with treatment. Do not start, stop or switch them yourself. A rescue medicine needs an individual plan and training for whoever may give it.

Sources: nccn-patient

Safety at home and outside

Ask your clinical team about driving, work, bathing, swimming, heights and machinery in light of your symptoms and any seizures. Local medical and licensing rules need individual advice; this guide does not give a universal driving clearance date. Occupational therapy can help adapt daily routines and the home.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 07

Your overall treatment plan and choices

Treatment should account for the tumour and the life you want to lead.

Understand the sequence

Surgery or biopsy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and follow-up have different jobs. Ask your team to draw the intended sequence and identify where you are now. Concurrent treatment means treatments overlap; adjuvant treatment follows the main treatment. A change of phase may change the dose, blood tests and support medicines.

Sources: nccn-patient

Agree what treatment is trying to achieve

Ask whether the aim is to control the tumour, delay growth, relieve symptoms, preserve function or a combination of these. Discuss likely benefits, burdens, alternatives and what would prompt a pause or stop. The answer depends on the exact diagnosis, previous treatment and your priorities.

Put evidence in context

Clinical trials describe groups of people and may use older pathology names or different schedules. They cannot predict one person’s response or lifespan. Ask how closely the evidence matches your tumour and treatment. No benefit number from one schedule should automatically be applied to another.

Discuss options available to you

Ask about appropriate clinical trials, specialist opinions, symptom support and other treatment options. A medicine or device described in an international guide is not automatically licensed, funded or suitable in Ireland. The treating multidisciplinary team explains what is available and relevant.

EORTC 26101: evidence and schedule limits

The randomised trial compared lomustine plus bevacizumab with lomustine alone after glioblastoma progression.

Adding bevacizumab delayed measured progression in the trial but did not improve overall survival. Treatment goals and burdens need an individual discussion.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

What is my exact diagnosis and how does this regimen fit?

NCCP 00742: recurrent malignant glioblastoma; bevacizumab use is off-label. Ask for the pathology, relevant molecular findings and the treatment goal in plain language.

Which medicines do I receive on which days?

Each 42-day cycle has lomustine capsules taken once on Day 1 and bevacizumab infusions on Days 1, 15 and 29. Up to six cycles are planned, with review before each treatment.

Which tests must happen before the next treatment?

Before starting: Blood count, kidney/liver tests, glucose, urine protein, blood pressure and cardiac history/examination; NCCP lung testing for patients at high risk. Current lomustine SmPC additionally advises baseline lung-function assessment. During treatment: Regular blood count, kidney/liver tests and urine protein; blood pressure before each bevacizumab dose and after treatment. Current lomustine SmPC advises weekly blood counts for at least six weeks after a dose and continuing lung assessment. MRI and neurological review follow your individual treatment plan. Echocardiography is considered only when indicated, including previous anthracycline treatment or chest-wall radiotherapy. If taking warfarin, INR is checked weekly until stable and before subsequent cycles.

How do I handle a missed, vomited or held dose?

Contact the oncology team or pharmacist for medicine-specific instructions. Do not double a dose or replace treatment that was held for toxicity.

Who helps with steroids, seizures, hearing, memory or daily tasks?

Ask for written medicine plans and a named contact. Rehabilitation, practical support and a trusted helper may support the daily plan.

Which symptoms mean I should call now?

Use your oncology unit’s written emergency plan. Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice even on days without chemotherapy. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. If you have an established seizure rescue plan, follow it and its emergency instructions. Call urgently for bleeding, severe abdominal pain, chest symptoms, new headache with visual changes or wound problems. Tell the team about planned surgery before treatment.

When do we review whether treatment is helping?

Agree the next clinical and imaging review, what would count as benefit, how side effects will be weighed and what would lead to continuing, changing or stopping.

Try explaining the plan back: Can you show the day for each medicine, your next safety tests, the current support plan and whom to call for fever, new neurological symptoms or another urgent change?
