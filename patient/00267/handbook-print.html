My treatment handbook

Two-day EP for emergency treatment of GTN

Your treatment and how the medicines work

This short first treatment aims to bring the disease under control while your hospital team stabilises you and prepares the next treatment.

Etoposide · Etoposide

Chemotherapy. Stops dividing cells repairing breaks in their DNA. Tell the nurse at once if you feel faint, hot or breathless during the infusion.

Cisplatin · Cisplatin

Platinum chemotherapy. Joins strands of DNA together so dividing cancer cells cannot copy their DNA normally. Report ringing ears, trouble hearing, tingling, reduced urine or vomiting that prevents drinking.

What the research found

A specialist centre reported fewer early deaths in a later group of people treated for high-risk disease. In that group, doctors selected some of the people most seriously ill for short induction chemotherapy.

Hospital care also changed over time. The study cannot tell us exactly how much of the improvement came from the two-day treatment.

Common effects and what to do

What you may notice

Tiredness, changes in appetite and nausea can affect meals and daily activity. Tell your team early if you are eating or drinking less.

At home

Hair loss and a sore mouth can occur. Ask for mouth care or dietary advice before eating and drinking become difficult.

Serious effects: when to call

Hearing changes · Cisplatin

Report ringing or muffled hearing before the next infusion. Changes can persist.

Kidney injury · Cisplatin

Tell the team if you pass much less urine or cannot keep drinks down.

Nerve changes · Cisplatin

Report new tingling, numb feet or difficulty with buttons.

Infection or bleeding · Etoposide

Call urgently for fever, shivering, bleeding that will not stop or feeling suddenly unwell.

Infusion reaction · Etoposide

During the infusion tell the nurse immediately about tight breathing, dizziness or flushing.

Sore mouth · Etoposide

Report painful ulcers or difficulty drinking so the team can help.

Contact your oncology service urgently. Tell ward staff immediately about new breathlessness, coughing blood, a severe headache, confusion or a seizure. At home, call 112 or 999 for severe breathing difficulty, collapse or a seizure. Call the oncology number urgently for fever or shaking chills. If you have severe breathing difficulty, severe chest pain, collapse or a seizure, call 112 or 999. Follow the 24-hour contact instructions from your treating hospital.

Practical support

Etoposide

Tell the nurse at once if you feel faint, hot or breathless during the infusion.

Cisplatin

Report ringing ears, trouble hearing, tingling, reduced urine or vomiting that prevents drinking.

Fluids and nausea

Kidney-protecting fluid is given before cisplatin. Follow your dated anti-sickness plan and call if vomiting prevents drinking.

Gaps between treatment visits

Keep your 24-hour oncology number and treatment card with you. Report fever or sudden illness straight away, including at night and during a break.

Planning your visits

Your appointments

Both medicines on days 1 and 2. The specialist may repeat the block weekly for 1–3 weeks.

Taking treatment

Treatment starts urgently in hospital. On each treatment day etoposide runs into a vein over about one hour, followed by cisplatin over about two hours. Extra fluid before cisplatin helps protect the kidneys.

Planning the next step

The GTN team decides when to switch to EMA/CO or EMA/EP, combinations of chemotherapy medicines used after induction.

Preparing for your oncology discussion

  • Why is the first treatment only two days? Your team can begin treating the disease while you are acutely unwell. They reassess your breathing, organ function and hormone results before choosing the next combination.
  • What happens after this part of treatment? The GTN team decides when to switch to EMA/CO or EMA/EP, combinations of chemotherapy medicines used after induction.
  • Which symptoms need urgent help? Tell ward staff immediately about new breathlessness, coughing blood, a severe headache, confusion or a seizure. At home, call 112 or 999 for severe breathing difficulty, collapse or a seizure. Call the oncology number urgently for fever or shaking chills.
  • Why do I still need the hormone test? GTN cells can produce a hormone called human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG). Changes in its level help your specialist see how treatment is working, and follow-up tests can pick up an increase before symptoms appear.
