When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call urgently for severe diarrhoea or dehydration, bleeding, new swelling, much less urine, severe skin blistering or symptoms of low blood sugar. Chest pain, severe breathlessness, collapse, seizure or severe headache with vision changes needs emergency assessment.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

How your medicines work

Sunitinib is a daily targeted capsule used to help control a pancreatic NET that cannot be removed or has spread and is progressing. The team reviews control, side effects and your priorities.

Targeted therapy capsule

Sunitinib

Sutent
Blocks several growth and blood-vessel signals

Sunitinib blocks several signalling proteins involved in tumour growth and blood-vessel formation. This can slow a pancreatic NET. Effects on healthy blood vessels and other tissues help explain high blood pressure, bleeding, wound-healing problems, thyroid changes and sore hands or feet.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Capsules and missed doses

The usual starting dose for this pancreatic NET schedule is 37.5 mg once daily. Follow your label, swallow whole with water and take at the same time each day consistently with or without food. If a dose is missed, do not take an additional dose; take the usual prescribed dose the next day.

STEP 2

Interactions

Avoid grapefruit and St John’s wort. Ask the pharmacist to review every medicine and supplement, including blood thinners, antibiotics and medicines affecting heart rhythm.

STEP 3

Surgery and dental work

Tell your team about planned operations, wounds or invasive dental work. A treatment interruption may be needed for major surgery; the oncology and surgical teams agree when to stop and restart. Ask about a dental assessment.

STEP 4

Storage and supply

Keep capsules in their original labelled packaging, away from children, and follow storage instructions. Return unused medicine to the pharmacy. Bring your current prescription and medicine list to review.

STEP 5

Pregnancy, feeding and fertility

Tell the team about pregnancy, planned pregnancy or breastfeeding before treatment. Ask for the contraception and fertility plan specific to sunitinib, including how long precautions continue after stopping.

STEP 6

Tests and visits

Baseline blood count, kidney/liver, thyroid and blood pressure; urine protein assessment and cardiac tests as indicated. Counts, kidney and liver tests before each cycle; BP at each attendance; thyroid every 12 weeks. Heart, urine and glucose monitoring are individualised. Scans and tumour markers follow the consultant plan.

What the trial can tell us

Sunitinib delayed progression compared with placebo in this population. The trial stopped early; early survival findings require caution and do not predict one person’s lifespan. The results apply to the pancreatic NET population and continuous dose studied, not every NET site or another sunitinib schedule.

Treatment reference · 04

Understanding your particular NET

Several different features help the team choose treatment.

Where it started and where it is now

A neuroendocrine tumour (NET) starts in cells that receive signals and can release hormones. The original organ is the primary site. A pancreatic NET is different from the more common pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Stage describes how far the cancer has spread; the stage system depends on its original site. A tumour in the liver may be a NET that spread there from elsewhere.

Sources: nccn-patient

Differentiation, grade and Ki-67

Differentiation describes how the cells look under the microscope. Grade describes their growth activity. Ki-67 is a laboratory measure of the proportion of tumour cells showing a marker of cell growth and helps assess grade in relevant NETs. It is not the percentage of your body affected and is not a survival percentage. Ask for the exact pathology wording, grade and Ki-67 result, including the sample date.

Sources: nccn-patient

Grade is different from stage

A low-grade NET can have spread, and a high-grade tumour is not automatically stage 4. Well-differentiated grade 3 NET and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma are different diagnoses with different treatment choices. These medicine guides must not be applied to another diagnosis simply because its name contains neuroendocrine.

Sources: nccn-patient

Functioning and nonfunctioning

A functioning NET causes a hormone-related syndrome. A nonfunctioning NET does not cause a recognisable excess-hormone syndrome, but it can still grow or cause symptoms by its size or location. Ask which description applies to you. A positive somatostatin receptor scan does not by itself mean the tumour is functioning.

Sources: nccn-patient · nci-pnet

Your diagnosis record

Keep the primary site, differentiation, grade, Ki-67 if reported, stage or sites of spread, hormone syndrome and receptor-scan result together in your passport. Ask the team to explain changes rather than trying to interpret one result in isolation.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 05

Making sense of tests and results

Safety tests, tumour tests and hormone tests answer different questions.

Blood tests for treatment safety

Blood counts measure infection-fighting cells, oxygen-carrying cells and platelets. Kidney and liver tests help the team assess how your body can handle treatment. The schedule for your medicine is shown separately. Attend these checks even when you feel well; a scan does not replace them.

Sources: nccn-patient · nccp

CT, MRI and receptor imaging

CT and MRI help show tumour size and where it is. A somatostatin receptor (SSTR) scan looks for receptor targets that certain treatments can attach to. An FDG-PET scan answers a different question about tumour activity and may be useful in selected cases. You will not necessarily need every scan. The specialist decides which test can change your care.

Sources: nccn-patient

Hormones and tumour markers are individual

The team may measure selected blood or urine markers when they answer a question about your NET or symptoms. These can include serotonin-related testing such as 5-HIAA for selected patients, or specific pancreatic hormones. Chromogranin A is not a universal test before every treatment. A changing marker alone does not prove cancer growth. Record why each test is being done, how to prepare and who will explain it.

Sources: nccn-patient · nci-net

Preparing for a test

Ask whether you need fasting, a timed urine collection, dietary preparation or a medicine review. Follow the laboratory’s instructions for your particular test. Do not stop an acid-suppressing medicine, diabetes medicine or hormone-control treatment yourself. Tell the team about kidney problems, allergies and all medicines before a scan or blood test.

Sources: nccn-patient · nci-net

Heart and inherited-risk checks

A heart ultrasound may be needed for particular treatment risks or to assess heart-valve problems associated with a hormone syndrome. It is not required on the same timetable for every NET. Depending on the diagnosis, age and family history, the team may offer genetic counselling; some NETs occur with inherited conditions such as MEN1. A tumour gene test and an inherited-risk test are not interchangeable.

Sources: nccn-patient

Who does what, and when?

Use the passport to write each test, its purpose, planned date, booking service, result-review contact and next step. Your consultant sets response-scan and marker intervals. The postoperative schedules in general guides are not automatically the right schedule while you are taking these medicines.

Sources: nccn-patient · nccp

Treatment reference · 06

Hormone symptoms and day-to-day changes

A new symptom may come from the NET, treatment or another illness.

Build a picture of what is normal for you

Record your usual bowel pattern, flushing, pain, appetite, weight and energy before starting. Note what changes, how long it lasts, what you were taking and how it affects daily life. This helps the team distinguish a hormone problem from treatment toxicity without assuming that every symptom is cancer progression.

Sources: nccn-patient

Hormone-related symptoms

Depending on the tumour, excess hormones may cause flushing, frequent watery stools, wheezing, acid-related pain or blood-sugar disturbances. An insulin-producing tumour can cause low blood sugar. Not every NET causes these problems. Ask which symptoms matter for your diagnosis and whether you need a separate rescue plan.

Sources: nccn-patient

Hormone treatment is a separate part of the plan

Somatostatin analogue injections and other symptom treatments may continue alongside anticancer treatment when prescribed. They may have their own appointment dates. Never stop them simply because tablets are paused. PRRT requires a carefully coordinated interruption and restart plan, which the nuclear-medicine service must supply.

Sources: nccn-patient · nccp

When a change is urgent

Follow your oncology emergency plan for sudden or severe symptoms. Collapse, severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, confusion or uncontrolled bleeding needs emergency assessment. Severe diarrhoea, persistent vomiting, dehydration, fever or sudden illness needs urgent oncology advice. Do not wait for a scan, an online answer or the next clinic visit.

Sources: nccp

Procedures and hormone syndromes

Tell the surgical, dental, anaesthetic or imaging team if you have a hormone-producing NET or a previous hormone crisis. Ask your specialist whether special preparation is needed. Carry your current medicines and emergency plan; the wallet card supports this information but does not replace specialist instructions.

Sources: nccn-patient · nccp

Treatment reference · 07

Your overall plan and choices

Your priorities belong in the treatment plan.

What are we trying to achieve?

Ask whether the main aim is to slow growth, reduce tumour size, relieve hormone symptoms, make another treatment possible or protect day-to-day quality of life. The aim and likely benefit depend on your particular NET and previous treatment. A stable scan may represent useful control even without tumour shrinkage.

Sources: nccn-patient

How treatments fit together

Some people need surgery, hormone-control treatment, tablets, chemotherapy, PRRT, liver-directed treatment or external radiotherapy at different points. These are not all automatically part of your regimen. Ask the multidisciplinary team to draw your own sequence and name the clinician coordinating it.

Sources: nccn-patient

Choosing between options

Ask why this option suits your tumour site, grade, growth rate, symptoms, receptor status and other health conditions. Discuss alternatives, the option of observation when appropriate, supportive care, and any suitable clinical trial. A treatment listed in a US guide is not a guarantee of availability or funding in Ireland.

Sources: nccn-patient

Evidence is about groups

A trial can measure time before cancer worsens, tumour shrinkage or survival. These are different outcomes. A group result does not predict your own response or lifespan. Ask how closely the study patients match your diagnosis and what benefit would make the treatment worthwhile to you.

Sources: nccn-patient

Review points and shared decisions

Agree when you will review symptoms, tests and scans, and what would lead to continuing, delaying, changing or stopping treatment. Discuss work, caring duties, travel and the effects you most want to avoid. You can revisit your choices as your situation changes.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 08

Eating, energy, practical help and support

Supportive care can be used alongside treatment from the start.

Food, weight and digestion

Tell the team about weight loss, poor appetite, early fullness, persistent diarrhoea or greasy stools. Ask for a dietitian familiar with NETs and your surgery or medicines. Different causes need different support; do not assume every bowel symptom needs the same diet or medicine. Ask whether digestive-enzyme assessment or replacement is relevant to you.

Sources: nccn-patient · nci-pnet

Fluids and safe activity

Follow your individual fluid advice, especially with diarrhoea, kidney disease or heart problems. Balance manageable activity with rest. Tell the team if fatigue, dizziness, pain or breathlessness limits usual tasks; these symptoms can have treatable causes.

Sources: nccn-patient · nccp

Emotions, relationships and quality of life

Living with repeated tests and treatment can affect sleep, mood, relationships and confidence. Ask about psychological support, sexual health, fertility advice, symptom-control services and support for carers. Supportive or palliative care aims to improve comfort and daily life and can accompany active anticancer treatment.

Sources: nccn-patient

Practical help

Ask your nurse or social worker about transport, work, finances, home support, caring responsibilities and local NET support organisations. A general resource list may include services in other countries; your team can identify options available to you.

Sources: nccn-patient

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Is my dose the continuous pancreatic NET schedule?

For this NCCP pancreatic NET regimen, the standard starting schedule is 37.5 mg once daily continuously, with 28-day reviews and no planned rest week. Your own prescribed dose may differ.

Who checks my BP and when are my 12-week thyroid tests?

Blood pressure is checked at each attendance and thyroid tests every 12 weeks, with extra tests if indicated. Ask who will book them and where to record readings.

What should I do about diarrhoea or painful hands and feet?

Report these symptoms early and follow the written rescue and hold plan. Painful hands or feet can require interruption even at the first episode; the team confirms dose changes and restarting.

Do my diabetes or blood-thinning medicines need extra monitoring?

Ask the pharmacist to check the full medicine list. Diabetes may require closer glucose checks and changes to diabetes treatment; bleeding risks and interactions need individual review.

What is the plan for dental work or surgery?

Tell the oncology team before arranging procedures. Surgery, wounds and dental work may need a coordinated interruption, with restart decided by the treating teams.

When will we review response and quality of life?

Ask for the planned symptom, scan and treatment-review dates. There is no fixed total number of cycles; continued treatment depends on benefit, tolerability and your priorities.

Try explaining the plan back: Can you explain why this is continuous daily dosing, where to record blood pressure and when painful hands/feet or diarrhoea need a call?
