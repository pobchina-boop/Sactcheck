My treatment handbook

Five-day EP for good-risk metastatic germ-cell tumours

Your treatment and how the medicines work

The aim is to cure a germ-cell tumour that has spread. Each course includes five days of chemotherapy followed by time for recovery.

Etoposide · Etoposide

Chemotherapy. Stops dividing cells repairing breaks in their DNA. Tell the nurse at once if you feel faint, hot or breathless during the infusion.

Cisplatin · Cisplatin

Platinum chemotherapy. Joins strands of DNA together so dividing cancer cells cannot copy their DNA normally. Report ringing ears, trouble hearing, tingling, reduced urine or vomiting that prevents drinking.

What the research found

At one specialist centre, about 94 of every 100 people treated with four cycles were alive five years later without the cancer getting worse. These figures describe a group of people with good-risk disease.

Most people in this study had testicular germ-cell tumours. Your own outlook depends on the type of tumour and how it responds to treatment.

Common effects and what to do

What you may notice

Tiredness, changes in appetite and nausea can affect meals and daily activity. Tell your team early if you are eating or drinking less.

At home

Hair loss and a sore mouth can occur. Ask for mouth care or dietary advice before eating and drinking become difficult.

Serious effects: when to call

Hearing changes · Cisplatin

Report ringing or muffled hearing before the next infusion. Changes can persist.

Kidney injury · Cisplatin

Tell the team if you pass much less urine or cannot keep drinks down.

Nerve changes · Cisplatin

Report new tingling, numb feet or difficulty with buttons.

Infection or bleeding · Etoposide

Call urgently for fever, shivering, bleeding that will not stop or feeling suddenly unwell.

Infusion reaction · Etoposide

During the infusion tell the nurse immediately about tight breathing, dizziness or flushing.

Sore mouth · Etoposide

Report painful ulcers or difficulty drinking so the team can help.

Contact your oncology service urgently. Call your oncology unit urgently for fever, shaking chills, unexpected bleeding or sudden illness, including during the treatment break. Sudden chest pain, collapse or severe breathlessness needs emergency help. If you have severe breathing difficulty, severe chest pain, collapse or a seizure, call 112 or 999. Follow the 24-hour contact instructions from your treating hospital.

Planning your visits

Your appointments

Both medicines on days 1–5 of a 21-day cycle. Four cycles are planned if treatment remains suitable.

Taking treatment

Each treatment day starts with etoposide into a vein over about one hour. You also receive fluid before cisplatin, which runs over about one hour. Allow extra time for blood tests, hydration and review.

Planning the next step

Days 6–21 have no scheduled EP infusions. Your blood count can still fall. The next cycle starts only after review.

Preparing for your oncology discussion

  • Can I skip the last infusion if I feel unwell? Call the team straight away. The prescribing consultant weighs any delay against the aim of cure and tells you what to do about the next visit.
  • What happens after this part of treatment? Days 6–21 have no scheduled EP infusions. Your blood count can still fall. The next cycle starts only after review.
  • Which symptoms need urgent help? Call your oncology unit urgently for fever, shaking chills, unexpected bleeding or sudden illness, including during the treatment break. Sudden chest pain, collapse or severe breathlessness needs emergency help.
  • What are tumour markers? Some germ-cell tumours release substances into the blood. Your team may follow alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), a hormone called beta-hCG, and an enzyme called LDH. They interpret changes alongside your scans.

Can you point to the next infusion date, show which anti-sickness doses you already took and find the 24-hour number?
