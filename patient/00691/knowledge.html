When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Fever, shivers or sudden illness needs immediate oncology advice. Chest pain, collapse or severe breathing difficulty needs emergency help. Call urgently for severe or persistent diarrhoea, severe abdominal pain, inability to drink, reduced urine or a line/pump problem.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

Understand your treatment

Your medicines and what they do

This is a planned course before surgery assessment for locally advanced rectal cancer. Six FOLFIRINOX cycles are followed by capecitabine with pelvic radiotherapy. The team reviews response and the next part of your overall rectal-cancer plan.

Platinum chemotherapy

Oxaliplatin

Eloxatin
Links DNA strands

Oxaliplatin makes links within DNA. These interfere with copying and repair, which can stop a cancer cell dividing. Healthy tissues can also be affected.

Platinum enters cell↓DNA links form↓Copying is disrupted

What to watch for
Cold can trigger tingling or throat discomfort. Tell the team about persistent numbness, difficulty with buttons or walking, or infusion symptoms. New breathing difficulty always needs assessment.

Sources: nccp

Chemotherapy modulator

Folinic acid

Calcium leucovorin
Strengthens fluorouracil action

Folinic acid helps fluorouracil remain bound to an enzyme needed to make DNA. In this regimen it increases fluorouracil activity and can also increase its unwanted effects. It is not a vitamin supplement to start yourself.

Folinic acid↓Stronger enzyme binding↓Fluorouracil effect increases

What to watch for
The combined treatment can increase mouth soreness and diarrhoea. Report these promptly. Rare allergic reactions can occur.

Sources: nccp

Topoisomerase I chemotherapy

Irinotecan

Campto
Interrupts DNA copying

The body converts irinotecan into SN-38. This traps an enzyme called topoisomerase I while DNA is being copied, causing damage that can stop cancer cells dividing. Normal bowel and blood-forming cells can also be affected.

Irinotecan becomes SN-38↓DNA-repair enzyme is trapped↓Dividing cells are damaged

What to watch for
Tell the nurse immediately about cramps, sweating or diarrhoea during the infusion. Diarrhoea starting later needs the separate written treatment and contact plan.

Sources: nccp

Chemotherapy

Fluorouracil

5-FU
Disrupts cell building blocks

Fluorouracil interferes with the production and use of DNA and RNA, the instructions cells need to grow. The infusion extends exposure over approximately two days.

Drug enters cell↓DNA / RNA disrupted↓Growth slows

What to watch for
Diarrhoea, mouth sores and skin soreness may become serious. Chest pain during the pump infusion needs emergency assessment. Unexpected severe early toxicity can occur even after DPD testing.

Sources: nccp

Oral chemotherapy

Capecitabine

Xeloda
Becomes fluorouracil in the body

After you swallow capecitabine, your body changes it into fluorouracil. This interferes with the DNA and RNA processes cells use to grow. Normal cells can also be affected, which helps explain mouth soreness, bowel symptoms and changes in blood counts.

Swallowed tablet↓Converted to fluorouracil↓Cell growth disrupted

What to watch for
Stop tablets and contact the team for significant diarrhoea, painful mouth ulcers, hand-foot soreness affecting activities, fever or dehydration. Chest pain needs urgent assessment. Do not restart until instructed.

Sources: nccp

Local radiation treatment

Pelvic radiotherapy

External beam treatment
Targets the pelvic treatment area

Radiation is directed at the planned area. Capecitabine helps sensitise cancer cells to it. Nearby bowel, bladder and skin may also become irritated. Effects can build over the weeks and may continue after the final session.

Planned radiation field↓Damage to cancer-cell DNA↓Cells lose the ability to multiply

What to watch for
Tell the radiotherapy team about diarrhoea, pain, skin soreness or urinary changes. Discuss possible longer-term bowel, bladder, sexual and fertility effects before treatment.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Infusion and pump connection

The team checks symptoms, blood results and the active treatment plan. Day-ward infusions are followed by connection of the fluorouracil pump. Before leaving, confirm the pump-care instructions, disconnection appointment and contact number.

Day 2–3: The pump is delivering treatment

The pump runs for approximately 46 hours from connection. Keep the device and line secure and dry as taught. The team confirms the actual disconnection appointment. Call for a leaking, disconnected or unexpectedly stopped pump. Do not reconnect, alter settings or disconnect it yourself unless specifically trained and instructed.

Day 4–7: After disconnection

Delayed diarrhoea can begin after the first 24 hours and may continue after pump removal. Use your written plan and contact the team early. Cold-triggered tingling may occur and nerve symptoms can persist. Nausea and tiredness can overlap; symptoms are not restricted to these days. Report poor intake, persistent diarrhoea or mouth pain early. Chest pain or major breathing difficulty requires emergency help.

Day 8–14: Between visits and the next review

Blood counts can fall between visits. Tell the team about bowel symptoms, mouth pain and how you are managing daily activities. Treatment proceeds only after review. Confirm the next induction cycle only after review. After cycle 6, ask for the separate capecitabine and radiotherapy calendar; do not continue FOLFIRINOX automatically.

Your treatment, step by step

Your port and treatment pump

From preparing the line to coming home after disconnection. Your team arranges the appointments and shows you what to do.

1 · Before treatment

Choose and prepare the line

The team explains whether a port or PICC suits your treatment. You receive instructions for the insertion appointment.

2 · Insertion and recovery

A separate appointment may be needed

For a port, numbing medicine is used and a small device is placed under the skin. Ask about sedation, transport and wound care.

3 · Day 1

The nurse connects your pump

After the day-ward medicines, the nurse checks and connects the fluorouracil pump. Ask for a demonstration before leaving.

4 · At home

Treatment continues for about 46 hours

Carry the pump as shown. Keep the dressing dry and protect the line from pulling, heat and damage.

5 · As arranged

Return for disconnection

A trained professional checks the infusion, disconnects the pump and cares for the line. The appointment is based on connection time.

6 · Between cycles

The access device usually stays

A port needle can be removed while the port stays under the skin. A PICC remains in the arm with ongoing dressing and line care.

Port or PICC: what is the difference?

A port is a small chamber beneath the skin, usually on the chest, attached to a tube ending in a large vein. A special needle goes through the skin into the port when it is used. A PICC enters a vein in the upper arm; a short section remains outside under a dressing. Both can deliver a home infusion. Your team helps choose the device and teaches its care.

Before insertion and during recovery

Tell the team about allergies, blood thinners, infections and any other cancer medicines. Follow the instructions about eating, drinking and transport; do not stop medicines yourself. Port placement uses local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation or general anaesthetic. The team explains the procedure and risks, including infection, bleeding, clots and insertion complications. Ask how to manage discomfort, protect the wound and resume activity. Report increasing pain, redness, swelling, discharge or fever.

Connection: what the nurse does

The nurse checks the line and treatment, secures the connection and dressing, and starts the pump. Before leaving, you should have the disconnection location and time, contact numbers including out of hours, and the written spill instructions. Ask to be shown what normal pump progress looks like. You do not need to calculate a rate.

Carrying it, sleeping and washing

Use the supplied carrier and the position recommended for your pump. Keep tubing free from pulling and kinks. Ask how to place the pump safely for sleep and how to protect the line and dressing when washing. Do not immerse it or swim. Avoid direct heat and cold. An elastomeric pump has a balloon that shrinks slowly and may have no alarm; an electronic pump has different instructions. Follow the instructions for the device you actually receive.

Disconnection is not port removal

Keep the appointment even if the pump looks empty, unless the team changes the plan. Staff check the pump and line and arrange safe disposal. After flushing and other line care as required, the port needle may be removed and a small dressing applied. The implanted port stays in place until a separate removal procedure is arranged. A PICC remains in place and needs scheduled dressing and flushing care.

Leakage, disconnection or unexpected pump progress

Call the team immediately if the pump leaks, is damaged, finishes unexpectedly early, appears not to run, or the line disconnects. Follow the emergency or spill steps you have been specifically taught, including use of a clamp if instructed. Do not reconnect, change settings or disconnect the pump yourself. Keep other people away from leaked medicine. If medicine touches skin, rinse it off and wash with soap and water, then seek advice.

Pain, swelling or feeling unwell

Contact oncology immediately for pain or swelling around the line, a wet/loose dressing, redness, fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell. A painful swollen arm or neck needs urgent advice. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty or collapse: call 112 / 999. Do not wait for pump disconnection or the next clinic visit.

The drawings show general steps, not instructions for inserting or accessing a line. Your hospital confirms its own arrangements and the care instructions for your device.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Nausea prevention and rescue

This chemotherapy combination has a high risk of sickness without prevention. Follow the prescribed prevention and rescue medicines. Call if vomiting prevents drinking; do not duplicate steroids or anti-sickness doses.

Blood counts and recovery

Growth-factor injections depend on your risk, blood counts and previous complications. If prescribed, confirm their timing in relation to chemotherapy and pump removal; do not assume everyone receives them.

Early and delayed diarrhoea plans

Atropine is normally prescribed before irinotecan for early cramps, sweating or diarrhoea. It is avoided with glaucoma; tell the team about asthma as well. At home use the separately prescribed delayed-diarrhoea instructions. This can differ from standard over-the-counter dosing. Keep the medicine and urgent-contact plan available.

Cold and mouth care

Avoid cold drinks, cold air and cold objects if they trigger symptoms. Do not use ice chips during oxaliplatin treatment. Ask for non-cold mouth-care advice. New breathing difficulty must still be assessed rather than assumed to be a cold reaction.

Support during chemoradiotherapy

Capecitabine alone has a lower sickness risk than the induction combination, so the supportive prescription changes with the phase. Ask about pelvic skin care, bowel and bladder symptoms, diet and radiotherapy preparation.

Your treatment aim and evidence

The setting and sequence matter.

Why this treatment?

This is a planned course before surgery assessment for locally advanced rectal cancer. Six FOLFIRINOX cycles are followed by capecitabine with pelvic radiotherapy. The team reviews response and the next part of your overall rectal-cancer plan.

Sources: nccp

What studies can tell us

PRODIGE 23 studied the whole treatment strategy: six FOLFIRINOX cycles, chemoradiotherapy, surgery and postoperative chemotherapy. It improved disease-free survival compared with the comparator strategy of chemoradiotherapy, surgery and postoperative chemotherapy without initial FOLFIRINOX. The result cannot be attributed to induction chemotherapy alone and is not a personal prediction of cure.

Sources: trial

Review and your priorities

Ask when benefit will be reviewed and which symptoms, scans or tests will be used. Discuss work, caring responsibilities, time at home and what matters most to you. Delaying or reducing a medicine can be necessary for safety and does not itself prove treatment has failed.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 02

Your treatment schedule

Use your prescription and team-confirmed calendar.

Medicines and sequence

Phase 1 is six cycles every 14 days: oxaliplatin 85 mg/m² and folinic acid 400 mg/m² (200 mg/m² may be considered), irinotecan 180 mg/m², then fluorouracil 2,400 mg/m² continuously over 46 hours; no fluorouracil bolus. Phase 2 is capecitabine 800 mg/m² twice daily on Days 1–5 of each seven-day cycle for five weeks with radiotherapy. NCCP permits a consultant-selected 825 mg/m² alternative; your actual tablet prescription and radiation calendar govern treatment.

Sources: nccp

How long treatment lasts

Six FOLFIRINOX cycles are followed by five weeks of capecitabine with radiotherapy. The multidisciplinary team then confirms assessment and next steps.

Sources: nccp

The disconnection appointment

The fluorouracil infusion lasts 46 hours from connection. Arrange the completion visit before leaving the unit. Pump removal does not remove an implanted port: the port remains until a separate procedure is arranged.

Sources: nccp · pump

Treatment reference · 03

Before, during and after treatment

Testing detects problems that may not cause symptoms.

Before the first dose

Blood count, kidney and liver tests and DPD testing are required before fluoropyrimidine treatment unless the DPD test has already been done. DPD helps the body clear fluorouracil; deficiency changes the treatment plan. A normal result does not exclude all severe toxicity.

Sources: nccp

Before each cycle

Blood count, kidney and liver results and symptoms are reviewed before each cycle. In this two-phase plan that means each 14-day FOLFIRINOX cycle and each weekly capecitabine cycle. Neuropathy is assessed every cycle; report troublesome symptoms sooner. Missing results are not assumed normal.

Sources: nccp

Heart tests are conditional

Tell the team about heart disease and previous chest symptoms. An ECG is needed when cardiac function is compromised or clinical findings warrant it. There is no automatic recurring echocardiogram schedule for every patient.

Sources: nccp

Scans, markers and aftercare

Your consultant decides response scans, tumour-marker use and follow-up according to your cancer and treatment aim. This regimen does not set one scan or marker interval for everyone. Unresolved side effects or abnormal blood results need a follow-up plan.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 04

Bowel symptoms and hydration

Act early; do not wait for the next appointment.

Keep the written plan

Record the change from your usual bowel pattern, including stoma output. Keep the prescribed diarrhoea medicine and your oncology contact details available. Ask for specific instructions before the first treatment.

Sources: nccp

Two different irinotecan reactions

During or soon after infusion, cramps, sweating, a runny nose or diarrhoea can be an early cholinergic reaction: tell the nurse immediately. Diarrhoea starting more than 24 hours later needs the separate prescribed delayed-diarrhoea plan, which may differ from packet instructions. If it persists despite that plan, or you become unwell, seek urgent advice.

Sources: nccp

Urgent symptoms

Contact oncology urgently for persistent diarrhoea, inability to drink, reduced urine, dizziness, blood in stools, fever or severe abdominal pain. Do not assume severe abdominal pain is an ordinary treatment effect. A stoma can make fluid loss less obvious: report a marked increase in output.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 05

Cold sensitivity and longer-lasting nerve symptoms

These are two related but different oxaliplatin problems.

Cold-triggered symptoms

Cold drinks, cold air and cold objects can bring on tingling or throat sensations. Avoid triggers and do not use ice chips during oxaliplatin treatment. New breathing difficulty still needs urgent assessment; it must not be diagnosed at home as simply a cold reaction.

Sources: nccp

Persistent neuropathy

Report numbness, tingling, pain or difficulty doing buttons, writing, holding objects or walking. Symptoms can accumulate over cycles and sometimes persist after treatment. Explain how long symptoms lasted and what activities changed.

Sources: nccp

Review before more oxaliplatin

Nerve assessment is required each cycle for this regimen. The team may change or stop oxaliplatin independently of the other medicines to reduce the risk of lasting harm. Do not wait for a scheduled assessment if symptoms are worsening.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 06

The second phase: tablets and pelvic radiotherapy

This has its own calendar and side-effect plan.

Tablet days and dose

After six FOLFIRINOX cycles, NCCP describes capecitabine 800 mg/m² twice daily on Days 1–5 of each seven-day cycle, for five weeks with radiotherapy. A consultant may select the specified 825 mg/m² alternative. The pharmacy label states your tablet strengths and number of tablets; do not calculate your own dose from this handbook.

Sources: nccp

Taking capecitabine

Swallow tablets whole with water within 30 minutes after food. Do not cut, crush or chew them. If a dose is missed or vomited, do not take a replacement or double the next dose; record it and follow the team’s advice.

Sources: nccp

If treatment is held

Stop capecitabine and contact the team for significant diarrhoea, painful mouth ulcers, hand-foot soreness affecting activity, fever or dehydration. Chest pain needs urgent assessment. Do not restart until instructed. If radiotherapy is cancelled or changed, ask whether that day’s tablets change too.

Sources: nccp

Pelvic effects and preparation

Radiotherapy may affect the bowel, bladder and skin in the treatment area, and can have later effects on bowel function, sexual function and fertility. Ask for the radiation unit’s bladder/bowel preparation, skin-care and symptom plan. Report urinary pain, worsening pelvic symptoms or skin breakdown.

Sources: nccp

After the two phases

The multidisciplinary team arranges assessment of response and suitability for surgery or other next steps. The cited trial included surgery and postoperative chemotherapy, but these are separately agreed parts of your overall plan rather than automatic extra cycles in this document.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 07

Mouth, skin, energy and infection

Frequency and urgency are different.

Mouth and hands or feet

Fluorouracil and capecitabine can cause mouth ulcers and painful redness or peeling of hands and feet. Report symptoms early, especially if eating, drinking, walking or using your hands becomes difficult. Ask for non-irritating mouth care and skin-care advice.

Sources: nccp

Nausea, appetite and fatigue

Use the anti-sickness prescription and call if it is not controlling symptoms. Small regular meals and manageable activity with rest may help daily routines. Fatigue can have several causes; persistent or worsening fatigue may need blood tests or review. Irinotecan may also cause hair thinning or loss; ask about practical support.

Sources: nccp

Low blood counts and infection

Chemotherapy can reduce white cells, red cells and platelets. Fever, shivers or sudden illness needs immediate oncology advice, even if your temperature is not raised. Follow the unit’s temperature instructions and do not wait for a scheduled blood test. Unusual bruising, bleeding, breathlessness or marked weakness also needs review.

Sources: nccp

Heart and infusion symptoms

Fluorouracil and capecitabine can rarely cause serious heart problems. Chest pain, collapse or severe breathing difficulty needs emergency assessment. Tell the nurse immediately if flushing, swelling, wheeze, rash or pain occurs during an infusion.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 09

Supportive medicines and daily life

The prescription changes with the active treatment.

Your supportive prescription

Confirm prevention and rescue medicines for nausea, the diarrhoea plan and any growth-factor injections. Atropine may be used before irinotecan for the early reaction; mention glaucoma and asthma. Do not duplicate anti-sickness medicines, start supplements or take leftover prescriptions without checking with the oncology pharmacist.

Sources: nccp

Medicines and interactions

Bring all prescribed and non-prescribed medicines, supplements and anticoagulants to review. Tell the team before starting antibiotics or a new medicine. Do not independently stop regular treatment such as anticoagulants before a procedure.

Sources: nccp

Fertility, pregnancy and sex

Discuss fertility preservation before starting where relevant. Ask which contraception, pregnancy, breastfeeding and sex precautions apply during treatment and after the last dose. The period depends on your medicines and circumstances.

Sources: nccp

Practical support

Ask about work, driving, travel, nutrition, caring responsibilities and emotional support. Report symptoms in terms of what you can eat, drink or do. An interpreter, another explanation or a written plan can help you and a chosen family member or carer.

Sources: nccp

Carry the treatment card

The example telephone numbers on the wallet card are placeholders. Your team must replace them with working daytime and out-of-hours numbers before patient use. Keep the card with you; the QR opens the regimen information, not your confidential medical record.

Cycle and phase record

Ask the team to stamp or write the treatment actually given. The six card spaces apply only to FOLFIRINOX induction. Record the following five chemoradiotherapy weeks separately in the passport; do not count them as more FOLFIRINOX cycles.

Before leaving

Confirm your next tests and appointments, pump disconnection when relevant, medicines to take at home and how to obtain urgent help. Explain the plan back in your own words; ask for anything unclear to be written down.

Understanding the evidence

Ask which study population and treatment sequence match your plan.

PRODIGE 23 studied the whole treatment strategy: six FOLFIRINOX cycles, chemoradiotherapy, surgery and postoperative chemotherapy. It improved disease-free survival compared with the comparator strategy of chemoradiotherapy, surgery and postoperative chemotherapy without initial FOLFIRINOX. The result cannot be attributed to induction chemotherapy alone and is not a personal prediction of cure.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Does having more side effects mean treatment is working?

No. Side effects do not reliably show whether the cancer is responding. Report them so they can be treated. Your team assesses benefit using the tests and review plan appropriate to your cancer.

Why might a dose be delayed or reduced?

Your team may allow recovery, investigate a symptom or reduce the chance of lasting harm. Ask which drug changes, why, and when review is planned. A delay does not itself mean treatment has failed.

What should I say when calling?

Give the regimen name, last treatment date, symptoms and when they started. Explain whether you can eat, drink, pass urine and manage usual activities. Give your temperature if available, but do not delay calling to collect details.

What belongs in the consent conversation?

The individual aim and likely benefit, uncertainty, reasonable alternatives including not proceeding, common and serious risks, lasting effects, fertility and practical concerns. You can ask for an interpreter, more time or another explanation and can reconsider your decision.

How many cycles should I use this passport for?

The passport provides reusable recording pages, not a prescribed course length. Your oncology team sets the duration and can provide extra pages.

Will I need a port or a PICC?

The team chooses the most suitable access device with you. A port is under the skin; a PICC enters through the upper arm and has an external section. Both require specific care, and the pump can be used with either.

Does disconnecting the pump remove my port?

No. At the arranged visit the pump is disconnected and the line cared for. A port needle may be removed, but the implanted port stays under the skin until a separate removal procedure is arranged.

What if the pump finishes early or still looks full?

Contact the team for advice. Do not change delivery settings or disconnect or reconnect it yourself. Follow only the specific emergency steps you have been taught.

Is delayed diarrhoea the same as cramps during infusion?

No. Irinotecan can cause an early reaction with cramps, sweating or diarrhoea, and a later diarrhoea problem. Tell the nurse about early symptoms and follow the separate written plan at home.

Does the wallet QR contain my treatment record?

No. The printed QR opens this regimen’s patient information. Your cycle stamps, appointments, test results and treatment changes remain in your own records and the clinical record.

Can I use the printed information without a phone?

Yes. The three-page summary, full handbook and passport can be printed. Ask your team to write the next appointments and working contact numbers on the paper copies.
