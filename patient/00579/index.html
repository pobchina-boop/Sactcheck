How your medicines work

Immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Releases an immune brake
Immune cell→Brake blocked→Cancer cell

PD-1 is a control switch on T cells, a type of immune cell. When it connects with PD-L1 or PD-L2, the T cell receives a signal to reduce its activity. Some cancers use this pathway to escape immune attack. Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, helping T cells recognise and attack the cancer. It does not directly poison cancer cells like chemotherapy. Releasing this brake can also allow immune cells to attack healthy organs, which explains the different side-effect pattern and why symptoms can appear after treatment stops.

The immune system can also inflame healthy organs. New cough, diarrhoea, unusual weakness, severe headache, thirst or a spreading rash need prompt advice. Effects can start during treatment or after it finishes.

Chemotherapy

Paclitaxel

Disrupts cell division
Dividing cell→Microtubules held→Division disrupted

Paclitaxel stabilises tiny structures called microtubules that cells need to divide. This disrupts cancer-cell division. Healthy tissues can be affected too, including blood-forming cells and nerves. That explains why infection risk, hair loss and numbness or tingling matter during treatment.

Report numbness, tingling or difficulty using your hands before the next dose. During the infusion, tell the nurse immediately about itching, flushing, chest tightness, back pain or feeling faint.

Chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Damages cancer-cell DNA
DNA strands→Platinum links→Copying disrupted

Carboplatin is a platinum-containing chemotherapy. Inside cells, it forms chemical attachments to DNA, including links that stop DNA strands working normally. This disrupts copying and repair and can lead to cell death. Healthy cells can also be injured, especially blood-forming cells in the bone marrow. The kidneys help remove carboplatin, so kidney function is used to calculate the dose. AUC 6 describes a target drug exposure over time; it is not a fixed dose of 6 mg.

Low blood counts, nausea and vomiting can occur. An allergic reaction is also possible: tell the nurse immediately if you feel flushed, itchy, tight-chested or unwell during the drip.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Appointments and time in the unit

Pembrolizumab first over 30 minutes, then paclitaxel over 3 hours, then carboplatin over 30 minutes during induction. Premedication and observation add time. Clinic time includes assessment, preparation and waiting; ask your unit about the full visit.

STEP 2

Treatment phase

Induction: all three medicines on Day 1 every 21 days for up to four cycles. Maintenance: paclitaxel and carboplatin stop; pembrolizumab alone may continue every 21 days while appropriate. The team agrees duration and review points.

STEP 3

Medicines and practical help

Check the paclitaxel premedication and anti-sickness plan for induction. Maintenance has different medicines and may be a shorter visit. Plan transport if medicines make you drowsy. Keep the immunotherapy alert card and urgent numbers with you.

STEP 4

After treatment

Response imaging follows the consultant plan; no fixed universal scan interval is specified in these NCCP regimens. Immune toxicity can arise after the final pembrolizumab dose. Continue symptom vigilance, immunotherapy alert card and the team’s follow-up plan. Review persistent neuropathy, hearing effects and cytopenias even after chemotherapy stops; immunotherapy maintenance has a different supportive-care plan.

Support around treatment

Induction support

Anti-sickness medicines help with carboplatin. Paclitaxel premedication helps prevent infusion reactions. Ask for one clear steroid schedule and report infusion symptoms immediately.

Maintenance support

Chemotherapy and taxane premedication stop. Pembrolizumab alone usually does not need the same preventive anti-sickness medicines; ask for the updated prescription. Immune symptom vigilance continues.

Do not wait for the next appointment
