How your medicines work

Immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Blocks an immune brake
Immune cell→PD-1 blocked→Cancer attacked

Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, a control switch on immune T cells. Cancer cells can use this switch to reduce immune attack. Blocking it can help the immune system recognise and attack cancer. The same increased immune activity can inflame healthy organs, which is why new symptoms need attention even after treatment ends.

Report new breathing, bowel, skin, hormone, heart or nerve symptoms promptly; these may be immune inflammation.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Appointments

Pembrolizumab 400 mg is given as a drip over about 30 minutes on Day 1 every 6 weeks (42 days). The hospital visit is longer because of checks and preparation.

STEP 2

Course length

After complete surgery and platinum chemotherapy, the adjuvant course lasts up to 12 months. For metastatic cancer, treatment continues while it remains helpful and tolerable; the team may consider stopping after 24 months of sustained response without progression, or continuing when appropriate.

STEP 3

Checks between visits

Blood tests are due before each infusion. Thyroid tests stay every 3–6 weeks on either dose schedule. Ask about travel, work, transport and whom to contact if a test or visit is delayed.

STEP 4

Home medicines and records

Keep the alert card, symptom record, current medicine list and urgent contact details accessible. Your team may prescribe steroids or hormone replacement only if needed.

STEP 5

After the final dose

Keep reporting new symptoms. Some hormone medicines and monitoring continue after the cancer treatment finishes.

Support around treatment

Sickness medicines

Pembrolizumab alone has a minimal risk of causing sickness. Routine anti-sickness medicines or infusion premedicines are not usually needed; your team can prescribe symptom relief if required.

Immune alert card

Carry your alert card during treatment and afterwards. Show it when seeking urgent care; symptoms can be mistaken for an infection or an ordinary stomach upset.

Hormones and steroids

Some immune effects need steroids or hormone replacement. Follow the prescribed plan and do not stop steroids suddenly. Ask for help if symptoms worsen or you cannot take essential medicines.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Discuss pregnancy, fertility and breastfeeding before treatment. Use effective contraception during treatment and for at least four months after the last dose. If pregnancy is possible or occurs, contact your oncology team promptly.

Do not wait for the next appointment

Emergency: 112 / 999. Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain needs emergency help.
