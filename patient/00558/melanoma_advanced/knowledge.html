When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Immunotherapy can cause inflammation in healthy organs during treatment or after it stops. Contact the oncology team promptly for new diarrhoea, blood in stools, cough or breathlessness, yellow skin or eyes, severe headache, vision changes, marked thirst, unusual weakness or a spreading rash. Tell any clinician that you are receiving or have received immunotherapy.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Medicines in this regimen

Pembrolizumab helps immune cells act against melanoma that cannot be completely removed or has spread. The aim is disease control; benefit and side effects are reviewed for you.

PD-1 checkpoint immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Keytruda
Releases a PD-1 immune brake

Pembrolizumab blocks PD-1, a signal that can reduce T-cell activity. This can help immune cells act against melanoma, but it can also allow inflammation in healthy organs. It is not conventional chemotherapy, and side effects can occur after treatment stops.

PD-1 blocked↓Immune activity increases↓Healthy organs can inflame

What to watch for
Carry your immunotherapy alert card and report new symptoms early, including after treatment ends.

Ongoing treatment and review

Pembrolizumab is given as 400 mg by IV infusion over 30 minutes, on Day 1 every 6 weeks. Allow extra time for assessment, preparation and observation. For sustained response without progression, stopping after 24 months may be considered by the treating team; this is not an automatic stop.

The team confirms treatment dates, recovery and each safety decision. A planned treatment count does not authorise the next dose.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Taking or receiving treatment

Pembrolizumab is given as 400 mg by IV infusion over 30 minutes, on Day 1 every 6 weeks. Allow extra time for assessment, preparation and observation.

STEP 2

Missed, vomited or held treatment

If a visit is missed or treatment is held, contact the unit for a new plan. Do not count a missed appointment as a dose received. An interval change, operation or new phase does not erase earlier immune toxicity or a permanent-stop decision.

STEP 3

Appointments and supply

Arrange dates, medicine supply, transport and any needed interpreter or carer support. Ask whether bloods are taken in the oncology unit or elsewhere and how the result reaches the prescriber.

Paper copies and contact information

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

An individual symptom plan

Ask which anti-sickness or other symptom medicines are prescribed, when to take them and when to call if they do not help. Do not start a borrowed medicine or alter treatment to manage a side effect yourself.

Your urgent contacts

Keep your actual oncology, out-of-hours and pharmacy contacts on the wallet card. Example numbers on a template must be replaced before you use it.

Pregnancy and fertility precautions

Discuss fertility before starting. Use effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 months after the last dose. Tell the team promptly about a possible pregnancy. Discuss breastfeeding restrictions with the team before treatment; do not breastfeed without their advice. Do not stop prescribed steroids or hormone replacement yourself.

Eating, activity and practical help

Report falling intake, weight loss, pain or fatigue early. The team can tailor nutrition, activity, wound, psychological and practical support to your needs.

Steroids and hormone replacement

Some immune effects need steroids or lasting hormone replacement. Follow the exact prescription and taper. Carry the relevant alert information, and do not stop or reduce these medicines yourself when anticancer treatment is held or completed.

Treatment reference · 01

Understanding your diagnosis and treatment goal

Use these questions with your own pathology report and agreed treatment plan.

What the diagnosis says

Keep the pathology summary, original skin site, stage and details of any lymph-node involvement together. Breslow thickness and ulceration describe features of the original melanoma; they are different from the stage. If the diagnosis arose in the eye or a mucosal surface, ask whether this cutaneous-melanoma information applies.

Sources: nccn-patient

BRAF and other tumour tests

A BRAF V600 result helps decide whether the BRAF/MEK targeted medicines as a separate treatment option from the immunotherapy described in this guide are suitable. This is usually a test of the tumour, not proof of an inherited family condition. Keep the actual result and test date; ask about genetic counselling only when your personal or family history makes that relevant.

Sources: nccn-patient

Surgery, radiotherapy and medicine have different roles

Record what surgery removed and whether the present medicine is postoperative treatment to lower recurrence risk or treatment of cancer that remains or has spread. Radiotherapy, stereotactic treatment or another operation may have a separate role; their timing and side effects need their own plan.

Sources: nccn-patient

Stage and response are different

Stage describes the extent of melanoma at diagnosis or a later reassessment. A response assessment asks how known cancer has changed with treatment. In the adjuvant setting there may be no visible tumour to measure: surveillance looks for recurrence while safety checks look for medicine effects.

Sources: nccn-patient

A new skin change still needs review

Continue to report a new or changing mole, a non-healing lesion, a new lump or an unexplained persistent symptom. Do not assume every new lesion is melanoma, and do not use a photograph or a good response at one site to rule out a problem elsewhere.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 04

Tests: what is required and what each result means

Safety tests, tumour tests and response scans answer different questions.

Before treatment

Blood count, kidney and liver tests, glucose and thyroid tests; review autoimmune conditions, transplants, previous immune reactions and all current medicines.

Sources: nccp

During treatment

Blood count, kidney/liver tests and glucose before every 6-week cycle. TSH is checked every 3–6 weeks, including between treatment visits when needed. Cortisol and other tests are added when clinically indicated. Report new symptoms at every visit and between visits; normal blood results do not rule out immune toxicity.

Sources: nccp

Scans and tumour markers

Ask the team to write the next assessment and who will arrange it. Bloods used to monitor kidneys, liver, hormones or muscle are not automatically measures of cancer response. There is no universal tumour-marker blood schedule for these skin-cancer regimens; a scan or extra blood test is requested for a specific clinical reason.

Sources: nccp

After a pause or finish

Continue to report possible immune effects after the last dose; your team records the follow-up tests and dates. Keep any prescribed steroid taper, hormone replacement and specialist follow-up. Scans follow your individual disease plan.

Sources: nccp

Getting the result

For each test, record its purpose, due date, result and action. Agree how results reach you and who checks a result from another hospital. If an appointment or result has not arrived, contact the named service rather than assuming no news means no action is needed.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 05

What to expect between visits

You can feel well and still need the planned checks. New symptoms deserve attention even on a day without an appointment.

Your usual pattern

Record the start and change in symptoms, temperature if advised, bowel pattern, drinking, urine and the effect on normal tasks. A simple few-line record is enough. Use it to describe a change rather than trying to grade or diagnose a toxicity yourself.

Sources: nccp

When to call

Immunotherapy can cause inflammation in healthy organs during treatment or after it stops. Contact the oncology team promptly for new diarrhoea, blood in stools, cough or breathlessness, yellow skin or eyes, severe headache, vision changes, marked thirst, unusual weakness or a spreading rash. Tell any clinician that you are receiving or have received immunotherapy.

Sources: nccp

Eating, drinking and activity

Tell the team early about nausea, taste change, a sore mouth, diarrhoea or falling weight. Ask for an individual fluid and food plan if heart, kidney or other conditions limit intake. Gentle activity and planned rest can help maintain function; adjust to symptoms and ask for support when fatigue limits daily life.

Sources: nccn-patient

A held dose or changed plan

If a visit is missed or treatment is held, contact the unit for a new plan. Do not count a missed appointment as a dose received. An interval change, operation or new phase does not erase earlier immune toxicity or a permanent-stop decision.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 06

Skin checks, sun protection and optional photographs

This complements clinical assessment and does not replace it.

Protect your skin

Use shade, protective clothing, a hat and broad-spectrum sunscreen of at least SPF 30, and avoid tanning beds. Reapply sunscreen according to its instructions. Protection matters even when it is cloudy; choose a practical approach that lets you remain active safely.

Sources: nccn-patient

Know the pattern of your skin

Ask how to examine your skin, scars and relevant lymph-node areas and how often your own team recommends doing so. A mirror or a trusted helper can make hard-to-see areas easier to check. Report a new or changing lesion, a non-healing sore or a new lump rather than waiting for a routine visit.

Sources: nccn-patient

Photographs are optional

If useful, keep a dated photograph with consistent lighting and a size reference, following your team’s advice. Photographs are optional and do not diagnose a cancer or replace examination. Keep them privately; this printable passport does not require you to upload images or other personal health information.

Sources: nccn-patient

Wounds, nails and sensitive skin

Use the dressings and skin-care plan recommended for your lesion and treatment. Ask before applying new creams or remedies to an open or treated area. Worsening pain, discharge, redness, bleeding or loss of function needs advice; routine skin-care instructions are not a substitute for assessment.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 07

Pregnancy, fertility and protecting others

Discuss these precautions before treatment even if pregnancy is not currently planned.

Medicine-specific precautions

Discuss fertility before starting. Use effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 months after the last dose. Tell the team promptly about a possible pregnancy. Discuss breastfeeding restrictions with the team before treatment; do not breastfeed without their advice. Do not stop prescribed steroids or hormone replacement yourself.

Sources: nccp

Planning ahead

Ask early about fertility preservation, sexual health and symptoms affecting intimacy. Tell the team about any chance of pregnancy, breastfeeding or a partner’s pregnancy so that the correct medicine-specific advice can be given. Keep the precautions and their end dates in the written plan.

Sources: nccp

Safe handling and supply

Do not share anticancer medicines. Keep oral medicines in their original labelled packaging, away from children and anyone who could take them accidentally. Return unused medicines to the supplying pharmacy and follow its handling advice. A treatment hold does not remove pregnancy precautions.

Sources: nccp

Treatment reference · 08

Questions, decisions and practical support

Your priorities and the burden of treatment belong in the discussion.

Benefits and uncertainties

Ask what evidence applies to your diagnosis, stage, biomarker and treatment setting, what outcome the team hopes to improve, and what may happen without this treatment. A trial average cannot predict an individual result; a benefit measured in one setting should not be transferred to another.

Sources: nccn-patient

Alternatives and review points

Discuss other suitable treatments, clinical trials if relevant, local treatment options and symptom-focused care. Ask when the decision will be reviewed and what would prompt a change. Supportive and palliative care can help with symptoms and quality of life alongside anticancer treatment.

Sources: nccn-patient

Help with daily life

Tell the team about work, transport, caring responsibilities, cost concerns, body image, anxiety or sleep. Ask which nurse, social worker, dietitian, rehabilitation professional or support organisation can help. Use resources relevant to your country and service; a US guide may describe different access or funding.

Sources: nccn-patient

Shared understanding

Ask someone on the team to check that you can explain the medicine schedule, next tests and urgent-contact route in your own words. An interpreter, large-print copy or a trusted supporter can help; none requires you to use a smartphone.

What the evidence means for your treatment

The evidence discussion must match the diagnosis, biomarker and treatment setting shown in this guide.

Evidence in advanced melanoma measures control of existing cancer; adjuvant evidence measures recurrence after surgery. Perioperative evidence concerns treatment around surgery. Ask which study and outcome match your stage and plan. These results cannot predict your individual benefit.
