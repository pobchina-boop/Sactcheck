When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Contact the oncology team promptly for new or worsening cough, breathlessness or chest pain; diarrhoea, blood in stools or significant abdominal pain; yellow skin/eyes or dark urine; reduced urine; a spreading or blistering rash; unusual weakness, severe headache, dizziness, marked thirst or passing much more urine. Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, confusion, seizures, rapidly worsening weakness or difficulty swallowing need emergency assessment. Do not wait for the next infusion.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Your immunotherapy medicine

Durvalumab is given after definitive chemoradiotherapy to reduce the chance of lung cancer returning or worsening and improve the chance of longer survival. This is part of a treatment pathway that aims for long-term control and possible cure; it cannot guarantee cure.

PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor

Durvalumab

Intravenous infusion every 14 days
Blocks PD-L1 to support immune recognition of cancer

Durvalumab blocks PD-L1, a protein that can reduce the immune system’s ability to attack cancer cells. Removing this signal can help immune cells act against cancer, but the immune system can also inflame healthy organs.

PD-L1 can dampen an immune response↓Durvalumab blocks the PD-L1 signal↓Immune cells can act against cancer cells

What to watch for
Report new symptoms early and carry the immunotherapy alert card. Do not wait for the next infusion.

The bigger treatment plan

Chemoradiotherapy first, then durvalumab

Consolidation

Durvalumab every 14 days after completed concurrent CRT

After completed concurrent CRTEvery 14 daysUp to 12 months (26 cycles)

Durvalumab is infused on Day 1 of each 14-day cycle. The course lasts up to 12 months (26 cycles), and can finish earlier if the cancer progresses or side effects make continuation unsuitable. The first dose should start within 42 days after chemoradiotherapy finishes.

The earlier chemotherapy and radiation course has its own prescribing and support plan. Durvalumab is the consolidation phase covered here.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Infusion visit

Durvalumab 10 mg/kg reference dose by IV infusion over about 60 minutes after clinical review and planned tests. Report new symptoms and any treatment changes before the infusion.

Day 2–14: Between infusion visits

No durvalumab tablets are taken at home. Follow the agreed blood-test, supportive-medicine and symptom plan. Report new or worsening symptoms promptly; do not wait for the next cycle.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Two different parts of treatment

Platinum chemotherapy and radiation are given together first. Durvalumab follows after that course has finished and the team has confirmed that the cancer has not progressed.

STEP 2

Infusion visits

The usual reference dose is 10 mg/kg, given into a vein over about 60 minutes every 14 days. Allow extra time for the clinical review, tests and preparation.

STEP 3

Bloods, scans and transport

Confirm where blood tests are taken, who reviews them and the planned clinic/scan appointments. Allow extra time at the treatment unit and arrange help with travel if fatigue or symptoms make it difficult.

STEP 4

A pause or early finish

Treatment can be paused to investigate symptoms or treat an immune reaction. A missed dose does not automatically extend the course beyond the planned maximum. Restart decisions belong to the consultant team.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Immune side effects and the alert card

Immune side effects may start during treatment or after the last dose. Keep the immunotherapy alert card and tell every treating clinician that you have received durvalumab, even during a treatment break or after finishing.

Lung symptoms after radiotherapy

Radiation inflammation, immune inflammation, infection and cancer can cause similar symptoms. Report new or worsening breathing symptoms promptly so the team can assess the cause. Do not assume a new cough is only an old radiotherapy effect.

Support medicines

Durvalumab alone has minimal sickness risk and no routine premedication is specified. Use anti-sickness or other medicines only as prescribed. Steroids for an immune reaction are a treatment plan supervised by the team, not routine premedication; do not start, reduce or stop them yourself.

Hormone replacement and blood tests

Thyroid, adrenal or diabetes problems may need hormone replacement or insulin, sometimes long term. Keep taking prescribed medicines and attend blood tests even when you feel well. Sudden severe weakness, vomiting, faintness, unusual thirst or drowsiness need urgent advice.

Pregnancy, fertility and breastfeeding

Discuss pregnancy plans, fertility and breastfeeding before treatment. Effective contraception is advised during durvalumab and for at least three months after the last dose. Durvalumab is not recommended during pregnancy. The team must help decide whether breastfeeding or durvalumab should be stopped or avoided, weighing the benefits and risks.

Treatment reference · 01

Treatment and its aim

Durvalumab is given after definitive chemoradiotherapy to reduce the chance of lung cancer returning or worsening and improve the chance of longer survival. This is part of a treatment pathway that aims for long-term control and possible cure; it cannot guarantee cure.

Who this guide covers

Consolidation for unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer after concurrent platinum chemotherapy and radiotherapy, with no cancer progression and tumour PD-L1 of at least 1%.

Sources: nccp

Sequence and duration

Durvalumab is infused on Day 1 of each 14-day cycle. The course lasts up to 12 months (26 cycles), and can finish earlier if the cancer progresses or side effects make continuation unsuitable. The first dose should start within 42 days after chemoradiotherapy finishes.

Sources: nccp

Infusion and personal decision

The usual reference dose is 10 mg/kg, given into a vein over about 60 minutes every 14 days. Allow extra time for the clinical review, tests and preparation. PACIFIC directly studied durvalumab every two weeks after concurrent chemoradiotherapy. This guide uses that interval.

Sources: nccp · pacific

Treatment reference · 04

Scans and follow-up

Follow-up continues after treatment finishes.

Agree your scan plan

Published expert guidance suggests chest/abdomen CT with contrast if suitable every 3 months in year 1 after chemoradiotherapy, at least every 6 months in year 2 and at least yearly in years 3–5. Your team agrees your actual plan; this is supplemental guidance, not a fixed NCCP requirement. New symptoms may need earlier assessment. Routine brain scans are not recommended without symptoms after baseline staging.

Sources: imaging · nccp

Why this treatment may be chosen

Durvalumab is given after definitive chemoradiotherapy to reduce the chance of lung cancer returning or worsening and improve the chance of longer survival. This is part of a treatment pathway that aims for long-term control and possible cure; it cannot guarantee cure.

In PACIFIC, durvalumab reduced cancer worsening and improved overall survival compared with placebo after chemoradiotherapy. The results describe groups of trial participants and do not predict what will happen to one person.

Sources: nccp · pacific

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Is this more chemotherapy?

Durvalumab is an immunotherapy infusion. It follows the completed chemotherapy and radiation course and works by changing immune signalling.

How long will I receive it?

Durvalumab is infused on Day 1 of each 14-day cycle. The course lasts up to 12 months (26 cycles), and can finish earlier if the cancer progresses or side effects make continuation unsuitable. The first dose should start within 42 days after chemoradiotherapy finishes.

Why report a cough after chest radiotherapy?

Radiation effects, immune inflammation, infection and cancer can look alike. The team needs to assess new or worsening symptoms rather than assume their cause.

Will I need routine steroids before each infusion?

No routine premedication is specified for this regimen. Steroids may be prescribed to treat particular immune side effects, with a supervised dose and taper.

Can side effects happen after I stop?

Immune side effects may start during treatment or after the last dose. Keep the immunotherapy alert card and tell every treating clinician that you have received durvalumab, even during a treatment break or after finishing.
