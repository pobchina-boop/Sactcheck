When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Use your oncology unit’s written emergency plan. Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice even on days without chemotherapy. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. If you have an established seizure rescue plan, follow it and its emergency instructions. Contact the team promptly for a worsening headache, repeated vomiting, new cough or breathlessness, unusual bruising, yellow skin/eyes or symptoms that prevent drinking. Do not restart held chemotherapy without instructions.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

Understand your treatment

How your treatment works

This eight-week PCV regimen combines procarbazine, lomustine and vincristine after radiotherapy for grade II glioma. It aims to improve disease control. NCCP describes this indication as unlicensed; the treating team explains the evidence, alternatives and local consent process.

Oral chemotherapy capsule

Lomustine

CCNU
Damages DNA

Lomustine damages genetic material so cells cannot divide normally. It can affect cancer cells and healthy bone-marrow cells. Blood counts may fall several weeks after the single dose, so a day without capsules is not a day without risk.

One scheduled dose↓DNA damaged↓Delayed blood-count effects possible

What to watch for
Take only the prescribed Day 1 dose. Do not take it daily or repeat it without oncology instructions. Report fever, bleeding or new breathing symptoms.

Sources: nccp

Oral chemotherapy capsule

Procarbazine

Procarbazine capsules
Interferes with DNA and cell growth

Procarbazine is converted in the body into substances that interfere with DNA and cell growth. Healthy blood-forming and digestive tissues can also be affected. It has important interactions with some medicines, alcohol and certain foods, so a pharmacy review and written instructions are essential.

Capsules on specified days↓DNA processes disrupted↓Tumour and healthy cells affected

What to watch for
Follow the exact Days 8–21 calendar and the pharmacist’s medicine, food and alcohol instructions. Report fever, rash or persistent vomiting.

Sources: nccp

Chemotherapy given into a vein

Vincristine

Vincristine infusion
Disrupts the machinery used for cell division

Vincristine interferes with structures that cells use when dividing. It can also affect nerves controlling sensation, strength and bowel movement. This explains why tingling, weakness and constipation need early review even if blood tests are satisfactory.

Infusion into a vein↓Cell division disrupted↓Nerves and bowel may be affected

What to watch for
Report tingling, weakness, walking difficulty or constipation before the next dose. Tell staff immediately if the infusion site stings, burns or swells.

Complete timing and symptom notes

Day 1–7: One lomustine dose on Day 1

Take only the labelled Day 1 dose. No procarbazine is scheduled until Day 8. Check the capsule supply and anti-sickness plan; do not repeat lomustine.

Day 8–21: Daily procarbazine and first vincristine

Take procarbazine each day from Day 8 through Day 21. Attend the Day 8 vincristine infusion and required tests. Follow the bowel, alcohol, food and medicine-interaction plan.

Day 22–29: Procarbazine finished; Day 29 infusion

There are no planned procarbazine doses after Day 21. Vincristine is scheduled again on Day 29 after assessment. Report numbness, weakness or constipation before the infusion.

Day 30–56: Recovery before the next cycle

No scheduled PCV medicines in this period. Blood-count effects can still develop or persist. Attend tests and restart only after team review.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Taking the treatment

Lomustine is taken once on Day 1 only; procarbazine is taken daily on Days 8–21; vincristine is given into a vein on Days 8 and 29. Follow the pharmacy label for capsule number, food and timing. Do not open or crush capsules. Lomustine is not a daily medicine. Vincristine must only be given intravenously by trained staff.

STEP 2

Missed or vomited medicine

If a capsule dose is missed or vomited, contact the oncology pharmacy for advice. Do not take extra capsules, repeat the single lomustine dose, or extend procarbazine days without instructions.

STEP 3

Medicine and supplement review

Bring an up-to-date list of all medicines, including steroids, seizure medicines, anticoagulants, non-prescription treatments, vitamins and herbs. Ask before starting something new. Keep a record of all dose changes.

STEP 4

Procarbazine interactions

Avoid alcohol during treatment. Ask for a written food list and medicine interaction check, including the period after procarbazine when precautions still apply. Do not assume an over-the-counter cold remedy, antidepressant, pain medicine or herbal product is safe without pharmacy review.

STEP 5

Storage and handling

Keep capsules in their original labelled packs, away from children. Do not open, crush or chew. If swallowing or medicine organisation is difficult, contact the team before the next dose and agree safe help. Return unused chemotherapy to the pharmacy.

STEP 6

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and fertility

Treatment can harm a pregnancy and may affect fertility. Discuss fertility preservation, contraception for patients and partners, and the required period after the final dose before starting. Do not breastfeed during treatment. Ask the team for written, medicine-specific advice.

STEP 7

Appointments and transport

Before starting: full blood count, kidney and liver tests, glucose and baseline lung-function assessment. NCCP requires blood count, kidney and liver tests before each treatment. Low counts can be delayed and cumulative after lomustine, so keep any additional blood tests the team arranges. Repeat lung-function assessment during prolonged therapy or when clinically indicated. MRI timing follows the individual glioma plan. Current lomustine product information additionally advises weekly blood counts for at least six weeks after each dose; the team should arrange these alongside the NCCP pre-treatment checks.

STEP 8

Help with the daily plan

Ask a trusted person to help check the calendar if memory, vision, fatigue or hand strength makes treatment difficult. Confirm who is responsible for checking doses, collecting medicine, transport and calling for help. Keep consent and personal preferences central.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Nausea prevention on the correct days

Lomustine and procarbazine have moderate-to-high oral emetic potential. Vincristine alone has minimal emetic risk. The team supplies a day-specific anti-sickness plan, including the procarbazine days, and a rescue plan.

Prevent constipation

Vincristine can slow the bowel through its effect on nerves. Preventive laxatives are recommended in NCCP; follow the prescribed plan and report constipation early. Severe abdominal pain, vomiting or inability to pass stool or wind needs urgent advice.

Food, alcohol and medicine review

Procarbazine has important interactions. Avoid alcohol during treatment and follow the pharmacist’s written food restrictions and advice about when restrictions end. Check all prescription, over-the-counter, herbal and supplement products before taking them.

Support for neurological symptoms

Keep any prescribed seizure medicine and steroid plan separate from the PCV calendar. Report new weakness, tingling, gait change or constipation before vincristine. A medicine review is needed before adding any treatment for symptoms.

Treatment reference · 04

Understanding your particular glioma

Your diagnosis includes the tumour’s cells, molecular findings and behaviour, not just the word brain cancer.

The name and location

Gliomas begin in supporting cells of the brain or spinal cord. Astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma and glioblastoma are different diagnoses. A tumour that spread to the brain from another organ is a brain metastasis and is not covered by these glioma regimens. Record the exact integrated diagnosis and where it is located.

Sources: nccn-patient

Grade is not the same as stage

Grade describes features linked to how a tumour behaves. Adult gliomas are usually discussed by their type, grade and molecular findings rather than the stage system used for many other cancers. Grade 4 does not mean cancer has spread through the body. Ask your specialist to explain your own report and any older terminology in the treatment protocol.

Sources: nccn-patient · nci-cns

IDH and 1p/19q

IDH1 or IDH2 changes and loss of parts of chromosomes 1p and 19q help classify particular gliomas. The combination of findings matters: one result should not be read in isolation. These tests are usually performed on tumour tissue. They do not automatically mean a change has been inherited or that relatives need testing.

Sources: nccn-patient

MGMT promoter methylation

MGMT is involved in repairing certain DNA damage. Methylation of its promoter can reduce that repair activity and helps the team discuss the likely benefit of temozolomide in relevant settings. It is not a blood tumour marker, a percentage of tumour left, or an individual survival prediction. Record the reported result and ask how it affects your treatment choice.

Sources: nccn-patient

A useful diagnosis record

Keep the biopsy or operation date, pathology wording, grade, IDH result, 1p/19q result and MGMT result if tested. Mark a result as pending or not applicable rather than assuming it is negative. Ask whether specialist pathology review or further testing would change the plan.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 05

Tests and scans: what each one tells you

Safety blood tests, tumour tissue tests and MRI answer different questions.

Safety blood tests

A full blood count checks infection-fighting white cells, oxygen-carrying red cells and platelets that help stop bleeding. Kidney and liver tests help assess treatment safety. Attend the regimen’s required tests even when you feel well; a reassuring scan does not replace them. The schedule in this guide separates checks before, during and after treatment.

Sources: nccp · nccn-patient

MRI and neurological review

MRI is the main scan used to assess many gliomas. Your team also considers symptoms, strength, movement, speech, memory, daily function and medicines such as steroids. Tell staff before MRI about implants or metal, kidney problems, previous contrast reactions or difficulty lying still. Ask about support if scans make you anxious.

Sources: nccn-patient

Changes on a scan need interpretation

After treatment, inflammation and other treatment effects can resemble tumour growth on MRI. This is sometimes called pseudoprogression. The team may need comparison scans, specialist imaging or further assessment before deciding what a change means. New or worsening symptoms still need prompt review; do not assume they are harmless treatment effects.

Sources: nccn-patient

An individual follow-up timetable

Write the next MRI date, what question it should answer and who will explain the result. Timing depends on your diagnosis, treatment phase and previous findings. General follow-up intervals in an international booklet are not automatically your schedule. These regimens do not require a generic CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or a blood tumour marker before every cycle.

Sources: nccn-patient

Keep results with decisions

Use the passport to distinguish a test being booked, completed and reviewed. Record the agreed next step and whom to call if an appointment or result is missing. Do not alter chemotherapy or steroids yourself in response to a number or scan report.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 06

Brain symptoms, medicines and when to call

Symptoms may reflect the tumour, swelling, treatment, another medicine or a separate illness.

Know your usual symptoms

Write down your usual headache, strength, walking, speech, vision, memory, mood, sleep and seizure pattern before treatment. Note new changes, when they started and how they affect daily tasks. With your permission, a trusted person can help spot changes or record what happened.

Sources: nccn-patient

Urgent neurological changes

Use your oncology unit’s written emergency plan. Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice even on days without chemotherapy. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. If you have an established seizure rescue plan, follow it and its emergency instructions.

Sources: nccp · nccn-patient

Steroids need a separate written plan

Steroids may be prescribed to reduce swelling around the tumour. They are not automatically needed for everyone. Ask for the exact dose, time, review date and any taper plan. Do not stop or change them suddenly without advice. Report sleep or mood changes, increased thirst, infection symptoms or muscle weakness and ask which additional checks or protection you need.

Sources: nccn-patient · nhs-dexamethasone

Seizure medicines and rescue instructions

If prescribed, take seizure medicines on the agreed schedule. Ask what to do after a missed or vomited dose and whom to contact for breakthrough symptoms. Some medicines can affect mood, alertness or interact with treatment. Do not start, stop or switch them yourself. A rescue medicine needs an individual plan and training for whoever may give it.

Sources: nccn-patient

Safety at home and outside

Ask your clinical team about driving, work, bathing, swimming, heights and machinery in light of your symptoms and any seizures. Local medical and licensing rules need individual advice; this guide does not give a universal driving clearance date. Occupational therapy can help adapt daily routines and the home.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 07

Your overall treatment plan and choices

Treatment should account for the tumour and the life you want to lead.

Understand the sequence

Surgery or biopsy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and follow-up have different jobs. Ask your team to draw the intended sequence and identify where you are now. Concurrent treatment means treatments overlap; adjuvant treatment follows the main treatment. A change of phase may change the dose, blood tests and support medicines.

Sources: nccn-patient

Agree what treatment is trying to achieve

Ask whether the aim is to control the tumour, delay growth, relieve symptoms, preserve function or a combination of these. Discuss likely benefits, burdens, alternatives and what would prompt a pause or stop. The answer depends on the exact diagnosis, previous treatment and your priorities.

Sources: nccn-patient

Put evidence in context

Clinical trials describe groups of people and may use older pathology names or different schedules. They cannot predict one person’s response or lifespan. Ask how closely the evidence matches your tumour and treatment. No benefit number from one schedule should automatically be applied to another.

Sources: nccn-patient

Discuss options available to you

Ask about appropriate clinical trials, specialist opinions, symptom support and other treatment options. A medicine or device described in an international guide is not automatically licensed, funded or suitable in Ireland. The treating multidisciplinary team explains what is available and relevant.

Sources: nccn-patient

Make room for your priorities

Bring up fatigue, thinking, independence, family time, work, travel and side effects you most want to avoid. Ask for information at a pace and in a format that suits you. You may ask for a trusted person to join the discussion, written notes and time to reconsider decisions.

Sources: nccn-patient

Treatment reference · 08

Living well, rehabilitation and support

Practical and symptom support can begin alongside anticancer treatment.

Thinking, communication and daily tasks

Tell the team about word-finding, attention, memory, reading, vision or planning difficulties. Rehabilitation may include physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, or neuropsychological support. Written routines, rest breaks and help with medicine organisation can make day-to-day care more manageable.

Sources: nccn-patient

Food, nausea, bowels and energy

Report poor appetite, weight change, vomiting or constipation early. Ask for dietitian help if eating is difficult. Use the prescribed anti-sickness and bowel plan; do not wait until symptoms are severe. Choose activity that is safe for your balance and strength and balance it with rest. Ask about treatable causes of fatigue.

Sources: nccp · nccn-patient

Emotional support and carers

Anxiety, low mood, changes in behaviour and uncertainty can affect both patients and carers. Tell the team what has changed and ask for appropriate psychological or social support. Carers may need their own information, practical help and breaks. Agree who the team may share information with.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

What is my exact diagnosis, grade and relevant molecular result?

Ask for the integrated pathology wording and an explanation of IDH, 1p/19q and MGMT where relevant. The diagnosis and treatment history guide how this regimen applies to you.

Which phase and capsule calendar am I on now?

Lomustine once on Day 1; procarbazine daily on Days 8–21; vincristine into a vein on Days 8 and 29. The cycle lasts 56 days. Six cycles are planned, with review before treatment.

Can you check the Day 1 lomustine and Days 8–21 procarbazine instructions?

Ask the pharmacist to mark each medicine on the calendar. Lomustine is a single Day 1 dose; procarbazine is a different medicine taken over fourteen days. Confirm food, alcohol, interaction and bowel instructions.

Which tests must happen before I can take the next dose?

Before starting: full blood count, kidney and liver tests, glucose and baseline lung-function assessment. NCCP requires blood count, kidney and liver tests before each treatment. Low counts can be delayed and cumulative after lomustine, so keep any additional blood tests the team arranges. Repeat lung-function assessment during prolonged therapy or when clinically indicated. MRI timing follows the individual glioma plan. Current lomustine product information additionally advises weekly blood counts for at least six weeks after each dose; the team should arrange these alongside the NCCP pre-treatment checks.

Who helps with seizures, steroids, memory or daily tasks?

Ask for a named contact, written seizure and steroid plans if prescribed, and rehabilitation or practical support. Agree who can help you organise treatment.

Which symptoms mean contact the team today?

Use your oncology unit’s written emergency plan. Fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell needs urgent advice even on days without chemotherapy. Call 112 or 999 for a new seizure, sudden weakness or speech difficulty, loss of consciousness, severe breathing difficulty or uncontrolled bleeding. If you have an established seizure rescue plan, follow it and its emergency instructions. Contact the team promptly for a worsening headache, repeated vomiting, new cough or breathlessness, unusual bruising, yellow skin/eyes or symptoms that prevent drinking. Do not restart held chemotherapy without instructions.

When will we review the benefit and my priorities?

Ask for the next clinical and MRI review, how treatment effects will be distinguished from growth, and what would lead to continuing, changing or stopping treatment.

Try explaining the plan back: Can you show the days for each medicine, the current phase, your next blood tests and whom to call for fever, vomiting or new neurological symptoms?
