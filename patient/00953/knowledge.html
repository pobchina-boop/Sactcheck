Your complete treatment reference

Durvalumab, paclitaxel and carboplatin

Your treatment and how the medicines work

The aim is to control the cancer, ease or prevent symptoms and delay worsening. Your team can explain the benefit they expect for you and your other options.

Durvalumab · Imfinzi

PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitor. Durvalumab blocks PD-L1, a signal that can slow an immune attack. This can help immune cells recognise cancer, but sometimes causes inflammation in healthy organs. New cough, breathlessness, diarrhoea or severe weakness needs urgent advice.

Paclitaxel · Generic paclitaxel; brand varies

Taxane chemotherapy. Paclitaxel prevents cells from completing division. It can also injure growing hair and blood cells and affect nerves in hands and feet. Tell the nurse immediately about flushing, wheezing or dizziness during infusion; mention new numbness before the next dose.

Carboplatin · Generic carboplatin; brand varies

Platinum chemotherapy. Carboplatin links strands of DNA inside cells, making it difficult for dividing cancer cells to copy their DNA. Blood-forming cells can also be affected. Call oncology urgently for fever, chills, unexplained bruising or bleeding.

Your infusion schedule

Three-drug combination every 21 days

Day 1: durvalumab, then paclitaxel, then carboplatin. Repeat every 21 days for 4–6 cycles unless the team pauses or stops earlier.

Durvalumab alone every 28 days

The first durvalumab-only dose is 3 weeks after the final combination infusion. It then repeats every 4 weeks while helping and tolerated.

Common effects and what to do

Tiredness

Plan rest around visits and report fatigue that is new, severe or associated with dizziness.

Nausea and appetite changes

During combination therapy take the anti-sickness medicines on your plan and report difficulty drinking.

Hair loss, aches and tingling

Paclitaxel can cause hair loss, muscle aches and numb or painful fingers or toes. Report nerve symptoms early.

Serious effects: when to call

Pneumonitis · lung inflammation

New cough or breathlessness needs an immediate oncology call. Severe breathing difficulty needs 112 or 999.

Infection or low blood counts

Fever, chills or sudden illness can be serious even with a normal temperature. Call oncology immediately.

Colitis · bowel inflammation

New or worsening diarrhoea, belly pain or blood in stools needs urgent oncology advice.

Hormone problems

Severe weakness, dizziness, vomiting or extreme thirst can signal changes in hormone or blood-sugar levels. Call oncology immediately.

Infusion allergy

Tell the nurse immediately if you feel flushed, wheezy, dizzy or unwell during a drip.

Contact your oncology service urgently. Call the oncology team immediately for a fever or feeling suddenly unwell, new cough or breathlessness, diarrhoea or bloody stool, blistering rash, marked weakness or dizziness. Do this even after treatment ends. Call 112 or 999 for severe breathing trouble, severe chest pain, collapse or confusion. If you have severe breathing difficulty, severe chest pain, collapse or a seizure, call 112 or 999. Follow the 24-hour contact instructions from your treating hospital.

Practical support

Preventing sickness

Combination treatment has high sickness risk, mainly from carboplatin. Take your prescribed prevention and rescue medicines as directed. Durvalumab alone has minimal sickness risk, so the plan may change.

Before paclitaxel

The team gives medicines to lower the chance of an infusion reaction, including a steroid and antihistamine according to its plan. Tell the nurse immediately about wheeze, flushing or faintness.

Nerve or muscle symptoms

Contact the team early about numb or painful hands or feet, difficulty with buttons or walking, or troublesome muscle or joint aches.

Immune alert card

Carry your immunotherapy alert card during and after treatment. Show it to any urgent-care clinician. Do not start, stop or change steroids yourself.

Fertility and pregnancy

Discuss pregnancy, contraception and fertility before treatment. Durvalumab requires effective contraception during treatment and for at least three months after the last dose; ask your team for the longest interval required by any medicine you receive. Do not breastfeed during treatment.

Planning your visits

On a combination day

All medicines are given into a vein. On combination days durvalumab is given first over about 1 hour, then paclitaxel over about 3 hours and carboplatin over about 30 minutes. Allow extra time for review, premedicines, blood results and preparation. Durvalumab alone takes about an hour.

Before your visit

Bring your medicines list, alert card and 24-hour oncology contact. Tell the team about prior allergic reactions, hearing issues, diabetes, autoimmune disease, steroids and nerve symptoms.

Leaving the unit

Write down the date of the next blood test, the next infusion and the 24-hour phone number. Confirm whether the next visit is for three drugs or one.

What the research found

The dMMR subgroup receiving durvalumab had a lower rate of progression or death over follow-up (hazard ratio 0.42, 95% confidence interval 0.22–0.80). This compares groups over time and does not predict how long treatment will help one person.

This was a prespecified exploratory subgroup. A separate study arm also used olaparib; olaparib is not part of this NCCP regimen.
