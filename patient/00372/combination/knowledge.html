When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call your oncology team immediately for fever, shaking chills, sudden illness, breathing difficulty, chest pain, significant bleeding, severe tummy pain, uncontrolled vomiting or new severe headache, confusion, seizure or vision change. Do not wait for the next appointment.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Three medicines. Different jobs.

To help control adenocarcinoma lung cancer after first-line chemotherapy; treatment benefit and side effects are reviewed together.

Taxane chemotherapy

Docetaxel

Docetaxel
Stabilises microtubules

Docetaxel prevents normal rearrangement of the cell structures needed for division. It affects cancer cells and can also suppress blood cells, irritate nerves and cause fluid retention.

Stabilises microtubules↓Cancer growth disrupted↓Healthy tissues may also be affected

What to watch for
Fever or feeling suddenly unwell needs urgent advice. Tell the nurse immediately about infusion symptoms or pain.

Sources: nccp

Oral angiokinase inhibitor

Nintedanib

Nintedanib
Blocks VEGF, FGF and PDGF receptor signalling

Nintedanib blocks several signals involved in the growth of blood vessels that support a tumour. Its effects can also cause diarrhoea, liver changes, bleeding and wound-healing problems.

Blocks VEGF, FGF and PDGF receptor signalling↓Cancer growth disrupted↓Healthy tissues may also be affected

What to watch for
Call early for diarrhoea or poor fluid intake; report bleeding, yellow eyes, dark urine, severe tummy pain or new neurological symptoms urgently.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Docetaxel day

Docetaxel infusion after checks. Do not take nintedanib today. Follow the dexamethasone prescription. Use the current prescription and contact plan.

Day 2–21: Nintedanib at home

Take the prescribed capsule dose twice daily about 12 hours apart. Continue the agreed monitoring and support plan. Use the current prescription and contact plan.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Dexamethasone before docetaxel

Take the prescribed steroid course from the day before docetaxel. If missed, contact the unit before attending; do not invent a catch-up dose.

STEP 2

Infection prevention

The team should consider preventive granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (white-cell growth support) (G-CSF) injections and arrange blood counts around the low-count period. Fever needs immediate advice.

STEP 3

Capsules

Take nintedanib about 12 hours apart with food; swallow whole. Do not open, chew or crush. If missed, take the next scheduled dose only; never double up.

STEP 4

Diarrhoea plan

Have the prescribed anti-diarrhoeal and fluid plan ready. Contact oncology early for diarrhoea, poor intake or vomiting; do not wait 7 days to seek help.

STEP 5

Medicine review

Check all medicines and supplements with oncology/pharmacy. Nintedanib has P-gp interactions; docetaxel has CYP 3 A interactions, including grapefruit concerns.

STEP 6

Pregnancy prevention

Discuss fertility and effective contraception. Nintedanib requires contraception during treatment and for at least 3 months after; follow the longest advice for your complete regimen. Vomiting/diarrhoea can reduce pill absorption.

Understanding the evidence

The trial found a progression-free benefit overall and an overall-survival benefit in the prespecified adenocarcinoma subgroup.

The licensed/NCCP population is adenocarcinoma after first-line chemotherapy. Overall-population PFS and adenocarcinoma-subgroup OS are labelled separately; this does not establish an independent benefit from maintenance-only initiation.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Who confirms my next dose?

Your treating team checks the prescription, tests and symptoms. A completed-cycle stamp records past treatment; it does not authorise the next dose.

What if the plan changes?

Ask the team to update your phase, medicines, test appointments and cycle record. Use the updated prescription.

When are my scans?

Your team sets a response and follow-up plan for your cancer and symptoms. This document does not invent a fixed scan or tumour-marker timetable.

Try explaining the plan back: Which medicines are you having now? What changes after induction? What would make you call today? Ask your team to fill any gaps.
