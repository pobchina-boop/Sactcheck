When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Report new breathlessness, jaundice, marked bleeding, severe diarrhoea, inability to drink, chest symptoms or a severe rash promptly.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Your medicines and their different roles

Two chemotherapy phases can reduce the risk of recurrence. Before surgery, treatment can also shrink the cancer. Your MDT confirms the indication and whether any separate targeted or endocrine treatment is needed.

Anthracycline chemotherapy

Doxorubicin

Interferes with DNA handling

Doxorubicin interferes with enzymes and DNA processes needed for cell division. It can also affect heart muscle, so the team considers previous exposure and cardiac risk.

DNA handling disrupted↓Cell damage↓Division reduced

What to watch for
Report pain or burning at the infusion site immediately. New breathlessness or chest symptoms need prompt review. Red urine for a short time can be a drug-colour effect; pain, clots or persistent bleeding still need advice.

Sources: nccp

Alkylating chemotherapy

Cyclophosphamide

Damages DNA needed for growth

Cyclophosphamide is activated in the body and damages DNA. This helps stop dividing cancer cells but can also affect blood-cell production and reproductive tissues.

Drug activated↓DNA damage↓Cell division affected

What to watch for
Use the prescribed anti-sickness and hydration plan. Report fever, painful urination or blood in the urine.

Sources: nccp

Taxane chemotherapy

Paclitaxel

Taxol (conventional paclitaxel)
Locks the cell division machinery

Cells use tiny structures called microtubules to separate their chromosomes when dividing. Paclitaxel stabilises these structures so they cannot rearrange normally, interrupting cell division. It can also affect nerves and other healthy tissues.

Microtubules form↓Structures remain locked↓Division is interrupted

What to watch for
Report tingling or numbness before the next weekly dose, especially if it affects walking or hand use. Tell the nurse immediately about flushing, chest or throat tightness, itching, dizziness or breathing changes during infusion.

Practical treatment guide

Your practical treatment plan

Four AC treatments 14 days apart, followed 14 days after the last AC by 12 weekly paclitaxel doses, subject to recovery and review.

STEP 1

Before the first visit

Blood tests, cardiac/immune checks as relevant, medicines review and the consent conversation come first.

STEP 2

Plan venous access

A cannula, PICC or implanted port may be used. If a port is chosen, a trained team inserts it in a separate procedure with local anaesthetic and sometimes sedation; the team explains fasting, blood-thinner and wound instructions.

STEP 3

Infusion day

Trained staff check and access the line, give the prescribed premedicines and infusions, and monitor for reactions. Tell the nurse immediately about pain, burning, rash or breathlessness.

STEP 4

Home and next review

This regimen does not routinely need a take-home infusion pump. Staff disconnect the infusion; a port is de-accessed when appropriate. Keep line-care and next-visit instructions.

Port insertion and removal

The line team explains placement, dressing care and when normal activities can resume. A port is accessed with a special needle during treatment. Removal of the implanted device is a separate planned procedure when no longer needed; do not remove or adjust it yourself.

PICC care and urgent line symptoms

A PICC usually needs regular dressing and flushing appointments even between treatments. Fever, redness, swelling, pain, leakage or damage requires prompt advice. Your hospital supplies the exact care instructions.

Visit time and transport

First visits can take longer because of loading doses, premedicines and observation. Ask the unit about expected time, transport and any escort needed after sedation or line insertion.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Support for this phase

Use the separate AC and weekly-paclitaxel supportive scripts. G-CSF is required with dose-dense AC. Taxane premedicines are reviewed when the phase changes.

Bloods and cardiac checks

FBC, kidney/liver tests → Before each AC treatment → Before each weekly dose. Symptoms or risk determine extra checks.

Visits and practical support

Ask about travel, work, fertility, menopause, exercise and help at home. The team arranges and reviews clinical checks; the patient should not have to calculate the monitoring timetable.

Follow-up and bone health

If an aromatase inhibitor or ovarian suppression is prescribed, assess fracture risk and baseline DEXA as indicated. Repeat timing depends on the result, risk and bone treatment; it is not an automatic scan for everyone.

Understanding the evidence

CALGB 9741 and E1199

Read the study population and endpoint alongside the numbers.

Show trial results and what the numbers mean

Separate randomised trials support dose density and weekly paclitaxel.

Pembrolizumab + chemotherapyPlacebo + chemotherapy

CALGB 9741 tested dose-dense schedules; E1199 tested taxane schedules after conventional AC. Neither is an exact randomised comparison of every step of this NCCP sequence. Benefits depend on tumour biology and recurrence risk.
