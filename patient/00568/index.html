When to get help

Call 112 / 999 for severe breathing difficulty, severe chest pain, collapse or a seizure.

More about my medicines

Pembrolizumab

PD-1 is a control switch on T cells, a type of immune cell. When it connects with PD-L1 or PD-L2, the T cell receives a signal to reduce its activity. Some cancers use this pathway to escape immune attack. Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, helping T cells recognise and attack the cancer. It does not directly poison cancer cells like chemotherapy. Releasing this brake can also allow immune cells to attack healthy organs, which explains the different side-effect pattern and why symptoms can appear after treatment stops.

Pemetrexed

Pemetrexed is an antifolate: it blocks several enzymes that cells use to turn folate-dependent materials into DNA and RNA building blocks. With fewer building blocks, cancer cells have more difficulty copying their genetic instructions and dividing. Healthy rapidly renewing tissues, including bone marrow and the mouth lining, can also be affected. That helps explain low blood counts and mouth soreness. Prescribed folic acid and vitamin B12 reduce toxicity; they are part of the treatment, not supplements to omit because the drug is an antifolate.

Carboplatin

Carboplatin is a platinum-containing chemotherapy. Inside cells, it forms chemical attachments to DNA, including links that stop DNA strands working normally. This disrupts copying and repair and can lead to cell death. Healthy cells can also be injured, especially blood-forming cells in the bone marrow. The kidneys help remove carboplatin, so kidney function is used to calculate the dose. AUC 5 describes a target drug exposure over time; it is not a fixed dose of 5 mg.

Between treatments

These are possible patterns, not guaranteed symptom dates.

Day 1: Your infusion day

Your team checks symptoms and blood results before treatment. Tell your nurse immediately about pain at the drip site, itching, flushing or breathing changes. The time in hospital includes checks and preparation, not just infusion time.

What to do: Before leaving, check your take-home medicines, contact details and next appointment.

Day 2–5: The first days at home

Feeling sick can start on treatment day or later. Appetite, taste, sleep or bowel habits may change. Take prescribed supportive medicines as directed; ask for help if they are not controlling symptoms.

What to do: Contact the team if vomiting persists, you cannot keep fluids down, or you develop new diarrhoea. Do not assume diarrhoea is ordinary chemotherapy upset.

Day 6–14: Keep checking how you feel

Blood counts may be reduced between treatments. You cannot tell your count from the date or from how well you feel. Fatigue or mouth symptoms may affect eating and daily activity.

What to do: Use the urgent advice below for fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell, including with a normal temperature.

Day 15–21: Prepare for your next review

Some symptoms may settle; others can continue or appear for the first time. Improvement does not prove your blood counts have recovered. The next treatment depends on your review and tests.

What to do: Bring your symptom record and questions. Confirm the next appointment: the next Day 1 is usually 21 days after this cycle's Day 1, if treatment proceeds.

Support at home

Vitamins and medicines at home

Take folic acid as prescribed and attend for vitamin B12 injections. Follow the written steroid and anti-sickness plan. Ask the team to explain any overlapping instructions.

When treatment changes

When carboplatin stops, the other medicines may continue. Vitamins and prescribed rash-prevention steroids are still needed with pemetrexed. Keep your immunotherapy alert card and report new symptoms promptly.
