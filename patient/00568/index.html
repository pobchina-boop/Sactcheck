How your medicines work

Immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Releases an immune brake
Immune cell→Brake blocked→Cancer cell

PD-1 is a control switch on T cells, a type of immune cell. When it connects with PD-L1 or PD-L2, the T cell receives a signal to reduce its activity. Some cancers use this pathway to escape immune attack. Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, helping T cells recognise and attack the cancer. It does not directly poison cancer cells like chemotherapy. Releasing this brake can also allow immune cells to attack healthy organs, which explains the different side-effect pattern and why symptoms can appear after treatment stops.

The immune system can also inflame healthy organs. New cough, diarrhoea, unusual weakness, severe headache, thirst or a spreading rash need prompt advice. Effects can start during treatment or after it finishes.

Chemotherapy

Pemetrexed

Disrupts DNA building
DNA building blocks→Supply disrupted→Cell division slows

Pemetrexed is an antifolate: it blocks several enzymes that cells use to turn folate-dependent materials into DNA and RNA building blocks. With fewer building blocks, cancer cells have more difficulty copying their genetic instructions and dividing. Healthy rapidly renewing tissues, including bone marrow and the mouth lining, can also be affected. That helps explain low blood counts and mouth soreness. Prescribed folic acid and vitamin B12 reduce toxicity; they are part of the treatment, not supplements to omit because the drug is an antifolate.

Healthy tissues can be affected too: low blood counts, a sore mouth, rash and kidney problems are important. Folic acid, vitamin B12 and prescribed steroids help reduce particular risks.

Chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Damages cancer-cell DNA
DNA strands→Platinum links→Copying disrupted

Carboplatin is a platinum-containing chemotherapy. Inside cells, it forms chemical attachments to DNA, including links that stop DNA strands working normally. This disrupts copying and repair and can lead to cell death. Healthy cells can also be injured, especially blood-forming cells in the bone marrow. The kidneys help remove carboplatin, so kidney function is used to calculate the dose. AUC 5 describes a target drug exposure over time; it is not a fixed dose of 5 mg.

Low blood counts, nausea and vomiting can occur. An allergic reaction is also possible: tell the nurse immediately if you feel flushed, itchy, tight-chested or unwell during the drip.

Induction: up to four cycles: Pembrolizumab, pemetrexed and carboplatin are given on Day 1 of a 21-day cycle for up to four cycles. The team may delay or change treatment after reviewing symptoms and test results.
Maintenance uses two medicines: After induction, carboplatin stops. Pembrolizumab and pemetrexed may continue every 21 days according to benefit, side effects and the consultant plan. Blood checks, folic acid, vitamin B12 and pemetrexed rash prophylaxis continue.
Symptoms do not follow a fixed calendar: Chemotherapy effects may overlap between cycles. Immune-related effects can begin during treatment or after it has ended. A symptom band does not identify a safe day or predict your personal recovery.
Care continues after the last infusion: Folic acid continues for 21 days after the final pemetrexed dose. Keep your immunotherapy alert information and follow the team’s monitoring and urgent-contact plan.
Why these bands stay broad: The treatment bands show a reference 21-day cycle. Induction and maintenance contain different medicines. Symptom bands are broad educational illustrations, not measured daily risks or a prediction of when you will feel well; immune-related effects can occur after treatment ends. Band length does not show likelihood or severity, and the chart cannot tell you whether it is safe to receive treatment.

There are no guaranteed “safe days”. Fever, shivers, sudden illness or new serious symptoms need prompt contact using your oncology team’s instructions. New breathlessness, cough, severe diarrhoea, marked weakness or other possible immune-related symptoms can occur during or after treatment. Do not wait for the next appointment or use the timeline to decide a symptom is safe.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Treatment phases and appointments

Induction: all three medicines on Day 1 every 21 days for up to four cycles. Maintenance: carboplatin stops; pembrolizumab and pemetrexed may continue every 21 days while appropriate.

STEP 2

Checks after treatment

Folic acid continues throughout pemetrexed and for 21 days after its last dose. Vitamin B12 remains due every three cycles while pemetrexed continues. Immune-related symptoms can arise during or after treatment; maintain alert-card and urgent-contact education. Response imaging follows the consultant plan.

STEP 3

Access and practical support

IV treatment; no 46-hour home fluorouracil pump. Venous access choice is individual. Check B12 appointments, daily folate supply, steroid directions and the immunotherapy alert card across both phases.

Support around treatment

Induction: carboplatin-containing cycles 1–4

High emetic risk is driven by carboplatin AUC5. Dedicated NCIS carboplatin row has no routine olanzapine. Do not combine rash and antiemetic dexamethasone courses without reconciliation. Carry immunotherapy alert card; do not wait until next cycle for possible immune toxicity.

Maintenance: pembrolizumab + pemetrexed

Folate, B12 and rash prophylaxis continue because pemetrexed continues. Carboplatin ends after induction; review supportive prescriptions at the phase change. Continue renal, glucose, thyroid and immune-toxicity monitoring.

Do not wait for the next appointment

Emergency: 112 / 999. Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain needs emergency help.
Contact oncology immediately for temperature ≥37.5°C or ≤36°C, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell, even with a normal temperature. Follow your own hospital’s instructions. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment.
