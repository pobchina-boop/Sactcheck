When to get help

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Understand your treatment

Three medicines. Different jobs.

The combination aims to control metastatic non-squamous lung cancer. Your team checks cancer markers, your health and your preferences. Chemotherapy and immunotherapy work in different ways.

Immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Keytruda
Releases an immune brake

PD-1 is a control switch on T cells, a type of immune cell. When it connects with PD-L1 or PD-L2, the T cell receives a signal to reduce its activity. Some cancers use this pathway to escape immune attack. Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, helping T cells recognise and attack the cancer. It does not directly poison cancer cells like chemotherapy. Releasing this brake can also allow immune cells to attack healthy organs, which explains the different side-effect pattern and why symptoms can appear after treatment stops.

Immune cell↓Brake blocked↓Cancer cell

What to watch for
The immune system can also inflame healthy organs. New cough, diarrhoea, unusual weakness, severe headache, thirst or a spreading rash need prompt advice. Effects can start during treatment or after it finishes.

Sources: pembro-label

Chemotherapy

Pemetrexed

Alimta
Disrupts DNA building

Pemetrexed is an antifolate: it blocks several enzymes that cells use to turn folate-dependent materials into DNA and RNA building blocks. With fewer building blocks, cancer cells have more difficulty copying their genetic instructions and dividing. Healthy rapidly renewing tissues, including bone marrow and the mouth lining, can also be affected. That helps explain low blood counts and mouth soreness. Prescribed folic acid and vitamin B12 reduce toxicity; they are part of the treatment, not supplements to omit because the drug is an antifolate.

DNA building blocks↓Supply disrupted↓Cell division slows

What to watch for
Healthy tissues can be affected too: low blood counts, a sore mouth, rash and kidney problems are important. Folic acid, vitamin B12 and prescribed steroids help reduce particular risks.

Sources: pem-label

Chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Platinum chemotherapy
Damages cancer-cell DNA

Carboplatin is a platinum-containing chemotherapy. Inside cells, it forms chemical attachments to DNA, including links that stop DNA strands working normally. This disrupts copying and repair and can lead to cell death. Healthy cells can also be injured, especially blood-forming cells in the bone marrow. The kidneys help remove carboplatin, so kidney function is used to calculate the dose. AUC 5 describes a target drug exposure over time; it is not a fixed dose of 5 mg.

DNA strands↓Platinum links↓Copying disrupted

What to watch for
Low blood counts, nausea and vomiting can occur. An allergic reaction is also possible: tell the nurse immediately if you feel flushed, itchy, tight-chested or unwell during the drip.

Sources: carboplatin · nccp · macmillan

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Your infusion day

Your team checks symptoms and blood results before treatment. Tell your nurse immediately about pain at the drip site, itching, flushing or breathing changes. The time in hospital includes checks and preparation, not just infusion time. Before leaving, check your take-home medicines, contact details and next appointment.

Day 2–5: The first days at home

Feeling sick can start on treatment day or later. Appetite, taste, sleep or bowel habits may change. Take prescribed supportive medicines as directed; ask for help if they are not controlling symptoms. Contact the team if vomiting persists, you cannot keep fluids down, or you develop new diarrhoea. Do not assume diarrhoea is ordinary chemotherapy upset.

Day 6–14: Keep checking how you feel

Blood counts may be reduced between treatments. You cannot tell your count from the date or from how well you feel. Fatigue or mouth symptoms may affect eating and daily activity. Use the urgent advice below for fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell, including with a normal temperature.

Day 15–21: Prepare for your next review

Some symptoms may settle; others can continue or appear for the first time. Improvement does not prove your blood counts have recovered. The next treatment depends on your review and tests. Bring your symptom record and questions. Confirm the next appointment: the next Day 1 is usually 21 days after this cycle's Day 1, if treatment proceeds.

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

Treatment phases and appointments

Induction: all three medicines on Day 1 every 21 days for up to four cycles. Maintenance: carboplatin stops; pembrolizumab and pemetrexed may continue every 21 days while appropriate.

STEP 2

Checks after treatment

Folic acid continues throughout pemetrexed and for 21 days after its last dose. Vitamin B12 remains due every three cycles while pemetrexed continues. IV treatment; no 46-hour home fluorouracil pump. Venous access choice is individual. Check B12 appointments, daily folate supply, steroid directions and the immunotherapy alert card across both phases.

Treatment reference · 01

Understanding your diagnosis and treatment aim

Start here if the names and abbreviations are new. You can read one section at a time and return when you need it.

What do the cancer terms mean?

Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a broad group of lung cancers. “Non-squamous” describes the cells seen by the pathologist and includes adenocarcinoma. “Metastatic” means the cancer has spread beyond its original site. “First-line” means the first drug treatment chosen for this stage of illness. A regimen is a planned combination and schedule of medicines.

Sources: nccp

What is the treatment trying to achieve?

The usual aims in this setting are to control cancer, help symptoms and extend life while preserving quality of life. It is generally not given with an expectation of cure. Some people have prolonged benefit; others do not respond. Ask what success would look like for you: easier breathing, less pain, stable scans, more time or a balance of these.

Sources: keynote189

Why are tumour tests important?

The team considers the cancer’s molecular results and PD-L1 result alongside your health. EGFR and ALK alterations can point towards a different treatment approach. PD-L1 is a protein involved in immune signalling; its result helps inform choices but is not a guarantee of benefit. This combination has evidence across PD-L1 groups, including low expression. Ask whether all relevant tumour testing is complete before your plan is finalised.

Sources: nccp · pembro-label · keynote189 · biomarkers

What are the alternatives?

The right comparison depends on tumour tests, symptoms, fitness and your preferences. Ask whether targeted therapy, immunotherapy alone, a different chemotherapy approach, a clinical trial or symptom-focused care is relevant. Supportive and palliative care can accompany active cancer treatment: help with breathlessness, pain, appetite and family concerns is not reserved for the last days of life.

Sources: pembro-label · palliative

Treatment reference · 02

Before the first infusion

Use this as a conversation checklist. Your own hospital’s instructions and prescription take priority.

Tell the team your starting point

Describe your usual breathing, bowel pattern, energy, skin symptoms and ability to manage daily tasks. Mention autoimmune illness, organ transplant, previous lung inflammation, infections and all regular medicines. Changes are easier to recognise when the team knows what is normal for you. Do not minimise symptoms because you are worried treatment will be delayed.

Sources: pembro-label · nccp

Make the first week manageable

Have the oncology and out-of-hours numbers available, a working thermometer, your medicine schedule and a way to get urgent help. Ask who to ring if you miss a supportive medicine. Plan transport and practical help if needed. Bring your questions and, if you wish, someone you trust to listen and take notes.

Sources: hse

During the infusion visit

Expect a symptom review and checks before drugs are released. Treatment is given through a cannula or another agreed venous access device. Tell the nurse immediately about burning or swelling around the drip, itching, flushing, dizziness or breathing changes. Waiting, pharmacy preparation and observation add to the time spent receiving the medicines.

Sources: eviq

Treatment reference · 03

What your tests and scans are checking

Different checks answer different questions: whether treatment is safe today, whether a symptom needs investigation, and whether the cancer is responding.

Blood counts: three separate jobs

Neutrophils help fight infection; a low level can make infection dangerous. Haemoglobin carries oxygen; a low level can contribute to tiredness and breathlessness. Platelets help stop bleeding; low levels can cause bruising or bleeding. Feeling well does not prove your counts are normal, and a previous normal result does not rule out a new problem.

Sources: infection · hse

Kidney, liver and hormone checks

Kidneys remove medicines and influence carboplatin dosing. Liver tests can show injury before obvious symptoms develop. Pembrolizumab can disturb hormone-producing glands, so blood glucose and thyroid monitoring matter too. Symptoms can prompt additional tests. Always report how you feel: routine blood tests alone do not exclude every immune side effect.

Sources: nccp · immune

Scans and the treatment decision

Your team sets the scan schedule and compares scans with symptoms and examination findings. Stable disease can be a worthwhile result. New symptoms need review even before the next scan. An unusual scan pattern sometimes needs clarification; do not assume that apparent growth is simply an immune response or that feeling worse means treatment is working.

Sources: nccp

Why a delay or a change can be appropriate

A pause may allow recovery or investigation. Chemotherapy doses can sometimes be reduced. Pembrolizumab is generally held or stopped for relevant toxicity rather than dose-reduced. Ask which drug is changing and why; stopping one drug does not automatically mean stopping every part of the plan. Long-term treatment decisions belong in a discussion about benefit, risk and what matters to you.

Sources: pembro-label

Treatment reference · 04

Side effects: what they mean and what to do

These are selected possibilities, not a prediction or a complete medicine leaflet. You do not need to work out the cause before asking for help.

Nausea, vomiting and appetite change

Medicines can stimulate sickness pathways in the gut and brain. Take preventive anti-sickness medicines as prescribed, even when you initially feel well. Smaller meals and frequent sips may be easier. Report poor control early so the plan can be adjusted. Persistent vomiting, inability to keep fluids down or much less urine need prompt advice. Do not wait until you are very dehydrated.

Sources: nausea

Fatigue: common, but not always “just treatment”

Fatigue may reflect treatment, disturbed sleep, reduced intake, anaemia or another illness. Balance rest with manageable movement and prioritise activities that matter to you. A sudden change, severe weakness, dizziness, breathlessness or inability to manage basic tasks needs assessment; hormone problems and other complications can resemble ordinary tiredness. Tell the team how fatigue affects daily life, not just that you feel tired.

Sources: fatigue · immune

Mouth soreness, ulcers and taste

Treatment can injure the lining of the mouth. Use a soft toothbrush, gentle mouth care and the rinses recommended by your team. Choose softer foods and avoid foods that sting. Ask for pain relief early. Report ulcers, white patches, painful swallowing or reduced intake; difficulty drinking or fever needs urgent advice. Arrange dental care with the oncology team before procedures.

Sources: mouth

Diarrhoea or abdominal pain

Pemetrexed can affect the bowel, but pembrolizumab can also cause bowel inflammation (colitis). Report new diarrhoea promptly, including how many stools above your normal pattern, blood or mucus, night-time symptoms and pain. Do not assume it is a harmless expected effect or rely on anti-diarrhoeal medicine without the team’s instructions. Severe pain, bleeding or dehydration needs urgent assessment.

Sources: immune · pem-label

Constipation

Anti-sickness drugs, opioid painkillers, less food and less movement can slow the bowel. Ask about a bowel plan if you are prone to constipation or take opioids. Keep track of your usual pattern. Increasing abdominal pain, vomiting, a swollen abdomen or inability to pass wind needs prompt assessment rather than simply increasing laxatives.

Sources: macmillan

Bruising, bleeding or breathlessness

Chemotherapy may reduce platelets or red cells. Report unexpected bruises, nosebleeds, bleeding gums or increasing breathlessness. Heavy bleeding, black stools with weakness, severe chest pain or severe breathing difficulty need emergency help. Do not start iron or alter blood thinners without review: the cause and treatment need to be established.

Sources: hse

Skin, hair, nerves and hearing

Skin can become dry, itchy or develop a rash; report new changes, especially if spreading. Hair may thin. Tingling, numbness or hearing changes should be reported before the next dose because they may affect the plan. Blisters, peeling skin or sores involving the eyes or mouth need urgent assessment. Use gentle skin care and discuss suitable sun protection.

Sources: eviq · macmillan

Treatment reference · 05

Pembrolizumab: symptoms that deserve special attention

Immune treatment can cause inflammation in healthy organs, during treatment or after it ends. Keep your immunotherapy alert card and tell other healthcare professionals you have received pembrolizumab.

Lungs, bowel, liver and kidneys

New cough or breathlessness, diarrhoea, yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, reduced urine or swelling can signal inflammation. These symptoms also have other causes, including infection and cancer. Contact the team promptly for assessment rather than trying to identify the organ or diagnosis yourself.

Sources: immune

Hormones, heart and nervous system

Report unusual weakness, persistent headache, dizziness, marked thirst, frequent urination, palpitations, muscle weakness or changes in vision. Severe chest pain, collapse, confusion, seizures or major breathing difficulty require emergency care. Some hormone effects need ongoing replacement treatment even after immunotherapy stops.

Sources: pembro-label · immune

Why early reporting matters

The team may pause treatment and investigate. Some reactions need steroids or other immune-suppressing treatment; others need hormone replacement. These are different from the short planned steroid course around chemotherapy. Do not start leftover steroids, change their dose or stop a prescribed course yourself. Carry the treatment name when attending emergency care.

Sources: immune

Treatment reference · 06

Living with treatment

The aim is a plan that fits your life as well as your treatment. Bring practical concerns to the team early.

Food, fluids and weight

Aim for enough food and fluid to maintain strength, using foods you tolerate. Tell the team about weight loss, swallowing problems or difficulty shopping or cooking; ask for dietitian support. A fixed fluid target is not right for everyone, particularly with heart or kidney disease. “Anti-cancer” diets, fasting and high-dose supplements are not substitutes for treatment and can create nutritional or interaction problems.

Sources: macmillan

Movement, work and rest

Try activity that feels manageable and increase or reduce it according to symptoms. Short walks or simple daily tasks may be more realistic than your usual exercise. Discuss work adjustments and travel plans. Do not drive if dizzy, unusually tired or affected by sedating medicines. Sudden deterioration should prompt assessment, not an attempt to push through exercise.

Sources: fatigue

Infection precautions without isolation

Wash hands, practise food hygiene and avoid close contact with people who are unwell. Ask about vaccinations and their timing; some vaccines are unsuitable during treatment. You can usually still spend time with family. Keep your urgent contact plan available: precautions reduce risk but do not make fever or sudden illness safe to watch at home.

Sources: infection · eviq

Intimacy, fertility and pregnancy

Discuss fertility preservation before treatment if relevant. Treatment may harm a developing baby; ask for a written contraception plan covering all three medicines and the period after the last dose. Do not breastfeed during treatment; confirm when it would be safe to restart. Tell the team promptly about a possible pregnancy. Ask about barrier protection and handling body fluids under your hospital’s advice.

Sources: pem-label · eviq

Emotional support and choices

It is reasonable to feel worried, angry, uncertain or overloaded. Ask for psycho-oncology, counselling, social work or family support. You can request information in smaller pieces, an interpreter or a written summary. Tell the team what you most want to preserve in daily life. You can revisit treatment decisions and ask for more time or another explanation.

Sources: macmillan

Treatment reference · 07

A short treatment dictionary

Terms you may hear in clinic.

Cycle · induction · maintenance

A cycle is a planned treatment-and-interval period. Induction is the initial combination phase. Maintenance is ongoing treatment after that phase; it does not mean side-effect-free treatment.

Sources: nccp

Response · stable disease · progression

Response means the cancer has reduced by the assessment used. Stable disease means it has not changed enough to meet response or progression criteria. Progression means growth or spread meeting the assessment criteria. Your team interprets these alongside how you are feeling.

Sources: keynote189

Neutropenia · anaemia · thrombocytopenia

Neutropenia means low infection-fighting neutrophils. Anaemia means low haemoglobin. Thrombocytopenia means low platelets. These are different results with different implications; one normal number does not establish that all treatment risks are low.

Sources: infection · hse

Immune-related · colitis · pneumonitis

Immune-related effects come from immune activity against healthy tissue. Colitis means inflammation of the colon; pneumonitis means inflammation in the lungs. A symptom alone cannot establish either diagnosis.

Sources: immune

Adding pembrolizumab to chemotherapy helped people in the study live longer and delayed cancer progression. Your benefit depends on your cancer and health.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

How will we know if it is helping?

Ask your oncologist what the treatment is aiming to achieve for you, when scans are planned and what would lead to continuing, changing or stopping it. How you feel and the amount of side effects do not reliably show how well treatment is working.

Why might a cycle be delayed?

A delay can give time to recover or investigate a new symptom. It is a safety decision, not a personal failure. Ask which medicines are affected, what needs to improve and when you will be reviewed.

Will I get every side effect?

No. The body map shows selected possibilities, not a prediction. Ask about common effects, serious less common effects, and any long-term risks that matter particularly to you. The same symptom can have several causes.

What should I organise before starting?

Confirm blood-test and Day Ward arrangements, transport home, your medicines schedule and a thermometer. Keep the oncology contact instructions and immunotherapy alert card available. Ask for an interpreter or accessible information if useful.

What else belongs in the consent conversation?

Your priorities, expected benefit, alternatives including supportive care, fertility, contraception, pregnancy or breastfeeding, work, driving and the possibility of lasting side effects. You can ask more questions or reconsider treatment at any stage.

Do more side effects mean the treatment is working better?

No. Side effects are not a reliable personal measure of cancer response. Report them so they can be treated; do not tolerate serious symptoms in the hope that they are a sign of benefit.

Will the same three medicines continue indefinitely?

No. The planned carboplatin phase is up to four cycles. Pembrolizumab and pemetrexed may continue afterwards. Ask about the intended duration of each drug, review points and reasons for a change.

Can I use this page to decide whether my blood tests are good enough?

No. Your treating team combines blood results, symptoms, prior toxicity and the prescribing protocol. This page explains the checks; it does not authorise a dose or a treatment decision.

What should I say when calling about a symptom?

Say you are receiving pembrolizumab, pemetrexed and carboplatin (or your current maintenance drugs), the last treatment date, what has changed, when it started, your temperature and whether you can eat, drink and pass urine. Say if symptoms are worsening. Do not delay calling to collect every detail.

Which checks continue when treatment changes or finishes?

Folic acid continues throughout pemetrexed and for 21 days after its last dose. Vitamin B12 remains due every three cycles while pemetrexed continues. Immune-related symptoms can arise during or after treatment; maintain alert-card and urgent-contact education. Response imaging follows the consultant plan.

Try explaining the plan back: Which medicines are you having now? What changes after induction? What would make you call today? Ask your team to fill any gaps.
