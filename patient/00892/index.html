Adjuvant ribociclib (Kisqali) + endocrine therapy

For eligible higher-risk HR-positive/HER2-negative early breast cancer, ribociclib is added to endocrine therapy to reduce recurrence risk.

The usual adjuvant starting dose is ribociclib 400 mg once daily on Days 1–21, followed by Days 22–28 off, for up to three years. The aromatase inhibitor continues every day on its own plan; ovarian suppression is added when indicated.

Tests and checks

full blood count (FBC), renal and liver profile; potassium, calcium, magnesium and phosphorus. electrocardiogram (ECG) before initiation; QTcF must be below 450 ms. Correct electrolytes and review QT/CYP3A interactions. Confirm stage, recurrence-risk criteria, prior treatment, aromatase inhibitor (AI) and ovarian-suppression plan; reconcile the National Cancer Control Programme (NCCP) node-negative eligibility/exclusion conflict before selection. FBC, renal and liver profile every two weeks for cycles 1–2, then before cycles 3–6, then as clinically indicated. Electrolytes before each of the first six cycles, then as clinically indicated. Repeat ECG around cycle 1 Day 14, then as clinically indicated. Increase liver monitoring for grade 2 or worse abnormalities; review fever, jaundice, palpitations/fainting, breathlessness and severe skin symptoms. Keep early-breast surveillance and bone/endocrine follow-up distinct from treatment-toxicity checks and from metastatic response imaging.

Between treatment visits

Planned treatment days are distinct from broad possible symptom patterns. Symptoms can occur outside these bands.

There are no guaranteed safe days. Fever, shivers, sudden illness, new breathing problems or severe symptoms need prompt advice on any day, including a treatment break.

Five warning signs: contact your team promptly

These are priority warning signs, not the five most common side effects. Keep using the detailed anatomical map. Do not wait for a diary entry or your next appointment to seek help.

New breathlessness or cough

Call your oncology team urgently for new or worsening shortness of breath, cough or chest pain. Lung inflammation can occur during treatment. Severe breathing difficulty or severe chest pain: call 112 / 999.

Fever, shivering or feeling suddenly unwell

Low white blood cells can make infection serious. Follow your hospital’s temperature threshold and call immediately for fever, shaking chills or sudden illness, even without a fever. Do not wait for the next appointment.

Palpitations, dizziness or fainting

Report a new fast or irregular heartbeat or dizziness urgently. Ribociclib can affect heart rhythm. Fainting, collapse or severe chest pain needs emergency assessment; call 112 / 999.

Yellow skin or eyes, or dark urine

Call your team urgently for yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, pain high on the right side of your tummy, or unusual bleeding. Liver changes may also occur without symptoms, so keep blood-test appointments.

Blistering or rapidly worsening rash

Seek urgent medical assessment for a painful or spreading rash, peeling skin, blisters, or sores involving the mouth, eyes or lips, especially with fever. Do not wait for the next clinic visit.

Everyday effects of ribociclib (Kisqali)

Tiredness

Pace activities and plan short rests. Tell the team if fatigue limits your usual activities or self-care; blood tests may identify a contributing cause.

Nausea, appetite or bowel changes

Try small meals and regular drinks. Use only the anti-sickness or bowel medicines agreed with your team. Call if symptoms persist, intake falls or you cannot keep fluids down.

Milder skin or hair changes

Gentle skin care and moisturiser may help dryness. Report new rashes rather than assuming they are harmless. Hair thinning can occur; ask about practical support.

Changes found on blood tests

White blood cells and liver tests can change before you feel unwell. Attend the planned tests and wait for the team’s advice about the next cycle.

Your anti-hormonal (endocrine) treatment

Ribociclib and endocrine treatment have different jobs. Your prescribed aromatase inhibitor has its own schedule. Ovarian suppression may also be part of your plan. Effects overlap: you do not need to decide which medicine caused a symptom.

Hot flushes and night sweats

Layers, a cooler bedroom and identifying personal triggers may help. Tell the team if flushes disturb sleep or daily life; treatment options should be checked for interactions with Kisqali.

Joint or muscle aches

Gentle movement and regular activity can help stiffness. Discuss persistent pain and suitable pain relief. Report new focal or worsening pain; do not automatically attribute it to hormone treatment.

Mood and sleep changes

Tell the team about ongoing low mood, anxiety or disrupted sleep. These can have several causes, and support is available. Seek urgent help if you feel unsafe or at risk of harming yourself.

Vaginal dryness and sexual wellbeing

Dryness, discomfort or reduced interest in sex can occur. Ask about non-hormonal moisturisers, lubricants and specialist support. Discuss any hormonal product with your oncology team first.

Bone health over time

Aromatase inhibitors and ovarian suppression can affect bone strength. Ask about your bone-density assessment, activity, calcium/vitamin D needs and whether bone-protective treatment is indicated.

Medicines, supplements and food: check before adding anything

Interactions can increase side effects, reduce treatment effectiveness or affect another medicine. This is a set of examples, not a complete interaction checker. Do not start, stop or change a prescribed medicine or your Kisqali dose without your team.

May increase Kisqali exposure and side effects

Some medicines slow the enzyme that clears ribociclib (CYP3A4).

Examples include clarithromycin, itraconazole and ritonavir-containing medicines. Grapefruit and grapefruit juice can also raise exposure.

What to do: Avoid grapefruit. Ask the oncology pharmacist to check any new prescription, including short courses of antibiotics or antivirals.

May reduce Kisqali exposure and effectiveness

Some medicines increase CYP3A4 activity, so the body removes ribociclib more quickly.

Examples include rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin and St John’s wort.

What to do: Avoid St John’s wort. Do not stop prescribed medicines yourself; ask the oncology team to arrange a suitable alternative when needed.

May add to heart-rhythm risk

Other medicines can also lengthen the heart’s QT interval.

Examples include amiodarone, moxifloxacin and ondansetron. Tamoxifen is not a recommended partner with ribociclib.

What to do: Check anti-sickness medicines, antibiotics and all other treatments with your team. An example list cannot establish that an unlisted medicine is safe.

Kisqali can increase other medicine levels

Ribociclib itself inhibits CYP3A4.

Some sedatives, pain medicines and other prescriptions may need an alternative, monitoring or dose adjustment.

What to do: Keep an up-to-date list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal products. Tell other prescribers that you take ribociclib.
