When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Immunotherapy can cause inflammation in healthy organs during treatment or after it stops. Contact the oncology team promptly for new diarrhoea, blood in stools, cough or breathlessness, yellow skin or eyes, severe headache, vision changes, marked thirst, unusual weakness or a spreading rash. Tell any clinician that you are receiving or have received immunotherapy.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Medicines in this regimen

Pembrolizumab is given before and after planned surgery in this selected melanoma pathway. The team explains the expected benefit, uncertainty, surgical plan and how side effects could change the sequence.

PD-1 checkpoint immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Keytruda
Releases a PD-1 immune brake

Pembrolizumab blocks the PD-1 checkpoint, allowing T cells to remain more active against cancer. Immune activity can also affect normal organs, so new symptoms matter during treatment and after it stops.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

An individual symptom plan

Ask which anti-sickness or other symptom medicines are prescribed, when to take them and when to call if they do not help. Do not start a borrowed medicine or alter treatment to manage a side effect yourself.

Your urgent contacts

Keep your actual oncology, out-of-hours and pharmacy contacts on the wallet card. Example numbers on a template must be replaced before you use it.

Pregnancy and fertility precautions

Discuss fertility before starting. Use effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 months after the last dose. Tell the team promptly about a possible pregnancy. Discuss breastfeeding restrictions with the team before treatment; do not breastfeed without their advice. Do not stop prescribed steroids or hormone replacement yourself.

Eating, activity and practical help

Let the team know if cramps, altered taste or poor appetite make eating or moving difficult. Ask for practical food and activity advice that fits your symptoms and any other illness.

Steroids and hormone replacement

Some immune effects need steroids or lasting hormone replacement. Follow the exact prescription and taper. Carry the relevant alert information, and do not stop or reduce these medicines yourself when anticancer treatment is held or completed.

Treatment reference · 01

Understanding your diagnosis and treatment goal

Use these questions with your own pathology report and agreed treatment plan.

What the diagnosis says

Record the original melanoma site and pathology findings. Breslow thickness and ulceration describe features of the skin tumour; stage describes its extent. Melanoma arising in the eye or a mucosal surface may need a different pathway.

BRAF and other tumour tests

BRAF V600 testing can identify an alternative targeted-treatment option in some melanomas. It is separate from the immunotherapy described here. Ask what your tumour result means for the choices available to you.

Surgery, radiotherapy and medicine have different roles

Ask whether medicine is intended to reduce recurrence after complete surgery or control cancer that remains. Add any operation or radiotherapy to the plan with its own purpose, timing and risks.

Stage and response are different

Stage describes the extent of disease. Response describes a change in measurable cancer during treatment. After complete removal there may be no visible tumour to measure; follow-up then looks for recurrence and treatment effects.

A new skin change still needs review

Report a new or changing mole, a sore that persists, a new lump or an unexplained symptom. A photo or a good response in one area cannot rule out a separate problem.

Your medicines and the overall plan

The schedule in this setting

Pembrolizumab 200 mg every 3 weeks: 3 doses before surgery, then 15 doses after surgery, subject to recovery and clinical review. The team sets the operation date and postoperative restart; do not assume that surgery or a held dose follows the routine three-week calendar.

How the treatment works

Pembrolizumab blocks the PD-1 checkpoint, allowing T cells to remain more active against cancer. Immune activity can also affect normal organs, so new symptoms matter during treatment and after it stops.

The role of earlier or later treatment

Ask how the medicine fits with previous or planned local treatment. The timing of an operation, radiotherapy and a tablet course should be written in one agreed plan.

Pauses and stopping

A safety pause is an active treatment decision. Record which medicine is paused, the reason, the tests or recovery needed and who may authorise restarting. Do not restart from an old prescription or reset the history when a new phase begins.

Treatment reference · 03

Taking treatment and preparing for visits

Keep the written medicine plan beside this guide; it records your own dose and dates.

Taking or receiving the medicine

Pembrolizumab is given as 200 mg by IV infusion over 30 minutes, on Day 1 every 3 weeks. Allow extra time for assessment, preparation and observation.

Missed, vomited or held treatment

If a visit is missed or treatment is held, contact the unit for a new plan. Do not count a missed appointment as a dose received. An interval change, operation or new phase does not erase earlier immune toxicity or a permanent-stop decision.

Other medicines and supplements

Show the pharmacist a current list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, herbal products and supplements. Include anticoagulants, heart medicines, steroids and recent antibiotics. Check before adding, stopping or changing anything; an apparently harmless product can alter treatment or confuse a symptom assessment.

Practical preparation

Bring your medicine list, allergy information and questions. Tell the team about practical problems with travel, swallowing, wound care or remembering doses before treatment starts.

Treatment reference · 04

Tests: what is required and what each result means

Safety tests, tumour tests and response scans answer different questions.

Before treatment

Blood count, kidney and liver tests, glucose and thyroid tests; review autoimmune conditions, transplants, previous immune reactions and all current medicines.

During treatment

Blood count, kidney/liver tests and glucose before every 3-week cycle. TSH is checked every 3–6 weeks, including between treatment visits when needed. Cortisol and other tests are added when clinically indicated. Report new symptoms at every visit and between visits; normal blood results do not rule out immune toxicity.

Scans and tumour markers

Ask the team to write the next assessment and who will arrange it. Bloods used to monitor kidneys, liver, hormones or muscle are not automatically measures of cancer response. There is no universal tumour-marker blood schedule for these skin-cancer regimens; a scan or extra blood test is requested for a specific clinical reason.

After a pause or finish

Continue to report possible immune effects after the last dose; your team records the follow-up tests and dates. Keep any prescribed steroid taper, hormone replacement and specialist follow-up. Scans follow your individual disease plan.

Getting the result

Keep a named contact for bookings and results. If an expected appointment or result is missing, call that service; do not take silence as confirmation that everything is complete.

Treatment reference · 05

What to expect between visits

You can feel well and still need the planned checks. New symptoms deserve attention even on a day without an appointment.

Your usual pattern

Record the start and change in symptoms, temperature if advised, bowel pattern, drinking, urine and the effect on normal tasks. A simple few-line record is enough. Use it to describe a change rather than trying to grade or diagnose a toxicity yourself.

When to call

Immunotherapy can cause inflammation in healthy organs during treatment or after it stops. Contact the oncology team promptly for new diarrhoea, blood in stools, cough or breathlessness, yellow skin or eyes, severe headache, vision changes, marked thirst, unusual weakness or a spreading rash. Tell any clinician that you are receiving or have received immunotherapy.

Eating, drinking and activity

Report nausea, mouth discomfort, diarrhoea or weight loss before they make eating and drinking difficult. The team can adjust symptom support and arrange dietitian or rehabilitation advice.

A held dose or changed plan

If a visit is missed or treatment is held, contact the unit for a new plan. Do not count a missed appointment as a dose received. An interval change, operation or new phase does not erase earlier immune toxicity or a permanent-stop decision. Feeling better or having normal tests does not by itself mean that pembrolizumab can restart. Your team reviews what happened, recovery and the steroid or hormone plan. Some reactions mean that treatment must remain stopped, even if the symptoms have settled. Follow the prescribed plan and never restart treatment or change steroids yourself. Before any restart, your team also reviews the original event, recovery and any steroid or hormone plan. Follow the prescribed plan; do not alter steroids or restart treatment yourself.

When heart, muscle and nerve symptoms overlap

Heart, muscle and nerve problems can occur together. New muscle pain or weakness with changed vision, unusual tiredness, breathlessness, chest pain or an irregular heartbeat needs immediate medical advice. Do not wait for several symptoms to appear together. Severe breathing difficulty, collapse or rapidly worsening weakness needs emergency care.

Treatment reference · 06

Skin checks, sun protection and optional photographs

This complements clinical assessment and does not replace it.

Protect your skin

Protect exposed skin with clothing, a hat and shade. Use broad-spectrum sunscreen with sun protection factor (SPF) 30 or higher and reapply as directed. Avoid sunbeds. Cloudy weather does not remove ultraviolet exposure.

Know the pattern of your skin

Ask the team to show you how to check your skin and treated area. Use a mirror or a helper for areas you cannot see. Report a new lump, a changing lesion or a sore that does not heal.

Photographs are optional

A private, dated photograph can help describe a change if your team finds it useful. Keep the lighting and size reference consistent. Photographs support a discussion; they do not replace examination and need not be uploaded.

Wounds, nails and sensitive skin

Follow the wound and skin-care instructions for the treated area. Ask before adding a cream or remedy. Increasing redness, discharge, pain, bleeding or difficulty using the area needs assessment.

Treatment reference · 07

Pregnancy, fertility and protecting others

Discuss these precautions before treatment even if pregnancy is not currently planned.

Medicine-specific precautions

Discuss fertility before starting. Use effective contraception during treatment and for at least 4 months after the last dose. Tell the team promptly about a possible pregnancy. Discuss breastfeeding restrictions with the team before treatment; do not breastfeed without their advice. Do not stop prescribed steroids or hormone replacement yourself.

Planning ahead

Ask early about fertility preservation, sexual health and symptoms affecting intimacy. Tell the team about any chance of pregnancy, breastfeeding or a partner’s pregnancy so that the correct medicine-specific advice can be given. Keep the precautions and their end dates in the written plan.

Safe handling and supply

Do not share anticancer medicines. Keep oral medicines in their original labelled packaging, away from children and anyone who could take them accidentally. Return unused medicines to the supplying pharmacy and follow its handling advice. A treatment hold does not remove pregnancy precautions.

Treatment reference · 08

Questions, decisions and practical support

Your priorities and the burden of treatment belong in the discussion.

Benefits and uncertainties

Ask what outcome this treatment aims to improve for your diagnosis and what alternatives exist. Results from a study group help a discussion but cannot promise your own result.

Alternatives and review points

Agree the next decision point and what would make the plan change. Ask about other suitable treatments, trials and symptom care. Support for comfort and function can continue alongside cancer treatment.

Help with daily life

Tell the team what is hardest in daily life: travel, work, caring duties, costs, sleep or confidence. Ask who can help and how to contact them.

Shared understanding

Before going home, check that you can describe your medicine schedule, next tests and urgent-contact route. Ask for an interpreter, larger print or a supporter if that would help.

Treatment reference · 09

Follow-up and your continuing record

Keep this record when treatment is paused, changed or completed.

Your follow-up plan

Your stage, symptoms and treatment determine follow-up. Write down the skin and lymph-node checks, any body or brain scans and the service arranging them. Confirm your own dates with the team.

Continuing medicine precautions

Continue to report possible immune effects after the last dose; your team records the follow-up tests and dates. Keep any prescribed steroid taper, hormone replacement and specialist follow-up. Scans follow your individual disease plan.

Treatment summary for other clinicians

Record the medicines and last dates, important reactions, any long-term hormone replacement or steroid taper, and the responsible oncology contact. Show this information in urgent care and to other specialists. A different treatment phase does not erase earlier toxicity.

The complete paper version

Use this handbook as your paper record. Keep the current schedule, medicines, tests and contacts together, and replace outdated pages after a change. The matching QR link is optional.

What the evidence means for your treatment

Discuss the evidence for your diagnosis, tumour test results and treatment setting.

Evidence in advanced melanoma measures control of existing cancer; adjuvant evidence measures recurrence after surgery. Perioperative evidence concerns treatment around surgery. Ask which study and outcome match your stage and plan. These results cannot predict your individual benefit.
