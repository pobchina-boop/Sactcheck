When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Contact the oncology team immediately for new or worsening breathlessness, chest pain, persistent diarrhoea, severe tummy pain, yellow skin/eyes, much less urine, severe headache, marked weakness, confusion, unusual thirst or passing much more urine, or a spreading/blistering rash. For severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or other immediately dangerous symptoms, seek emergency care. Do not wait for the next infusion.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

How pembrolizumab works

For metastatic lung cancer, the aim is to control cancer and help people live longer. After surgery and platinum chemotherapy, the adjuvant aim is to reduce the chance of cancer returning. Ask which aim applies to you.

Immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Keytruda
Blocks an immune brake

Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, a control switch on immune T cells. Cancer cells can use this switch to reduce immune attack. Blocking it can help the immune system recognise and attack cancer. The same increased immune activity can inflame healthy organs, which is why new symptoms need attention even after treatment ends.

What to watch for
Report new breathing, bowel, skin, hormone, heart or nerve symptoms promptly; these may be immune inflammation.

Your lung cancer and treatment aim

First confirm which lung setting applies to you.

Metastatic treatment

Metastatic means the cancer has spread. First-line means the first medicine treatment for this stage. Here, pembrolizumab alone is offered for suitable cancers with a high PD-L1 result and without EGFR or ALK alterations. The aim is usually control, symptom relief and more time.

After surgery

Adjuvant means treatment after surgery to reduce the chance of cancer returning. This pathway follows complete resection and platinum chemotherapy for cancer at high risk of recurrence. It does not use the same high PD-L1 requirement as the metastatic pathway.

Your alternatives

Ask which other treatments, clinical trials, surveillance or symptom-focused care fit your exact cancer and health. Supportive care can help during either pathway.

How pembrolizumab works

This is an immunotherapy medicine.

Why healthy organs can be affected

The immune system can also attack healthy tissues. Lung, bowel, liver, kidney, skin, gland, heart and nerve inflammation can range from mild to serious. Some hormone changes need long-term replacement.

A pause is a safety decision

Your team may delay a dose, treat side effects or stop pembrolizumab. The usual response to immune toxicity is to hold or stop treatment, not to give a smaller dose. Feeling better or having normal tests does not by itself mean that pembrolizumab can restart. Your team reviews what happened, recovery and the steroid or hormone plan. Some reactions mean that treatment must remain stopped, even if the symptoms have settled. Follow the prescribed plan and never restart treatment or change steroids yourself. Restart depends on the original reaction, recovery and any steroid or hormone plan. Some reactions mean treatment must remain stopped even after symptoms settle.

Why this treatment may be offered

In a trial of suitable people with metastatic lung cancer and a high PD-L1 result, pembrolizumab helped people live longer than chemotherapy. After surgery and chemotherapy, a separate trial found a lower risk of recurrence or death. Its final long-term analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in overall survival. The aims and evidence differ between these settings.

These results describe groups of people, not what will happen to one person. The metastatic and after-surgery studies answer different questions.

Changing schedules

Tests and reviews

Tell your team about symptoms even when recent blood tests were normal.

Before starting

Your team checks the cancer diagnosis, treatment aim and relevant tumour tests. Blood count, kidney/liver, glucose and thyroid tests provide a baseline. Discuss existing illnesses, medicines, pregnancy and breastfeeding.

During treatment

Blood count, kidney/liver function and glucose before every 3-week cycle; thyroid every 3–6 weeks, cortisol if indicated, and symptom review throughout and after treatment.

There are no guaranteed “safe days”. Immune effects can happen on any day and after treatment ends. Carry your immunotherapy alert card and tell any healthcare team that you receive pembrolizumab.

Between-treatment notes

Day 1: Your infusion visit

The team reviews your blood results, symptoms and medicines. Tell the nurse straight away about itching, flushing, a tight chest, dizziness or feeling suddenly unwell during the drip. Before leaving, confirm the next tests, treatment visit and urgent contact number.

Day 2–21: Between visits

You may feel well between infusions, but immune inflammation can begin at any time. Keep a short symptom record and take medicines exactly as prescribed. Contact your team about new symptoms instead of waiting for your next appointment. Confirm thyroid tests are booked every 3–6 weeks, even if infusions are six weeks apart.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Sickness medicines

Pembrolizumab alone has a minimal risk of causing sickness. Routine anti-sickness medicines or infusion premedicines are not usually needed; your team can prescribe symptom relief if required.

Immune alert card

Carry your alert card during treatment and afterwards. Show it when seeking urgent care; symptoms can be mistaken for an infection or an ordinary stomach upset.

Hormones and steroids

Some immune effects need steroids or hormone replacement. Follow the prescribed plan and do not stop steroids suddenly. Ask for help if symptoms worsen or you cannot take essential medicines. If treatment is paused because of an immune side effect, your team checks recovery and any steroid or hormone plan before deciding whether it can restart. Do not change steroids or restart treatment yourself.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Discuss pregnancy, fertility and breastfeeding before treatment. Use effective contraception during treatment and for at least four months after the last dose. If pregnancy is possible or occurs, contact your oncology team promptly.

Practical support and everyday life

Keep your team informed about what matters to you.

Energy and activity

Fatigue can affect daily activities. Balance activity with rest and ask for help if tiredness is new, marked or accompanied by dizziness, headache, thirst or weakness; it may need medical assessment.

Eating, drinking and bowels

Drink as advised and report ongoing vomiting or diarrhoea. Do not assume diarrhoea is a minor stomach upset; your team needs to check for immune inflammation and other causes.

Medicines and pregnancy

Bring a full medicine list, including supplements and steroids. Ask before starting new medicines. Use the contraception plan and discuss fertility or breastfeeding with your team.

When to get urgent help

Use the contact plan given by your oncology service.

During the drip

Tell the nurse immediately if you become itchy, flushed, breathless, dizzy, tight-chested or suddenly unwell.

Care after treatment ends

Immune inflammation can start late. Tell any healthcare team about pembrolizumab even after the course has finished.

When heart, muscle and nerve symptoms overlap

Heart, muscle and nerve problems can occur together. New muscle pain or weakness with changed vision, unusual tiredness, breathlessness, chest pain or an irregular heartbeat needs immediate medical advice. Do not wait for several symptoms to appear together. Severe breathing difficulty, collapse or rapidly worsening weakness needs emergency care.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Is my treatment for metastatic cancer or to reduce recurrence after surgery?

Ask your team to write down the treatment aim. For metastatic disease, the aim is usually cancer control, symptom relief and more time. After complete surgery and platinum chemotherapy, adjuvant treatment aims to reduce the chance of recurrence.

Which tumour-test results support this plan?

For the first-line metastatic monotherapy pathway, the source requires high PD-L1 expression and no EGFR mutation or ALK translocation. The adjuvant pathway has different eligibility. Ask which results apply to your own cancer.

How long is my course, and what could change it?

The adjuvant course lasts up to 12 months. Metastatic treatment continues while helpful and tolerable; a 24-month review may lead to stopping or continuing. Symptoms, response and your preferences can change the plan.

Who arranges blood and thyroid tests between visits?

The oncology team arranges and reviews tests. Blood count, kidney/liver function and glucose are checked before each cycle; thyroid tests are usually every 3–6 weeks. Ask whether an extra test is needed between six-weekly infusions.

Which symptoms should I report straight away, and whom do I contact out of hours?

Report new or worsening breathing problems, persistent diarrhoea, severe abdominal pain, yellow eyes, reduced urine, severe headache, weakness, thirst or a spreading rash promptly. Keep the local oncology and out-of-hours numbers available; severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain needs 112 / 999.

What are my alternatives if I choose not to have this treatment?

Ask about alternatives for your exact stage and tumour results, including other anticancer treatments, trials, surveillance where appropriate and symptom-focused care. Supportive care remains available whether or not you proceed.

Does an abnormal blood test mean pembrolizumab must be stopped permanently?

Not by itself. Your team checks whether the change is caused by immune inflammation, another medicine, cancer or another illness. They decide whether to pause treatment, investigate further or stop it. Do not change the treatment plan yourself.

Try explaining the plan back: Which medicines are you having now? What changes after induction? What would make you call today? Ask your team to fill any gaps.
