Durvalumab 1500 mg every 28 days

Durvalumab is given after definitive chemoradiotherapy to reduce the chance of lung cancer returning or worsening and improve the chance of longer survival. This is part of a treatment pathway that aims for long-term control and possible cure; it cannot guarantee cure.

How your medicines work

PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor

Durvalumab

Blocks PD-L1 to support immune recognition of cancer
PD-L1 can dampen an immune response→Durvalumab blocks the PD-L1 signal→Immune cells can act against cancer cells

Durvalumab blocks PD-L1, a protein that can reduce the immune system’s ability to attack cancer cells. Removing this signal can help immune cells act against cancer, but the immune system can also inflame healthy organs.

Report new symptoms early and carry the immunotherapy alert card. Do not wait for the next infusion.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Two different parts of treatment

Platinum chemotherapy and radiation are given together first. Durvalumab follows after that course has finished and the team has confirmed that the cancer has not progressed.

STEP 2

Infusion visits

The usual reference dose is 1500 mg, given into a vein over about 60 minutes every 28 days. Allow extra time for the clinical review, tests and preparation. If body weight is 30 kg or less, the team must prescribe an appropriate weight-based dose and interval instead of the fixed 1500 mg dose.

STEP 3

Bloods, scans and transport

Confirm where blood tests are taken, who reviews them and the planned clinic/scan appointments. Allow extra time at the treatment unit and arrange help with travel if fatigue or symptoms make it difficult.

STEP 4

A pause or early finish

Treatment can be paused to investigate symptoms or treat an immune reaction. A missed dose does not automatically extend the course beyond the planned maximum. Restart decisions belong to the consultant team.

Support around treatment

Immune side effects and the alert card

Immune side effects may start during treatment or after the last dose. Keep the immunotherapy alert card and tell every treating clinician that you have received durvalumab, even during a treatment break or after finishing.

Lung symptoms after radiotherapy

Radiation inflammation, immune inflammation, infection and cancer can cause similar symptoms. Report new or worsening breathing symptoms promptly so the team can assess the cause. Do not assume a new cough is only an old radiotherapy effect.

Support medicines

Durvalumab alone has minimal sickness risk and no routine premedication is specified. Use anti-sickness or other medicines only as prescribed. Steroids for an immune reaction are a treatment plan supervised by the team, not routine premedication; do not start, reduce or stop them yourself.

Hormone replacement and blood tests

Thyroid, adrenal or diabetes problems may need hormone replacement or insulin, sometimes long term. Keep taking prescribed medicines and attend blood tests even when you feel well. Sudden severe weakness, vomiting, faintness, unusual thirst or drowsiness need urgent advice.

Pregnancy, fertility and breastfeeding

Discuss pregnancy plans, fertility and breastfeeding before treatment. Effective contraception is advised during durvalumab and for at least three months after the last dose. Durvalumab is not recommended during pregnancy. The team must help decide whether breastfeeding or durvalumab should be stopped or avoided, weighing the benefits and risks.
