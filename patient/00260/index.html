How your medicines work

Anthracycline chemotherapy

Doxorubicin

Interferes with deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA) handling
DNA handling disrupted→Cell damage→Division reduced

Doxorubicin interferes with enzymes and DNA processes needed for cell division. It can also affect heart muscle, so the team considers previous exposure and cardiac risk.

Report pain or burning at the infusion site immediately. New breathlessness or chest symptoms need prompt review. Red urine for a short time can be a drug-colour effect; pain, clots or persistent bleeding still need advice.

Alkylating chemotherapy

Cyclophosphamide

Damages DNA needed for growth
Drug activated→DNA damage→Cell division affected

Cyclophosphamide is activated in the body and damages DNA. This helps stop dividing cancer cells but can also affect blood-cell production and reproductive tissues.

Use the prescribed anti-sickness and hydration plan. Report fever, painful urination or blood in the urine.

Taxane chemotherapy

Paclitaxel

Locks the cell division machinery
Microtubules form→Structures remain locked→Division is interrupted

Cells use tiny structures called microtubules to separate their chromosomes when dividing. Paclitaxel stabilises these structures so they cannot rearrange normally, interrupting cell division. It can also affect nerves and other healthy tissues.

Report tingling or numbness before the next next dose, especially if it affects walking or hand use. Tell the nurse immediately about flushing, chest or throat tightness, itching, dizziness or breathing changes during infusion.

What each check is for

1Before first doseBaseline

Baseline FBC, kidney and liver tests; review before each AC cycle and each paclitaxel administration under the regimen and local testing plan.

Ask / act: Confirm tests and consent before prescribing.

Sources: NCCP 00260

2Heart-monitoring planBaseline → cycles → after treatment

ESC supplementary guidance: baseline echo before anthracycline treatment. High/very-high cardiac risk: echo every two anthracycline cycles (for example before cycles 3 and 5 when continuing), and within 3 months after completion. All adults: echo within 12 months after completion. Other repeat tests depend on risk, cumulative exposure and symptoms.

Ask / act: Name the team responsible for booking and reviewing results.

Sources: NCCP 00260 · ESC

3Treatment-phase reviewBefore each transition

Four AC cycles 21 days apart, followed by 12 weekly paclitaxel doses. The team confirms recovery and the transition appointment.

Ask / act: Confirm active medicines, support script and the next test checkpoint.

Sources: NCCP 00260

4Response and follow-upPlanned review points

For early breast cancer, breast imaging and clinical review follow the agreed pathway. Routine CT/PET scans and blood tumour markers are not used to look for distant recurrence when there are no symptoms or clinical concerns.

Ask / act: Review symptoms and imaging together; do not change treatment on tumour markers alone.

Sources: NCCP 00260

Practical treatment guide

Your practical treatment plan

Four AC cycles 21 days apart, followed by 12 weekly paclitaxel doses. The team confirms recovery and the transition appointment.

STEP 1

Before the first visit

Blood tests, relevant cardiac assessment, medicines review, fertility discussion and the consent conversation come first.

STEP 2

Plan venous access

A cannula, peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) or implanted port may be used. If a port is chosen, a trained team inserts it in a separate procedure with local anaesthetic and sometimes sedation; the team explains fasting, blood-thinner and wound instructions.

STEP 3

Infusion day

Staff check the line, give prescribed premedicines and treatment, and observe for reactions. Tell them immediately about burning, pain, rash or breathlessness.

STEP 4

Home and next review

This regimen does not routinely need a take-home infusion pump. Staff disconnect the infusion; a port is de-accessed when appropriate. Keep line-care and next-visit instructions.

Port insertion and removal

The line team explains placement, dressing care and when normal activities can resume. A port is accessed with a special needle during treatment. Removal of the implanted device is a separate planned procedure when no longer needed; do not remove or adjust it yourself.

PICC care and urgent line symptoms

A PICC usually needs regular dressing and flushing appointments even between treatments. Fever, redness, swelling, pain, leakage or damage requires prompt advice. Your hospital supplies the exact care instructions.

Visit time and transport

First visits can take longer because of loading doses, premedicines and observation. Ask the unit about expected time, transport and any escort needed after sedation or line insertion.

Phase-specific visit pattern

Infusion and observation times differ by drug and first versus later doses; the unit confirms the full visit duration, including blood review and preparation.

Doxorubicin

  • Low counts, infection and mouth ulcers
  • Nausea, hair loss and fatigue
  • Heart damage, sometimes delayed
  • Severe tissue injury if the infusion leaks
  • Rare later blood cancers

Cyclophosphamide

  • Low counts and infection
  • Nausea, vomiting and hair loss
  • Bladder irritation / bleeding
  • Fertility effects and menopause
  • Rare later blood cancers

Paclitaxel

  • Low blood counts and infection
  • Numbness / tingling, sometimes lasting
  • Hair loss, muscle or joint pain
  • Infusion reaction
  • Mouth, bowel, skin and nail effects

Support around treatment

The correct phase prescription

AC is high emetogenic risk; a separate low-risk taxane script replaces it.

Premedicines and growth factors

Paclitaxel premedicines differ between weekly and fortnightly dosing. Use the phase-specific prescription.

Tests and appointments

The team should name the booking owner, due window and person reviewing each required test. Tell them if an expected appointment has not arrived.

After chemotherapy

Discuss recovery, fertility, rehabilitation and the next treatment or surveillance step.
