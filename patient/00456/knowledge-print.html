When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call oncology immediately for fever, sudden illness, new or worsening breathlessness, troublesome bleeding, inability to drink or swallow, uncontrolled vomiting, very little urine or severe rash. Tell staff at once about pain, swelling, faintness or breathing trouble during an infusion.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Chemotherapy and chest radiotherapy

Chemotherapy and chest radiotherapy work together to treat lung cancer. Your team will explain the aim, possible benefit and alternatives for you.

Chemotherapy

Cisplatin

Platinum chemotherapy
Damages cancer-cell DNA

Cisplatin damages the DNA that cancer cells need to divide. It can also affect healthy cells.

DNA damage↓Cell repair disrupted↓Cell growth reduced

What to watch for
Report fever, unusual bleeding, reduced urine, hearing changes or tingling.

Sources: nccp · cisplatin-smpc

Chemotherapy

Etoposide

Topoisomerase II chemotherapy
Interferes with DNA repair

Etoposide interferes with an enzyme used to copy and repair DNA. This can stop cancer cells growing and can also lower healthy blood counts.

DNA copying↓Repair interrupted↓Cell growth reduced

What to watch for
Report fever, bleeding, a sore mouth or feeling faint during an infusion.

Sources: nccp · etoposide-smpc

Radiotherapy

Chest radiotherapy

Local radiation treatment
Targets the planned chest area

Radiotherapy directs radiation at the planned chest area. Its appointments and dose are set by the radiation oncology team.

Planned chest area↓Targeted radiation↓Cancer-cell damage

What to watch for
Report painful swallowing, poor intake, skin changes, new cough or breathlessness.

Sources: hse-rt

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Both chemotherapy medicines

Treatment after blood-test and symptom review. Radiotherapy usually begins close to this first treatment. Confirm both treatment plans and take only the prescribed support medicines.

Day 2–5: Etoposide each day

Return for etoposide on each of these four days. Radiotherapy appointments may also be planned. The team checks blood results and symptoms before dosing. Report swallowing or breathing changes.

Day 6–7: No planned chemotherapy

Follow the separate radiotherapy calendar. Anti-sickness medicines may continue as prescribed. Call if unwell; do not wait for the next infusion.

Day 8: Cisplatin only

Return for the second cisplatin dose after safety checks. Extra drip fluids are needed; etoposide is not given today. Confirm today’s anti-sickness plan with the team.

Day 9–28: No further chemotherapy this cycle

Continue any planned radiotherapy appointments and safety checks. Tiredness and swallowing symptoms can build up. Report new symptoms and keep the urgent contact plan available, including after treatment ends.

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Your anti-sickness plan

Cisplatin days need a different plan from etoposide-only days. The team combines prevention and rescue medicines into one clear schedule. Do not repeat or add courses yourself.

Eating, drinking and swallowing

Report painful swallowing, weight loss or difficulty drinking early. The team can arrange pain relief, dietitian support or other help.

Fluids and kidney protection

Follow your individual drinking advice and report much less urine. Cisplatin needs drip fluids before and after dosing; tell staff if you cannot drink or feel swollen or breathless.

Other medicines and prevention

Bring a full medicine list. Ask before starting medicines, supplements or vaccines. Fertility and contraception advice should be agreed before treatment.

Understanding the evidence

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy are used together for selected people with Stage III lung cancer.

Published results help guide discussion but do not predict your outcome. The source study was small and did not compare this regimen with another treatment.

Sources: nccp · trial

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Do I attend every day?

Chemotherapy is planned on Days 1–5 and8 of each cycle. Radiotherapy has a separate appointment calendar; do not use this cycle diagram as the radiation timetable.

How many cycles will I have?

Two cycles are planned. Two further cycles may be offered only after review and a new confirmed plan.

Can side effects continue after treatment?

Yes. Keep reporting new or worsening symptoms after the last infusion or radiation session. The team agrees your follow-up checks.

Try explaining the plan back: Which chemotherapy days will I attend?,Where is my radiation calendar?,Who will I ring if unwell tonight?
