When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Report new breathing difficulty, eye pain or blurred vision, blistering or infected skin, severe diarrhoea or dehydration promptly. Also report new chest pain, fever or a line/pump problem immediately.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

Understand your treatment

How your treatment works

Panitumumab + modified FOLFOX-6 is used for bowel cancer that has spread and has a confirmed wild-type RAS result. This NCCP regimen is a first-line option. Your team explains the aim, alternatives and how benefit will be reviewed.

EGFR-targeted antibody

Panitumumab

Vectibix
Blocks growth messages at the cell surface

Panitumumab attaches to EGFR, a receptor on the outside of cells. This can interrupt signals that help some bowel cancers grow. A tumour RAS test helps identify whether this approach is suitable. EGFR also supports healthy skin, which explains why skin and nail changes need attention.

EGFR receives a growth signal↓Panitumumab blocks the receptor↓Some growth signals are reduced

What to watch for
Report rash, painful cracks or nail changes early. Eye pain or blurred vision, new breathing symptoms, severe diarrhoea, or blistering skin need prompt assessment. Blood tests check minerals such as magnesium.

Sources: smpc · leaflet

Platinum chemotherapy

Oxaliplatin

Eloxatin
Links DNA strands

Oxaliplatin makes links within DNA. These interfere with copying and repair, which can stop a cancer cell dividing. Healthy tissues can also be affected.

Platinum enters cell↓DNA links form↓Copying is disrupted

What to watch for
Cold can trigger tingling or throat discomfort. Tell the team about persistent numbness, difficulty with buttons or walking, or infusion symptoms. New breathing difficulty always needs assessment.

Sources: nccp · patient

Chemotherapy modulator

Folinic acid

Calcium leucovorin
Strengthens fluorouracil action

Folinic acid helps fluorouracil remain bound to an enzyme needed to make DNA. In this regimen it increases fluorouracil activity and can also increase its unwanted effects. It is not a vitamin supplement to start yourself.

Folinic acid↓Stronger enzyme binding↓Fluorouracil effect increases

What to watch for
The combined treatment can increase mouth soreness and diarrhoea. Report these promptly. Rare allergic reactions can occur.

Sources: nccp · patient

Chemotherapy

Fluorouracil

5-FU
Disrupts cell building blocks

Fluorouracil interferes with the production and use of DNA and RNA, the instructions cells need to grow. The infusion extends exposure over approximately two days.

Drug enters cell↓DNA / RNA disrupted↓Growth slows

What to watch for
Diarrhoea, mouth sores and skin soreness may become serious. Chest pain during the pump infusion needs emergency assessment. Unexpected severe early toxicity can occur even after DPD testing.

Sources: nccp · patient

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Infusion and pump connection

The team reviews symptoms, blood tests including minerals and your treatment plan. Panitumumab and the day-ward chemotherapy are followed by connection of the fluorouracil pump. Before leaving, confirm the pump-care instructions, disconnection appointment and contact number.

Day 2–3: The pump is delivering treatment

The infusion runs for about 46 hours from connection; the calendar day does not determine the exact finish time. Follow the instructions for keeping the line dry and secure. Avoid cold exposure if it triggers symptoms. Call for a leaking, disconnected or unexpectedly stopped pump. Do not reconnect, alter settings or disconnect it yourself unless specifically trained and instructed.

Day 4–7: After disconnection

Nausea, diarrhoea, mouth symptoms, fatigue or cold sensitivity may continue. Use prescribed support. New symptoms deserve advice even if the pump has finished. Report poor intake, persistent diarrhoea or mouth pain early. Chest pain or major breathing difficulty requires emergency help.

Day 8–14: Between visits and the next review

Blood counts can fall between visits. Numbness may accumulate over cycles. Describe any difficulty fastening buttons, walking or handling objects before more oxaliplatin. Bring the symptom passport and check the next appointment. Day 1 usually repeats 14 days after this cycle started, only if confirmed.

Your treatment, step by step

Your port and treatment pump

From preparing the line to coming home after disconnection. Your team arranges the appointments and shows you what to do.

1 · Before treatment

Choose and prepare the line

The team explains whether a port or PICC suits your treatment. You receive instructions for the insertion appointment.

2 · Insertion and recovery

A separate appointment may be needed

For a port, numbing medicine is used and a small device is placed under the skin. Ask about sedation, transport and wound care.

3 · Day 1

The nurse connects your pump

After the day-ward medicines, the nurse checks and connects the fluorouracil pump. Ask for a demonstration before leaving.

4 · At home

Treatment continues for about 46 hours

Carry the pump as shown. Keep the dressing dry and protect the line from pulling, heat and damage.

5 · As arranged

Return for disconnection

A trained professional checks the infusion, disconnects the pump and cares for the line. The appointment is based on connection time.

6 · Between cycles

The access device usually stays

A port needle can be removed while the port stays under the skin. A PICC remains in the arm with ongoing dressing and line care.

Port or PICC: what is the difference?

A port is a small chamber beneath the skin, usually on the chest, attached to a tube ending in a large vein. A special needle goes through the skin into the port when it is used. A PICC enters a vein in the upper arm; a short section remains outside under a dressing. Both can deliver a home infusion. Your team helps choose the device and teaches its care.

Before insertion and during recovery

Tell the team about allergies, blood thinners, infections and any other cancer medicines. Follow the instructions about eating, drinking and transport; do not stop medicines yourself. Port placement uses local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation or general anaesthetic. The team explains the procedure and risks, including infection, bleeding, clots and insertion complications. Ask how to manage discomfort, protect the wound and resume activity. Report increasing pain, redness, swelling, discharge or fever.

Connection: what the nurse does

The nurse checks the line and treatment, secures the connection and dressing, and starts the pump. Before leaving, you should have the disconnection location and time, contact numbers including out of hours, and the written spill instructions. Ask to be shown what normal pump progress looks like. You do not need to calculate a rate.

Carrying it, sleeping and washing

Use the supplied carrier and the position recommended for your pump. Keep tubing free from pulling and kinks. Ask how to place the pump safely for sleep and how to protect the line and dressing when washing. Do not immerse it or swim. Avoid direct heat and cold. An elastomeric pump has a balloon that shrinks slowly and may have no alarm; an electronic pump has different instructions. Follow the instructions for the device you actually receive.

Disconnection is not port removal

Keep the appointment even if the pump looks empty, unless the team changes the plan. Staff check the pump and line and arrange safe disposal. After flushing and other line care as required, the port needle may be removed and a small dressing applied. The implanted port stays in place until a separate removal procedure is arranged. A PICC remains in place and needs scheduled dressing and flushing care.

Leakage, disconnection or unexpected pump progress

Call the team immediately if the pump leaks, is damaged, finishes unexpectedly early, appears not to run, or the line disconnects. Follow the emergency or spill steps you have been specifically taught, including use of a clamp if instructed. Do not reconnect, change settings or disconnect the pump yourself. Keep other people away from leaked medicine. If medicine touches skin, rinse it off and wash with soap and water, then seek advice.

Pain, swelling or feeling unwell

Contact oncology immediately for pain or swelling around the line, a wet/loose dressing, redness, fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell. A painful swollen arm or neck needs urgent advice. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty or collapse: call 112 / 999. Do not wait for pump disconnection or the next clinic visit.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Start skin care early

Ask for your skin-care plan before the first infusion. Use the recommended moisturiser and sun protection; your team may prescribe a cream or an antibiotic. Do not use ordinary acne treatments without advice.

Minerals and blood tests

Panitumumab can lower magnesium and other minerals even when you feel well. Blood tests continue during treatment and for about eight weeks after it finishes. Take replacement treatment only as prescribed.

DPD testing before fluorouracil

DPD helps the body break down fluorouracil. Deficiency can cause severe toxicity. Testing guides the plan but cannot exclude all serious reactions; unexpectedly severe early diarrhoea, mouth sores or illness needs immediate review.

Cold precautions

Oxaliplatin can trigger cold-related nerve symptoms. Avoid cold drinks and direct cold exposure if advised. Do not use ice chips during this regimen. Throat tightness or breathing difficulty needs assessment; do not assume it is harmless cold sensitivity.

Living with treatment

Build the plan around what matters to you.

Skin and nails

Ask for your skin-care plan before the first infusion. Use the recommended moisturiser and sun protection; your team may prescribe a cream or an antibiotic. Do not use ordinary acne treatments without advice. Avoid friction on sore skin and tell the team about painful nail folds.

Food, activity and support

Keep fluids and nourishing food within reach. Pace activity around energy levels and ask for dietetic, emotional or practical support. If symptoms prevent eating, drinking or usual tasks, contact the team.

Sources: hse

Pregnancy and fertility

Discuss pregnancy plans and fertility before treatment. Panitumumab requires contraception during treatment and for two months afterwards, and breastfeeding should be avoided for that period. When treatment changes or finishes

Follow-up continues after the last infusion.

Monitoring and recovery

Continue your skin plan and planned magnesium, calcium and potassium checks for about eight weeks after panitumumab. New symptoms still need review. Ask when to stop prescribed supportive medicines.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Why do I need a RAS test?

RAS is part of the growth-signalling pathway inside cancer cells. If it is changed by a mutation, blocking EGFR is unlikely to help. Your team needs a validated wild-type RAS result before panitumumab. Other tumour tests and the treatment aim also affect the choice.

Is this the same as checkpoint immunotherapy?

Panitumumab is a targeted antibody against EGFR. It works differently from checkpoint treatments used in some other cancer regimens. Ask your team which treatment class and side effects apply to you.

Does a rash mean the treatment is working?

A rash is a recognised drug effect, but it cannot tell you whether your cancer is responding. Report it early so it can be treated. Scans, symptoms and clinical review guide the response assessment.

Will I need radiotherapy as part of this cycle?

These panitumumab regimens do not contain a scheduled radiotherapy course. If radiotherapy is recommended separately, your oncology teams will explain its purpose and timing. Do not assume it occurs on infusion or pump days.

What about pregnancy, breastfeeding or fertility?

Discuss pregnancy plans and fertility before treatment. Panitumumab requires contraception during treatment and for two months afterwards, and breastfeeding should be avoided for that period. When chemotherapy is also included, its precautions may last longer; ask for the instructions for the whole regimen.

Does having more side effects mean treatment is working?

No. Side effects do not reliably show whether the cancer is responding. Report them so they can be treated. Your team assesses benefit using the tests and review plan appropriate to your cancer.

Why might a dose be delayed or reduced?

Your team may allow recovery, investigate a symptom or reduce the chance of lasting harm. Ask which drug changes, why, and when review is planned. A delay does not itself mean treatment has failed.

What should I say when calling?

Give the regimen name, last treatment date, symptoms and when they started. Explain whether you can eat, drink, pass urine and manage usual activities. Give your temperature if available, but do not delay calling to collect details.

Try explaining the plan back: Can you explain which medicines you are receiving, the schedule, the contact plan and the symptoms that need prompt help?
