When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Report new breathlessness, chest symptoms, severe diarrhoea, inability to drink, jaundice, a painful swollen leg or a severe rash promptly. You do not need to identify which medicine caused the symptom.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

Medicines in the active phase

Treat the cancer before surgery as part of a plan intended to reduce the risk of it returning.

Platinum chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Damages deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA) inside cells

Carboplatin creates links in DNA, making it harder for cells to copy and repair their genetic instructions. Its dose depends on kidney function as well as the intended regimen.

Platinum enters cell↓DNA links form↓Copying is disrupted

What to watch for
Keep blood-test appointments. Fever, unusual bleeding, repeated vomiting or infusion symptoms need prompt advice.

Sources: nccp

Taxane chemotherapy

Paclitaxel

Taxol (conventional paclitaxel)
Locks the cell division machinery

Cells use tiny structures called microtubules to separate their chromosomes when dividing. Paclitaxel stabilises these structures so they cannot rearrange normally, interrupting cell division. It can also affect nerves and other healthy tissues.

Microtubules form↓Structures remain locked↓Division is interrupted

What to watch for
Report tingling or numbness before the next weekly dose, especially if it affects walking or hand use. Tell the nurse immediately about flushing, chest or throat tightness, itching, dizziness or breathing changes during infusion.

Sources: nccp

Anthracycline chemotherapy

Doxorubicin

Interferes with DNA handling

Doxorubicin interferes with enzymes and DNA processes needed for cell division. It can also affect heart muscle, so the team considers previous exposure and cardiac risk.

DNA handling disrupted↓Cell damage↓Division reduced

What to watch for
Report pain or burning at the infusion site immediately. New breathlessness or chest symptoms need prompt review. Red urine for a short time can be a drug-colour effect; pain, clots or persistent bleeding still need advice.

Sources: nccp

Alkylating chemotherapy

Cyclophosphamide

Damages DNA needed for growth

Cyclophosphamide is activated in the body and damages DNA. This helps stop dividing cancer cells but can also affect blood-cell production and reproductive tissues.

Drug activated↓DNA damage↓Cell division affected

What to watch for
Use the prescribed anti-sickness and hydration plan. Report fever, painful urination or blood in the urine.

Practical treatment guide

Your practical treatment plan

Four 21-day cycles: paclitaxel on Days 1, 8 and 15; carboplatin AUC 2 on Days 1, 8 and 15. Four further cycles (cycles 5–8): doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide on Day 1 every 14 days. G-CSF support is required for every dose-dense AC cycle; the team prescribes its product and timing separately. Surgery and pathology review follow chemotherapy. Further systemic treatment, radiotherapy and follow-up depend on the findings and the MDT plan; no routine postoperative immunotherapy is specified by this regimen.

STEP 1

Before the first visit

Blood tests, ECG and cardiac review, medicines review, fertility discussion where relevant and the consent conversation come first.

STEP 2

Plan venous access

A cannula, peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) or implanted port may be used. If a port is chosen, a trained team inserts it in a separate procedure with local anaesthetic and sometimes sedation; the team explains fasting, blood-thinner and wound instructions.

STEP 3

Infusion day

Trained staff check and access the line, give the prescribed premedicines and infusions, and monitor for reactions. Tell the nurse immediately about pain, burning, rash or breathlessness.

STEP 4

Home and next review

This regimen does not routinely need a take-home infusion pump. Staff disconnect the infusion; a port is de-accessed when appropriate. Keep line-care and next-visit instructions.

Port insertion and removal

The line team explains placement, dressing care and when normal activities can resume. A port is accessed with a special needle during treatment. Removal of the implanted device is a separate planned procedure when no longer needed; do not remove or adjust it yourself.

PICC care and urgent line symptoms

A PICC usually needs regular dressing and flushing appointments even between treatments. Fever, redness, swelling, pain, leakage or damage requires prompt advice. Your hospital supplies the exact care instructions.

Visit time and transport

Visits include assessment, pharmacy preparation, premedicines, infusions and observation. The first visit and a change in phase can take longer. Ask about transport and any escort needed after sedation or line insertion.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Separate anti-sickness plans

The platinum/taxane phase and AC need separate prescriptions. Carboplatin/paclitaxel is moderate emetic risk on each weekly treatment day. AC uses its own high-risk plan.

Growth-factor support

G-CSF is prescribed for every dose-dense AC cycle. Ask for the exact injection days and instructions.

Checks before the next dose

FBC, renal and liver tests weekly throughout chemotherapy, including review before each AC dose. Report new symptoms before the next dose.

After chemotherapy

Surgery and pathology review follow chemotherapy. Further systemic treatment, radiotherapy and follow-up depend on the findings and the MDT plan; no routine postoperative immunotherapy is specified by this regimen.

Treatment reference · 01

Why might my team recommend this treatment?

This is a chemotherapy pathway for triple-negative breast cancer before surgery. Different chemotherapy medicines attack cancer cells in different ways. The team considers cancer extent, previous treatment, kidney and heart health, fitness and whether a chemotherapy-only approach fits the individual plan. This card does not decide whether immunotherapy should be added.

Your treatment aim

Treat the cancer before surgery as part of a plan intended to reduce the risk of it returning.

Sources: nccp

Why more than one phase?

Four 21-day cycles: paclitaxel on Days 1, 8 and 15; carboplatin AUC 2 on Days 1, 8 and 15. Four further cycles (cycles 5–8): doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide on Day 1 every 14 days. G-CSF support is required for every dose-dense AC cycle; the team prescribes its product and timing separately.

Sources: nccp

What determines the next step?

Surgery and pathology review follow chemotherapy. Further systemic treatment, radiotherapy and follow-up depend on the findings and the MDT plan; no routine postoperative immunotherapy is specified by this regimen.

Treatment reference · 03

Surgery, radiotherapy and follow-up

Other treatments depend on the complete diagnosis and pathology.

How the treatments fit

Chemotherapy comes before surgery. The surgeon discusses lumpectomy or mastectomy as alternatives according to your situation. Radiotherapy, if indicated, follows surgery and recovery; timing is agreed by both teams. It is not automatically given at the same time as these chemotherapy phases.

Sources: early · neo

Hormone tablets and bone scans

This is a triple-negative pathway: endocrine therapy and endocrine-related dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (bone density) (DEXA) are not automatically part of it. Bone-density testing may still be appropriate for other personal risk factors.

Sources: early

If symptoms or tests change

The team may pause, change or stop a medicine, reassess imaging or revise the surgical plan. Report concerns promptly.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

When should I call the oncology team?

Call immediately for fever, shivers, sudden illness, new breathing difficulty or concerning symptoms. Do not wait for a scheduled blood test or appointment.

Why is my chemotherapy changing halfway through?

The planned course uses a platinum/taxane phase followed by AC, which works in a different way. The change is part of the pathway and does not by itself mean the first medicines failed.

Will I need to enter dates here?

No. The maps explain the sequence. Your clinical team provides actual appointment dates.

When are ECGs, echoes and blood tests done?

Baseline ECG and cardiac history/risk assessment. NCCP lists baseline echo/MUGA when clinically indicated. Recheck the cardiac assessment before starting doxorubicin; echo/MUGA during treatment is driven by symptoms, risk or the agreed cardio-oncology plan. There is no automatic three-monthly echo schedule for every person on this pathway. FBC, renal and liver tests weekly throughout chemotherapy, including review before each AC dose.

Where does radiotherapy fit?

Chemotherapy comes before surgery. The surgeon discusses lumpectomy or mastectomy as alternatives according to your situation. Radiotherapy, if indicated, follows surgery and recovery; timing is agreed by both teams. It is not automatically given at the same time as these chemotherapy phases.
