Understand your treatment

Targeted radiation and kidney protection

PRRT carries radiation to somatostatin receptors on NET cells to help control disease that cannot be removed or has spread. A specialist NET and nuclear-medicine team checks receptor imaging, organ function and your priorities before recommending it.

Receptor-targeted radiopharmaceutical

Lutetium-177 oxodotreotide

Lutathera / 177Lu-DOTATATE / PRRT
Carries radiation to somatostatin receptor targets

Lutathera joins a receptor-binding molecule to the radioactive element lutetium-177. After infusion, it attaches to somatostatin receptors on suitable NET cells and delivers radiation nearby. The receptor scan helps select patients. Kidneys and blood-forming marrow can also receive radiation, which is why kidney protection and long-term blood monitoring matter.

What to watch for
Keep all between-treatment and after-treatment blood tests; use your personalised radiation-safety and hormone-symptom plans.

Supportive infusion

Lysine / arginine amino acids

Kidney-protective infusion
Reduces kidney uptake of radioactivity

The amino-acid infusion reduces the amount of radioactive medicine retained in the kidneys. It is an essential part of kidney protection. It can cause nausea and affect potassium or fluid balance, so anti-sickness treatment, blood tests and observation are needed.

What to watch for
Tell staff promptly about nausea, palpitations, weakness, breathlessness or swelling during infusion.

Complete timing and symptom notes

Day 1–2: Treatment and early observation

Plan a long hospital visit; the first treatment generally involves an overnight stay. Staff give anti-sickness medicine and kidney-protective amino acids. Hormone reactions are especially important in the first 48 hours. Before discharge, obtain written emergency contacts, SSA timing and radiation precautions.

Day 3–21: Recovery and individual precautions

Energy, appetite and bowel symptoms may change. Radiation precautions may last two to three weeks for some activities or contacts; exact restrictions come from the medical physicist. Attend blood tests every two weeks. Call for fever, bleeding, reduced urine, severe pain or sudden hormone symptoms.

Day 22–56: Tests and next-treatment decision

Continue fortnightly bloods and attend the PRRT review. A post-infusion scan checks radiation distribution; response scans follow a separate specialist plan. The team confirms whether and when the next administration is suitable. Do not treat an eight-week date as automatic clearance.

Practical treatment guide

Practical treatment planning

Agree the arrangements with your oncology team.

STEP 1

Before admission

Confirm SSTR imaging, appointments, recent bloods, hormone medicines and pregnancy precautions. Tell the team about kidney/heart problems, incontinence, a catheter, childcare or close contact with pregnant people; these may change the practical plan.

STEP 2

Treatment day

The standard NCCP activity is 7.4 GBq by intravenous infusion, usually every eight weeks for four administrations; the specialist may alter, delay or stop treatment. Lutathera runs over approximately 30–40 minutes. A lysine/arginine amino-acid infusion starts 30 minutes beforehand and continues for four hours. An anti-sickness medicine is given at least 30 minutes before the amino acids. The first visit generally includes an overnight stay; later treatments may be day case or overnight. The service confirms your plan.

STEP 3

SSA injections: stop and restart

The NET and nuclear-medicine teams must coordinate the injection plan. Do not translate “about four weeks” into your own date: NCCP specifies 30 days for long-acting SSA and 24 hours for short-acting SSA before PRRT. Restart timing follows the written service plan; it can differ with day-case arrangements.

STEP 4

At home after treatment

Before discharge, obtain a paper plan with the end date for each restriction on contact, sleeping, work, travel and body-fluid handling. First-week instructions include daily bathing, careful handwashing, sitting to pass urine and separately washing body-fluid-soiled laundry. Ask for specific advice about pads, incontinence or a catheter. Examples include sleeping separately from a partner for 14 days and from pregnant people or children for three weeks; restrictions differ by contact and circumstance. Your medical physicist sets your personal instructions. Tell them before discharge if you cannot follow the plan at home.

STEP 5

Travel and other healthcare

Carry the discharge letter and the official radiation-treatment card for the period the medical physicist specifies. They identify the treatment and date and help with travel, radiation detectors and other healthcare. The SACTCheck wallet card is an additional record and does not replace them. Tell healthcare staff about the treatment; radiation precautions must never delay urgent care.

STEP 6

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and fertility

Lutathera can harm an unborn child and may affect fertility. Discuss fertility preservation before treatment. Use effective contraception during treatment and for seven months after the last dose for patients who could become pregnant; men with partners who could become pregnant need contraception during treatment and for four months afterwards. Breastfeeding must stop before treatment; follow specialist advice.

STEP 7

If a visit is delayed

Low blood counts, kidney or liver changes, other side effects or a change in the cancer may lead the specialist to postpone, reduce or stop treatment. A four-stamp record shows planned administrations, not medical clearance. A dose does not automatically become suitable at the maximum interval. Keep the blood-test and symptom plan while waiting for the team’s decision.

STEP 8

Contacts and paperwork

Write the actual NET, PRRT nursing and radiation-protection contacts on the paper plan. Example numbers are not clinical contact lines. Keep the written precautions and urgent hormone-symptom instructions with the discharge letter.

STEP 9

Your specialist team’s scan appointments

Radiation-assessment scans measure where the treatment went and the radiation delivered to organs. They answer a different question from CT or MRI scans of tumour response. The detailed treatment information describes three radiation scans on separate days in the first week after the first infusion, starting the next day, and one after later infusions; response CT or MRI is described between the second and third infusions and after the course. Confirm your actual bookings with the team: these are service arrangements, not a universal timetable for every neuroendocrine tumour.

STEP 10

Extra blood tests after the course

Blood counts, kidney and liver tests continue every four weeks for at least three months after the last administration, then every three months for the following year. Your written discharge plan may also include two-weekly tests for the first eight weeks after the last infusion. The team confirms those extra early appointments and who reviews the results.

STEP 11

Paper radiation instructions for home

Before discharge, obtain a paper copy with the exact end date for each restriction. The treatment information includes daily bathing, careful handwashing, sitting to pass urine and separate washing of body-fluid-soiled laundry in the first week. Ask for specific handling instructions for pads, incontinence or a catheter. Sleeping, travel, work and contact limits differ: examples include separate beds from a partner for 14 days and sleeping apart from pregnant people or children for three weeks. Your medical physicist’s personal instructions take precedence.

STEP 12

Carry the official radiation card too

The medical physicist supplies a radiation-treatment card to carry for the time stated. The SACTCheck wallet card is an additional treatment record and does not replace that official card, the discharge letter or the radiation-safety plan.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Kidney protection

A lysine/arginine infusion is required for kidney protection and begins before Lutathera. Follow the service’s fluid and toileting advice. Tell staff about heart or kidney problems; do not use a generic fluid target.

Nausea prevention

PRRT has moderate emetogenic potential. A 5-HT3 anti-sickness medicine is given at least 30 minutes before the amino-acid infusion, with additional treatment if needed. Tell staff promptly if nausea starts.

Somatostatin analogue coordination

Long-acting SSA injections can interfere with receptor targeting. NCCP uses a 30-day interval before PRRT and short-acting injections are withheld for 24 hours. The referring NET consultant decides your exact stop and restart plan, balancing hormone control. Never stop injections on your own.

Radiation safety and practical planning

The medical physicist gives individual written and verbal advice. Contact with young children or pregnant people may need restriction for up to three weeks, and close-contact work may require up to two weeks away. Arrange transport, sleeping, toileting, laundry and caring responsibilities before admission.

Treatment reference · 01

Your PRRT course

Receptor-directed radiation with specialist safety checks.

Why PRRT?

PRRT carries radiation to somatostatin receptors on NET cells to help control disease that cannot be removed or has spread. A specialist NET and nuclear-medicine team checks receptor imaging, organ function and your priorities before recommending it.

How it works

Lutathera joins a receptor-binding molecule to the radioactive element lutetium-177. After infusion, it attaches to somatostatin receptors on suitable NET cells and delivers radiation nearby. The receptor scan helps select patients. Kidneys and blood-forming marrow can also receive radiation, which is why kidney protection and long-term blood monitoring matter.

Why the amino acids matter

The amino-acid infusion reduces the amount of radioactive medicine retained in the kidneys. It is an essential part of kidney protection. It can cause nausea and affect potassium or fluid balance, so anti-sickness treatment, blood tests and observation are needed.

Four intended administrations

Four administrations are planned, usually eight weeks apart. Lutathera is infused over about 30–40 minutes, with a four-hour kidney-protective amino-acid infusion and anti-sickness treatment. The visit is much longer than the Lutathera infusion; an overnight stay may be needed.

Evidence and its limits

Lutetium-177 DOTATATE with standard-dose octreotide improved time before progression compared with high-dose octreotide. The final overall-survival comparison was not statistically significant. The findings do not promise a survival gain for an individual. NETTER-1 was a midgut study. Its results must not be presented as identical evidence for every pancreatic, other gastrointestinal (GI) or grade 3 NET. Your specialist team confirms that the treatment criteria apply to you.

Treatment reference · 03

Blood tests, scans and follow-up

Tests continue between treatments and after the course.

The full safety-test sequence

Baseline blood count, kidneys/liver, ECHO/MUGA and pregnancy assessment where applicable. Counts/kidneys/liver once 2–4 weeks before and shortly before each infusion, every two weeks between, then every four weeks for at least three months after the final dose and every three months for the following year. Potassium is checked before amino acids. Renal monitoring continues at least through the first year. Post-infusion radiation scans and response scans have separate purposes.

What may change treatment

The specialist may postpone, reduce or stop treatment for low counts, kidney/liver abnormalities, other toxicity or disease change. A four-stamp record shows intended administrations; it cannot confirm medical suitability.

Post-treatment scans

Your specialist team plans special scans after each infusion to measure radiation delivered to organs. CT/MRI or other scans to assess tumour response follow the specialist plan.

Delayed effects

Kidney injury and rare bone-marrow disorders, including myelodysplastic syndrome or acute leukaemia, can occur later. Persistent tiredness, infections, bruising or bleeding needs review even after the treatment course.

Treatment reference · 06

Understanding your particular NET

Several different features help the team choose treatment.

Where it started and where it is now

A neuroendocrine tumour begins in cells with hormone-making features. Its starting organ and the places it has spread are separate parts of the diagnosis. A pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour differs from pancreatic adenocarcinoma; a deposit in the liver may have come from another organ.

Differentiation, grade and Ki-67

The pathology report describes how organised the cells look (differentiation) and their growth activity (grade). Ki-67 measures cells showing a growth-related protein in the sample. Its percentage describes that sample, not the amount of your body affected or your chance of survival.

Grade is different from stage

Stage describes extent; grade describes tumour biology. Either can vary independently. A well-differentiated grade 3 neuroendocrine tumour and a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma are distinct diagnoses and may need different treatments.

Functioning and nonfunctioning

A functioning tumour produces a hormone syndrome. A nonfunctioning tumour can still cause problems through growth or pressure. Somatostatin receptor uptake on a scan shows a treatment target; it does not, on its own, identify a hormone syndrome.

Your diagnosis record

Ask for one diagnosis summary: starting organ, grade, differentiation, Ki-67 result if reported, sites involved, hormone syndrome and receptor-imaging result. Keep pending results clearly marked.

Treatment reference · 07

Making sense of tests and results

Safety tests, tumour tests and hormone tests answer different questions.

Blood tests for treatment safety

Blood counts assess oxygen-carrying cells, infection-fighting cells and platelets. Kidney and liver results help assess treatment safety. These checks are needed even when you feel well; a reassuring scan answers a different question.

CT, MRI and receptor imaging

CT and MRI show anatomy and tumour size. Somatostatin receptor imaging tests whether a particular receptor target is present. An fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scan measures uptake of a glucose-like tracer and is used selectively. Your team chooses the scans that can guide your care.

Hormones and tumour markers are individual

Selected markers may help answer a hormone or tumour question. Examples include 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) for serotonin-related symptoms, chromogranin A, or a specific pancreatic hormone. They are not a routine panel for every neuroendocrine tumour. Interpret changes with symptoms and imaging.

Preparing for a test

Use the laboratory instructions for fasting, food restrictions and any timed urine collection. Some medicines affect marker results, including acid-suppressing medicines and chromogranin A. Ask the team what to do; do not stop a prescribed medicine yourself.

Heart and inherited-risk checks

Some hormone syndromes can affect heart valves, so an ultrasound of the heart may be requested. Inherited-risk assessment depends on the tumour and family history; multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 is one example. An inherited-risk test and a test of tumour tissue answer different questions.

Who does what, and when?

For each test, write its name, purpose, booking date and result-review contact. Ask the team to set your next scan or marker date. A schedule for someone after surgery may not apply during your treatment.

Treatment reference · 08

Hormone symptoms and day-to-day changes

A new symptom may come from the NET, treatment or another illness.

Build a picture of what is normal for you

Keep a brief record of your usual bowel pattern, flushing, appetite, weight, pain and energy. Note new changes and when they started. This helps the team assess whether treatment, hormones or another problem needs attention.

Hormone-related symptoms

Hormone symptoms depend on the tumour: examples include flushing and watery diarrhoea, acid-related symptoms, or changes in blood sugar. An insulin-producing tumour can lower blood sugar. Ask which symptoms apply to you and whether you need a written rescue plan.

Hormone treatment is a separate part of the plan

Hormone-control medicines have their own schedule. A pause in anticancer tablets does not automatically mean pausing those medicines. Before peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, the specialist team coordinates any interruption and restart of somatostatin analogue injections.

When a change is urgent

Follow your oncology emergency plan for sudden or severe symptoms. Collapse, severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, confusion or uncontrolled bleeding needs emergency assessment. Severe diarrhoea, persistent vomiting, dehydration, fever or sudden illness needs urgent oncology advice. Do not wait for a scan, an online answer or the next clinic visit.

Procedures and hormone syndromes

Tell teams arranging an operation, anaesthetic or other procedure about a hormone-producing tumour or previous hormone crisis. Your specialist decides whether extra preparation is needed. Carry the agreed emergency instructions and current medicine list.

Treatment reference · 09

Your overall plan and choices

Your priorities belong in the treatment plan.

What are we trying to achieve?

Agree your treatment aim: slowing growth, reducing tumour size, controlling hormone symptoms or preserving daily function. A scan showing no growth may be a useful result even when the tumour has not shrunk.

How treatments fit together

Your plan may use different treatments at different times, including surgery, medicines, receptor-targeted radiation or treatment directed at the liver. Ask the team to mark your own sequence and who coordinates it.

Choosing between options

Ask why the proposed option fits the tumour site, grade, receptor findings, symptoms and your health. Discuss reasonable alternatives, observation when appropriate and suitable trials. Your team explains which options are available to you.

Evidence is about groups

Studies measure different outcomes, such as shrinkage, time before worsening and survival. Ask which outcome matters for your plan and how closely the study group matches your diagnosis. No study average can predict one person’s course.

Review points and shared decisions

Agree when to review the plan and what could lead to continuing, pausing or changing treatment. Include the effects on work, caring duties, travel and the activities that matter to you.

Treatment reference · 10

Eating, energy, practical help and support

Supportive care can be used alongside treatment from the start.

Food, weight and digestion

Report weight loss, persistent diarrhoea, greasy stools or difficulty eating. Different causes need different treatment. Ask for a dietitian and whether reduced digestive-enzyme function needs assessment.

Fluids and safe activity

Use your personal fluid advice, especially with diarrhoea or heart or kidney disease. Adjust activity and rest to your energy. Report dizziness, breathlessness or fatigue that limits daily tasks so treatable causes can be checked.

Emotions, relationships and quality of life

Ask for help with mood, sleep, relationships, intimacy or caring responsibilities when you need it. Symptom and palliative-care teams can support comfort and quality of life while anticancer treatment continues.

Practical help

Ask your specialist nurse or social worker about transport, employment, finances and help at home. Tell them about caring responsibilities and ask which support organisations fit your needs.

A record that works without a phone

Keep a paper schedule, current medicine list and real contact numbers together. The handbook can be read without internet access. Use the written emergency plan whenever you need urgent help.

Questions to take to the next visit

For the next visit, choose the questions that matter most: my exact diagnosis; the aim of treatment; which tests I need and why; who explains results; when to call; and what help is available.

What NETTER-1 did and did not show

The trial studied progressive SSTR-positive midgut NET after octreotide treatment.

Lutetium-177 DOTATATE with standard-dose octreotide improved time before progression compared with high-dose octreotide.

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

Does my receptor scan and exact NET diagnosis meet this PRRT indication?

The specialist team must confirm progressive, well-differentiated G1/G2 SSTR-positive gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (GEP-NET) and assess other eligibility factors. A receptor scan alone does not establish that treatment is suitable.

Will the first or later visits require overnight admission?

The treatment information describes an overnight stay for the first treatment; later treatments may be day case or overnight. Confirm the actual admission and discharge plan with the PRRT service.

Who provides the written stop/restart dates for my SSA injections?

The referring NET consultant and nuclear-medicine team coordinate the injection plan. Get exact written dates; do not stop or restart an SSA on your own.

When are my fortnightly and post-course blood tests?

NCCP specifies two-weekly bloods between administrations and continued tests after the course. Your specialist team may arrange additional early post-course checks. Ask for one reconciled written schedule and result-review contact.

Which household, work and travel precautions apply, and on what dates do they end?

The medical physicist gives individual restrictions, which can extend to three weeks for some contacts. Obtain each end date and discuss transport, sleeping, childcare and work before admission.

What symptoms suggest a hormone crisis, and who do I call after discharge?

Sudden severe flushing, faintness, diarrhoea, wheeze or breathing trouble can require urgent assessment, particularly in the first 48 hours. Follow the service’s written emergency plan and do not delay urgent care because of radiation precautions.

What fertility, contraception and long-term follow-up plan do I need?

Discuss fertility before starting. Current EMA/HSE information requires contraception during treatment and for seven months afterwards for patients who could become pregnant, and four months for men with partners who could become pregnant. Blood and organ-function follow-up continues after the fourth administration.

Try explaining the plan back: Can you explain the four planned visits, your blood-test timetable, the separate SSA injection plan, radiation precautions and who to call for sudden hormone symptoms?
