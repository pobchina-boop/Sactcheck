When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call oncology immediately for fever, shivering, sudden illness, persistent vomiting, diarrhoea, severe mouth pain or inability to drink. Chest pain during or after fluorouracil, or unusually early severe symptoms, needs urgent assessment even if dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (a medicine-processing enzyme) (DPD) testing was normal. Severe chest pain, severe breathing difficulty, collapse or a seizure is an emergency.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

How your medicines work

This chemotherapy is an additional treatment after chemoradiotherapy for nasopharyngeal cancer. It aims to treat cancer cells that may remain. The team explains the expected benefit and burden for you, including recovery from the earlier treatment.

Platinum chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Carboplatin
Damages cancer-cell deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA)

Carboplatin interferes with DNA repair and cell division. Its dose is calculated from kidney function. Blood counts can fall, including platelets that help stop bleeding. It is a different prescription from cisplatin.

DNA damage↓Cell division disrupted↓Cancer growth reduced

What to watch for
Report fever, unusual bruising or bleeding, marked tiredness or an infusion reaction.

Sources: nccp

Antimetabolite chemotherapy

Fluorouracil

5-FU
Disrupts production of DNA and RNA

Fluorouracil, often shortened to 5-FU, interferes with the building blocks used to make DNA and RNA, which cells need to grow. It runs through a vein over the prescribed hours. Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) is an enzyme that breaks it down. Testing for DPD deficiency before treatment reduces risk but cannot rule out every severe reaction.

Cell building blocks disrupted↓Cells cannot divide normally↓Pump provides prescribed exposure

What to watch for
Report severe diarrhoea, mouth sores, chest pain, sudden confusion, sore palms or soles, or unexpectedly early severe symptoms.

Tests in your plan

Full blood count

WhenAt baseline and before each cycle

Checks infection-fighting cells, oxygen-carrying cells and platelets.

Kidney and liver tests

WhenAt baseline and before each cycle

Guide safe treatment and identify organ problems.

DPD assessment

WhenBefore the first fluorouracil treatment unless already tested

Checks for reduced ability to break down fluorouracil; results affect treatment choice or dose. Testing cannot exclude every severe reaction. Kidney impairment can affect one blood-based test, so the team chooses the appropriate method.

Kidney filtration assessment

WhenBefore treatment; reassessment when kidney function or the clinical situation changes

Carboplatin dose is calculated from kidney filtration. A measured test is preferred where feasible; the team chooses a valid method and reassesses significant changes.

Hearing assessment

If part of your plan
WhenAt baseline when clinically indicated; review new hearing or balance symptoms during treatment

Investigates hearing loss or tinnitus and helps judge platinum-related risk.

Imaging and tumour-specific tests

If part of your plan
WhenAs individually arranged for the disease and symptoms; no universal marker panel or imaging interval

Assesses the cancer rather than whether a treatment-day blood count is safe.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1–4: Treatment and infusion days

Carboplatin is infused over 30 minutes on Day 1. Its dose is calculated from kidney function using a target exposure called AUC 5. Fluorouracil 1,000 mg/m² per day is given as a continuous 96-hour infusion, or as the source-listed daily 22-hour option on Days 1–4. Your unit confirms the actual prescription, pump changes and disconnection time. Confirm each treatment, pump change and final disconnection time. Report symptoms before the next dose.

Day 5–28: Recovery and review

Symptoms may appear or continue between visits. Blood tests and review determine whether the next cycle can go ahead. Follow the written home plan; contact the team promptly if unwell.

Your treatment, step by step

Your port and treatment pump

From preparing the line to coming home after disconnection. Your team arranges the appointments and shows you what to do.

1 · Before treatment

Confirm access and training

Your team selects the access device and teaches its care, including a port or peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) if used.

2 · Before Day 1

Write down the infusion plan

Continuous fluorouracil over 96 hours, or the prescribed daily 22-hour option on Days 1–4; confirm actual pump visits.

3 · Treatment days

Staff connect and change infusions

Only trained staff connect, program or change the infusion. Do not calculate or alter the rate yourself.

4 · While connected

Watch the line and device

Call for leakage, pain, swelling, damage, disconnection, unexpected completion or a pump that appears not to run. Follow the unit’s device-specific emergency instructions.

5 · At prescribed completion

Attend for disconnection

Completion depends on the actual start time and chosen infusion duration. Do not disconnect or reconnect the line yourself.

6 · Between cycles

Keep the access-care plan

A line may remain between treatments. Keep dressing, flushing and review appointments and report redness, pain, fever or swelling.

Port or PICC: what is the difference?

A port is a small chamber beneath the skin, usually on the chest, attached to a tube ending in a large vein. A special needle goes through the skin into the port when it is used. A PICC enters a vein in the upper arm; a short section remains outside under a dressing. Both can deliver a home infusion. Your team helps choose the device and teaches its care.

Before insertion and during recovery

Tell the team about allergies, blood thinners, infections and any other cancer medicines. Follow the instructions about eating, drinking and transport; do not stop medicines yourself. Port placement uses local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation or general anaesthetic. The team explains the procedure and risks, including infection, bleeding, clots and insertion complications. Ask how to manage discomfort, protect the wound and resume activity. Report increasing pain, redness, swelling, discharge or fever.

Connection: what the nurse does

The nurse checks the line and treatment, secures the connection and dressing, and starts the pump. Before leaving, you should have the disconnection location and time, contact numbers including out of hours, and the written spill instructions. Ask to be shown what normal pump progress looks like. You do not need to calculate a rate.

Carrying it, sleeping and washing

Use the supplied carrier and the position recommended for your pump. Keep tubing free from pulling and kinks. Ask how to place the pump safely for sleep and how to protect the line and dressing when washing. Do not immerse it or swim. Avoid direct heat and cold. An elastomeric pump has a balloon that shrinks slowly and may have no alarm; an electronic pump has different instructions. Follow the instructions for the device you actually receive.

Disconnection is not port removal

Keep the appointment even if the pump looks empty, unless the team changes the plan. Staff check the pump and line and arrange safe disposal. After flushing and other line care as required, the port needle may be removed and a small dressing applied. The implanted port stays in place until a separate removal procedure is arranged. A PICC remains in place and needs scheduled dressing and flushing care.

Leakage, disconnection or unexpected pump progress

Call the team immediately if the pump leaks, is damaged, finishes unexpectedly early, appears not to run, or the line disconnects. Follow the emergency or spill steps you have been specifically taught, including use of a clamp if instructed. Do not reconnect, change settings or disconnect the pump yourself. Keep other people away from leaked medicine. If medicine touches skin, rinse it off and wash with soap and water, then seek advice.

Pain, swelling or feeling unwell

Contact oncology immediately for pain or swelling around the line, a wet/loose dressing, redness, fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell. A painful swollen arm or neck needs urgent advice. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty or collapse: call 112 / 999. Do not wait for pump disconnection or the next clinic visit.

The drawings show general steps, not instructions for inserting or accessing a line. Your hospital confirms its own arrangements and the care instructions for your device.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

One written support-medicine plan

Ask for one list separating prevention medicines, rescue treatment and regular medicines. The schedule must match this treatment and visit. Record what you take and whether it helps; do not double a dose, restart an old prescription or add medicines without checking.

Preventing sickness

This platinum-containing combination needs an agreed anti-sickness plan. Take prevention medicines at the stated times and keep rescue instructions available. Contact the team for persistent vomiting or inability to drink.

Food, fluids and weight

Tell the team early about reduced intake or weight loss. A dietitian can adapt food texture, supplements and intake goals. Follow an individual fluid plan, especially with kidney or heart problems.

Mouth and swallowing care

Follow the mouth-care and pain-relief plan. Report ulcers, thrush or swallowing difficulty early. Ask for a swallowing assessment if food or drinks make you cough or choke; do not assume a general food texture is safe for you.

Fertility, pregnancy and medicine checks

Before treatment, discuss fertility, contraception, pregnancy and breastfeeding, including medicine-specific precautions after treatment. Bring all prescription, over-the-counter and herbal medicines to review. The team must check interactions and allergies.

Other treatment and off-label use

NCCP identifies carboplatin in this nasopharyngeal indication as an off-label use. Discuss why it is being offered, the evidence, alternatives and uncertainties, and document consent under local policy. The earlier carboplatin-radiotherapy course is a separate completed stage. This adjuvant course begins only after recovery and confirmation of the next plan.

Weight and intake

Understanding the evidence

The NCCP regimen brings together evidence and specialist guidance for this particular treatment setting.

Evidence for a whole chemoradiotherapy-and-adjuvant strategy cannot isolate the benefit of this adjuvant component. This carboplatin pathway is used when cisplatin is unsuitable; it should not inherit a survival figure from a different cancer setting.
