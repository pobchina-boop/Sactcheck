Progression preventing definitive surgery or unacceptable pembrolizumab toxicity changes this plan; postoperative treatment is not automatic.

How your medicines work

Immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Releases an immune brake
Immune cell→Brake blocked→Cancer cell

PD-1 is a control switch on T cells, a type of immune cell. When it connects with PD-L1 or PD-L2, the T cell receives a signal to reduce its activity. Some cancers use this pathway to escape immune attack. Pembrolizumab is an antibody that blocks PD-1, helping T cells recognise and attack the cancer. It does not directly poison cancer cells like chemotherapy. Releasing this brake can also allow immune cells to attack healthy organs, which explains the different side-effect pattern and why symptoms can appear after treatment stops.

The immune system can also inflame healthy organs. New cough, diarrhoea, unusual weakness, severe headache, thirst or a spreading rash need prompt advice. Effects can start during treatment or after it finishes.

Platinum chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Damages DNA inside cells
Platinum enters cell→DNA links form→Copying is disrupted

Carboplatin creates links in DNA, making it harder for cells to copy and repair their genetic instructions. Its dose depends on kidney function as well as the intended regimen.

Keep blood-test appointments. Fever, unusual bleeding, repeated vomiting or infusion symptoms need prompt advice.

Taxane chemotherapy

Paclitaxel

Locks the cell division machinery
Microtubules form→Structures remain locked→Division is interrupted

Cells use tiny structures called microtubules to separate their chromosomes when dividing. Paclitaxel stabilises these structures so they cannot rearrange normally, interrupting cell division. It can also affect nerves and other healthy tissues.

Report tingling or numbness before the next weekly dose, especially if it affects walking or hand use. Tell the nurse immediately about flushing, chest or throat tightness, itching, dizziness or breathing changes during infusion.

Anthracycline chemotherapy

Doxorubicin

Interferes with DNA handling
DNA handling disrupted→Cell damage→Division reduced

Doxorubicin interferes with enzymes and DNA processes needed for cell division. It can also affect heart muscle, so the team considers previous exposure and cardiac risk.

Report pain or burning at the infusion site immediately. New breathlessness or chest symptoms need prompt review. Red urine for a short time can be a drug-colour effect; pain, clots or persistent bleeding still need advice.

Alkylating chemotherapy

Cyclophosphamide

Damages DNA needed for growth
Drug activated→DNA damage→Cell division affected

Cyclophosphamide is activated in the body and damages DNA. This helps stop dividing cancer cells but can also affect blood-cell production and reproductive tissues.

Use the prescribed anti-sickness and hydration plan. Report fever, painful urination or blood in the urine.

Practical treatment guide

Your practical treatment plan

Four 21-day cycles: paclitaxel on Days 1, 8 and 15; carboplatin AUC 1.5 on Days 1, 8 and 15. Pembrolizumab 200 mg on Day 1 of each cycle (every three weeks). Four further cycles (cycles 5–8): doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide on Day 1 every 21 days. Pembrolizumab 200 mg on Day 1 of each AC cycle. After surgery: pembrolizumab 200 mg every 21 days for nine doses OR 400 mg every 42 days for five doses. These are alternative schedules, not consecutive courses. Progression preventing definitive surgery or unacceptable pembrolizumab toxicity changes this plan; postoperative treatment is not automatic.

STEP 1

Before the first visit

Blood tests, ECG and cardiac review, medicines review, fertility discussion where relevant and the consent conversation come first. Include the prescribed immune baseline checks.

STEP 2

Plan venous access

A cannula, PICC or implanted port may be used. If a port is chosen, a trained team inserts it in a separate procedure with local anaesthetic and sometimes sedation; the team explains fasting, blood-thinner and wound instructions.

STEP 3

Infusion day

Trained staff check and access the line, give the prescribed premedicines and infusions, and monitor for reactions. Tell the nurse immediately about pain, burning, rash or breathlessness.

STEP 4

Home and next review

This regimen does not routinely need a take-home infusion pump. Staff disconnect the infusion; a port is de-accessed when appropriate. Keep line-care and next-visit instructions.

Port insertion and removal

The line team explains placement, dressing care and when normal activities can resume. A port is accessed with a special needle during treatment. Removal of the implanted device is a separate planned procedure when no longer needed; do not remove or adjust it yourself.

PICC care and urgent line symptoms

A PICC usually needs regular dressing and flushing appointments even between treatments. Fever, redness, swelling, pain, leakage or damage requires prompt advice. Your hospital supplies the exact care instructions.

Visit time and transport

Visits include assessment, pharmacy preparation, premedicines, infusions and observation. The first visit and a change in phase can take longer. Ask about transport and any escort needed after sedation or line insertion.

Pembrolizumab

  • Immune inflammation of bowel, lungs, liver, kidneys or skin
  • Hormone-gland problems, sometimes permanent
  • Rare serious heart or neurological inflammation
  • Infusion reaction
  • Effects can first appear after treatment ends

Carboplatin

  • Low blood counts, infection and bleeding
  • Nausea, vomiting and fatigue
  • Allergic reaction, sometimes after previous exposure
  • Kidney or electrolyte changes

Paclitaxel

  • Low blood counts and infection
  • Numbness / tingling, sometimes lasting
  • Hair loss, muscle or joint pain
  • Infusion reaction
  • Mouth, bowel, skin and nail effects

Doxorubicin

  • Low counts, infection and mouth ulcers
  • Nausea, hair loss and fatigue
  • Heart damage, sometimes delayed
  • Severe tissue injury if the infusion leaks
  • Rare later blood cancers

Cyclophosphamide

  • Low counts and infection
  • Nausea, vomiting and hair loss
  • Bladder irritation / bleeding
  • Fertility effects and menopause
  • Rare later blood cancers

Support around treatment

Separate anti-sickness plans

The platinum/taxane phase and AC need separate prescriptions. Carboplatin/paclitaxel is moderate emetic risk on each weekly treatment day. AC uses its own high-risk plan.

Growth-factor support

The team decides whether growth-factor support is needed for this three-weekly AC regimen.

Checks before the next dose

FBC, renal and liver tests weekly during the carboplatin/paclitaxel phase, then before every AC cycle. Continue the required blood tests before each postoperative pembrolizumab cycle. Keep the thyroid and other immune-monitoring appointments.

After chemotherapy

After surgery: pembrolizumab 200 mg every 21 days for nine doses OR 400 mg every 42 days for five doses. These are alternative schedules, not consecutive courses. Progression preventing definitive surgery or unacceptable pembrolizumab toxicity changes this plan; postoperative treatment is not automatic.
