How this medicine supports your treatment

After early breast cancer treatment, endocrine therapy reduces the risk of recurrence. In advanced disease, the aim is to control cancer and symptoms. Your setting determines the duration and follow-up.

Hormone therapy · aromatase inhibitor

Anastrozole

Reduces the oestrogen available to stimulate cancer cells
Aromatase normally makes oestrogen↓The medicine blocks aromatase↓Less oestrogen reaches cancer-cell receptors

Anastrozole blocks aromatase, an enzyme that makes oestrogen in fat and other tissues after menopause. Less oestrogen is available to stimulate hormone-sensitive breast cancer cells. This also explains effects such as hot flushes, vaginal dryness and bone thinning. It does not reliably switch off functioning ovaries; any ovarian-suppression treatment needs a separate prescription and checks.

Tell the team if joint pain, hot flushes, vaginal dryness or sleep disturbance affects daily life. Bone thinning may cause no early symptoms, so keep the agreed bone-health reviews.

Individual risks and support

A functioning premenopausal ovarian state is not compatible with AI monotherapy. Severe hepatic impairment is not recommended by NCCP; moderate impairment requires caution. Confirm menopausal status rather than assuming that absent periods after chemotherapy mean permanent menopause. Any ovarian-suppression treatment must have its own prescribed schedule.

Heart checks

ECG or other heart tests depend on symptoms and personal risk; this medicine alone does not require a routine HER2-style echocardiogram schedule.

NCCP 00254 v5 · official regimen

Bone health

Offer baseline DXA when starting an adjuvant aromatase inhibitor if not already receiving adjuvant bisphosphonates; assess fracture risk and relevant previous results. Under the 2025 joint guidance, patients with a T-score above −2.0 and no additional risk factors generally have risk and bone density reassessed every one to two years. Patients on bone-protective treatment generally have BMD reassessed about every two years. Fracture, substantial bone loss or a change in risk can require earlier review. The clinician selects the interval and any bone treatment; these are not automatic yearly scans for everyone.

Your setting changes the pathway

Choose the pathway that your team has confirmed; these are not all extra treatments you must have.

Early-breast follow-up

After curative treatment, annual mammography is generally used for remaining breast tissue: both breasts after conservation or the opposite breast after unilateral mastectomy. The mastectomy side is not routinely mammographed. Routine CT, bone scans and tumour markers are not recommended for asymptomatic early-breast surveillance. New symptoms need assessment between appointments.

Pan-Asian adapted ESMO early breast cancer guidelines · NICE NG 101: early and locally advanced breast cancer

Surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy

In an early-breast pathway, chemotherapy, if indicated, precedes endocrine treatment. Surgery and its pathology guide adjuvant choices. Postoperative radiotherapy is usually delivered after recovery and any adjuvant chemotherapy; aromatase inhibitors may overlap radiotherapy under the agreed plan. Tamoxifen timing around radiotherapy is agreed by the treating teams. None of these additional treatments is automatically required by an endocrine prescription.

Pan-Asian adapted ESMO early breast cancer guidelines · eviQ 3650 v 6: adjuvant whole-breast radiotherapy

Advanced-disease scans

For advanced breast cancer, response assessment is generally every two to four months, adapted to disease pace, sites, symptoms and treatment. Indolent disease may be reviewed less often; new symptoms can prompt earlier scans. The team chooses the imaging method; this is not a universal three-month CT chest/abdomen/pelvis order.

Pan-Asian adapted ESMO metastatic breast cancer guidelines

Tumour markers

Tumour markers may supplement imaging and clinical assessment in advanced disease if they are informative, especially if elevated at baseline. They do not replace scans, and a marker change alone should not determine treatment failure. There is no universal fixed marker timetable.

ESO-ESMO ABC 5 consensus: tumour-marker interpretation

Why this endocrine treatment is offered

For hormone-sensitive early breast cancer, endocrine treatment helps reduce the chance of the cancer returning. For advanced disease, it can help control cancer and symptoms.

Your likely benefit depends on your cancer, previous treatment and health. The treating team explains why this option fits your plan.

Questions to ask

Do I have a treatment-free week?

Take anastrozole once daily continuously. There is no planned week off. The treatment setting determines the intended duration.

When are bloods, ECGs or echoes needed?

FBC, renal and liver profile. Confirm menopause; hormone tests may be needed if it is uncertain. Assess fracture risk and baseline bone density, particularly for adjuvant AI treatment. The NICE adjuvant baseline-DXA recommendation has an exception for patients already receiving adjuvant bisphosphonates. Assess symptoms, adherence and bone health at reviews. Repeat lipids and other blood tests when clinically indicated. Bone-density timing follows baseline results and fracture risk. There is no routine ECG or echo schedule for anastrozole alone. Review cardiovascular risk factors and investigate cardiac symptoms when indicated.

Which scans apply to me?

After curative treatment, annual mammography is generally used for remaining breast tissue: both breasts after conservation or the opposite breast after unilateral mastectomy. The mastectomy side is not routinely mammographed. Routine CT, bone scans and tumour markers are not recommended for asymptomatic early-breast surveillance. New symptoms need assessment between appointments. For advanced breast cancer, response assessment is generally every two to four months, adapted to disease pace, sites, symptoms and treatment. Indolent disease may be reviewed less often; new symptoms can prompt earlier scans. The team chooses the imaging method; this is not a universal three-month CT chest/abdomen/pelvis order.

What is my bone-health plan?

Offer baseline DXA when starting an adjuvant aromatase inhibitor if not already receiving adjuvant bisphosphonates; assess fracture risk and relevant previous results. Under the 2025 joint guidance, patients with a T-score above −2.0 and no additional risk factors generally have risk and bone density reassessed every one to two years. Patients on bone-protective treatment generally have BMD reassessed about every two years. Fracture, substantial bone loss or a change in risk can require earlier review. The clinician selects the interval and any bone treatment; these are not automatic yearly scans for everyone.

How long will treatment continue?

After early breast cancer treatment, the team sets the intended total endocrine treatment length, taking account of any earlier tamoxifen. A course of several years may later be reviewed for extension according to recurrence risk, tolerance and bone health. Advanced-disease treatment is reviewed according to benefit and tolerance. A review date is not an automatic stop date.

Do I need a port or home pump?

No port or home infusion pump is needed for this medicine itself. Any separately prescribed medicine has its own access requirements.

Can I use HRT, vaginal oestrogen or supplements?

Check with the oncology team before starting any hormone-containing treatment, including vaginal oestrogen, or any new medicine or supplement. Some can oppose this treatment or change how it works. The team can suggest symptom treatments and decide whether specialist review is needed.

What if periods return or menopause is uncertain?

Tell the team promptly about a return of periods or new vaginal bleeding. No periods after chemotherapy does not always mean the ovaries have permanently stopped working. Hormone blood tests may be needed. Ovarian suppression, if prescribed, has its own schedule and monitoring.

What should I do if symptoms make tablets difficult to continue?

Tell the team early about joint pain, hot flushes, sleep, vaginal or sexual symptoms, fatigue or missed tablets. Support, a supervised break or a change of medicine may be possible. Agree the plan with the prescriber rather than changing or stopping treatment yourself.

Do I need calcium and vitamin D?

The NCCP regimen advises daily calcium and vitamin D supplementation. Ask the team or pharmacist to confirm the suitable product and dose, taking account of your diet, blood results, kidney health and any supplements you already take. Bone-protective medicines and bone-density checks follow your individual fracture-risk plan.
