Understand your treatment

How your medicine works

Help control prostate cancer that has spread and progressed despite hormone treatment, where a qualifying BRCA1 or BRCA2 change is confirmed.

Targeted therapy: PARP inhibitor

Olaparib

Lynparza
Olaparib is a PARP inhibitor: it blocks proteins that help repair damaged deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA) and can trap them on the DNA. Cancer cells with a harmful BRCA1 or BRCA2 change already have difficulty using an important repair pathway. Blocking PARP can leave more damage than these cells can repair. Normal blood-forming cells can also be affected, which explains why blood counts and symptom checks remain important during targeted treatment.

STEP 3

Practical care

Swallow the prescribed tablets whole with or without food, once in the morning and once in the evening. Do not chew, crush, dissolve or divide them. The usual starting dose is two 150 mg tablets each time (300 mg twice daily); your own dose may be lower. Check both tablet strength and tablet count after every prescription change. Avoid grapefruit juice and check other grapefruit or bitter-orange products with your pharmacist.

STEP 4

Support medicines

Agree a written anti-sickness plan with your team, including what is prescribed regularly, what is for rescue, and when to call if it is not working. NCCP classifies oral olaparib as moderate-to-high sickness risk, but a fixed intravenous chemotherapy anti-sickness cycle does not apply. Some people need prescribed support and others do not need routine prevention. A stomach-acid medicine may be considered for symptoms under local policy. Give the pharmacist a complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal products. Some antibiotics, antifungals, seizure medicines and St John’s wort can change how much olaparib reaches your body. Even anti-sickness treatment needs checking: aprepitant can increase olaparib exposure. Do not start a borrowed support medicine, change the olaparib dose or restart a paused course yourself.

STEP 5

Before leaving

Confirm your next tests, dose, review date and verified daytime/out-of-hours contact numbers.

STEP 6

Blood-clot symptoms

A clot can develop in a vein or lung. Report a new painful swollen limb urgently. Chest pain, sudden severe breathlessness or collapse require emergency help. Clot risk is particularly relevant in this prostate setting; do not start an anticoagulant without the team’s assessment.

STEP 7

Genetic results and family questions

BRCA1 and BRCA2 help repair damaged DNA. A germline change is present in the body’s cells and may have implications for relatives. A tumour-only change does not by itself show that it is inherited. Ask which test you had, what the result means and whether genetic counselling is appropriate. A variant of uncertain significance is not automatically a treatment-qualifying result.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

Your prescribed sickness plan

Agree a written anti-sickness plan with your team, including what is prescribed regularly, what is for rescue, and when to call if it is not working. NCCP classifies oral olaparib as moderate-to-high sickness risk, but a fixed intravenous chemotherapy anti-sickness cycle does not apply. Some people need prescribed support and others do not need routine prevention. A stomach-acid medicine may be considered for symptoms under local policy.

Check every new medicine

Give the pharmacist a complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal products. Some antibiotics, antifungals, seizure medicines and St John’s wort can change how much olaparib reaches your body. Even anti-sickness treatment needs checking: aprepitant can increase olaparib exposure. Do not start a borrowed support medicine, change the olaparib dose or restart a paused course yourself.

Blood counts and extra support

Blood counts check red cells that carry oxygen, white cells that help fight infection and platelets that help stop bleeding. Low counts can lead to a pause, a lower prescribed dose, a transfusion or further tests. Persistent or unexplained changes sometimes need specialist bone-marrow tests to investigate myelodysplastic syndrome or acute myeloid leukaemia (MDS/AML). These are serious conditions; their risk differs by treatment setting and previous therapy. A treatment pause is not itself a diagnosis. There is no automatic white-cell injection, antibiotic or steroid course for everyone.

Eating, drinking and daily life

Ask for help early if nausea, mouth soreness, taste changes or poor appetite affect eating. Keep the team informed about weight loss or difficulty drinking. Fatigue may need a dose or blood-count review. Do not drive or use machinery if dizzy, weak or very tired.

A treatment pause

A prescribed pause can give blood counts or symptoms time to improve. It does not mean treatment has failed. Keep the agreed tests and ask exactly when the team will review restarting. Never take catch-up tablets.

Fertility and contraception

Use a condom during treatment and for three months after the last dose when having sex with a partner who is pregnant or could become pregnant. A partner who could become pregnant should also use highly effective contraception. Do not donate sperm during treatment or for three months afterwards; discuss fertility preservation before treatment if relevant.

Your continuing hormone treatment

Continue ADT, which keeps testosterone low, unless you have had surgical castration. Olaparib targets DNA repair rather than replacing hormone suppression. Abiraterone and other androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) act on hormone signalling; adding or switching them needs a separate evidence-based plan. Radiotherapy can treat selected sites or relieve symptoms, with timing agreed by the team.

Treatment reference · 04

Benefits, uncertainty and your choices

Evidence in this setting

In the BRCA1/2-mutated subgroup of PROfound, 160 patients received olaparib or the investigator’s choice of abiraterone or enzalutamide after progression on a new hormonal agent. Median radiological progression-free survival was 9.8 versus 3.0 months (hazard ratio 0.22; 95% confidence interval 0.15–0.32); median overall survival was 20.1 versus 14.4 months (hazard ratio 0.63; 0.42–0.95). These subgroup analyses were not controlled for multiple comparisons. They do not compare every modern alternative or establish the separate olaparib-plus-abiraterone regimen.
