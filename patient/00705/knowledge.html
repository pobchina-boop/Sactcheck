When to get help

Emergency: call 112 / 999

Severe breathing difficulty, collapse, seizure or severe chest pain: call emergency services. Do not wait for an online answer.

Contact your oncology service immediately

Temperature 37.5°C or higher, 36°C or lower, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell — even with a normal temperature. Call the oncology team promptly for fever, sudden illness, diarrhoea, painful mouth, reduced drinking, new breathlessness or immune symptoms. Chest pain during or after fluorouracil needs urgent assessment. Severe chest pain, breathing difficulty, collapse or a seizure is an emergency. Early severe fluorouracil symptoms can occur despite dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (a medicine-processing enzyme) (DPD) testing.

Follow the urgent contact instructions from your treating hospital. If you cannot reach the team, seek urgent medical assessment. Outside clinic hours, use your instructed out-of-hours pathway or the emergency department.

This page does not diagnose symptoms or decide whether it is safe to stay at home. You do not need to know which medicine caused a symptom before calling.

Understand your treatment

How each part of treatment works

Pembrolizumab and chemotherapy aim to control head and neck cancer that has spread or returned and cannot be removed. Your team explains the expected benefit, uncertainty and alternatives for you.

PD-1 checkpoint immunotherapy

Pembrolizumab

Keytruda
Releases an immune brake

Pembrolizumab blocks PD-1, a signal that can limit the activity of immune cells. This can help them attack cancer, but can also cause inflammation in healthy organs during treatment or after it ends.

PD-1 blocked↓Immune cells more active↓Healthy organs can inflame

What to watch for
Report new diarrhoea, cough, breathlessness, rash, headache, unusual weakness, thirst or changes in urine promptly.

Sources: nccp · keytruda

Platinum chemotherapy

Carboplatin

Carboplatin
Damages cancer-cell deoxyribonucleic acid (the cell’s genetic material) (DNA)

Carboplatin interferes with DNA repair and cell division. Its dose is calculated from kidney function. Blood counts can fall, including platelets that help stop bleeding. It is a different prescription from cisplatin.

DNA damage↓Cell division disrupted↓Cancer growth reduced

What to watch for
Report fever, unusual bruising or bleeding, marked tiredness or an infusion reaction.

Sources: nccp

Antimetabolite chemotherapy

Fluorouracil

5-FU
Disrupts production of DNA and RNA

Fluorouracil interferes with the materials cells need to make DNA and RNA. A pump gives it over the prescribed days. DPD is an enzyme that helps break it down; testing before treatment reduces risk but cannot prevent every severe reaction.

Cell building blocks disrupted↓Cells cannot divide normally↓Pump provides prescribed exposure

What to watch for
Report severe diarrhoea, mouth sores, chest pain, sudden confusion, sore palms or soles, or unexpectedly early severe symptoms.

Sources: nccp

Up to 6 cycles · every 3 weeks

Combination treatment

123456

Pembrolizumab 200 mg is infused over 30 minutes, followed by carboplatin AUC 5 over 30 minutes on Day 1. Fluorouracil is prescribed as 1,000 mg/m² each day by 22-hour infusion on Days 1–4, or 2,000 mg/m² over 48 hours twice. Staff confirm pump changes and disconnection from the actual connection time. The planned combination limit is six cycles; toxicity may change the plan earlier.

Every 3 weeks · ongoing review

Pembrolizumab alone

Ongoing maintenance

Pembrolizumab 200 mg over 30 minutes every 21 days continues after the combination phase when appropriate, until progression or unacceptable toxicity under this regimen. A six-cycle chemotherapy record is not a six-cycle total course; the protocol does not impose an automatic 24-month stop.

Dates and treatment decisions come from your team. A delayed or omitted dose does not count as treatment received.

Read the whole cycle as text

Day 1: Infusions and pump connection

Receive the Day 1 medicines after safety checks; trained staff start fluorouracil. Confirm the exact pump schedule, support medicines and contact plan.

Day 2–4: Fluorouracil continues

Four 22-hour infusions or two 48-hour infusions as prescribed; changes and completion are arranged by staff. Do not adjust the pump. Call for unexpected pump progress or symptoms.

Day 5–21: Recovery and review

No planned chemotherapy after completion of the pump in this reference cycle. Pembrolizumab immune effects can occur at any time. Report symptoms early and arrange the next required tests.

Your treatment, step by step

Your port and treatment pump

From preparing the line to coming home after disconnection. Your team arranges the appointments and shows you what to do.

1 · Before treatment

Agree the access device

The team discusses a port or peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) and trains you in its care.

2 · Before Day 1

Confirm the actual pump plan

Four daily 22-hour infusions or two 48-hour infusions may be prescribed. Confirm all change and disconnection appointments.

3 · Day 1

Trained staff connect fluorouracil

After the Day 1 infusions, staff start the prescribed pump. Do not calculate or alter its rate.

4 · Days 1–4

Follow the chosen infusion schedule

Daily pump changes or a change after the first 48-hour infusion are arranged by the unit. Protect the line and report leaks or unexpected progress.

5 · At prescribed completion

Professional disconnection

Timing is based on actual connection and infusion duration. Keep the appointment; do not disconnect or dispose of the pump yourself.

6 · Between cycles

Continue line care

The access device may stay in place. Follow its dressing, flushing and infection precautions, including during maintenance if it remains.

Port or PICC: what is the difference?

A port is a small chamber beneath the skin, usually on the chest, attached to a tube ending in a large vein. A special needle goes through the skin into the port when it is used. A PICC enters a vein in the upper arm; a short section remains outside under a dressing. Both can deliver a home infusion. Your team helps choose the device and teaches its care.

Before insertion and during recovery

Tell the team about allergies, blood thinners, infections and any other cancer medicines. Follow the instructions about eating, drinking and transport; do not stop medicines yourself. Port placement uses local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation or general anaesthetic. The team explains the procedure and risks, including infection, bleeding, clots and insertion complications. Ask how to manage discomfort, protect the wound and resume activity. Report increasing pain, redness, swelling, discharge or fever.

Connection: what the nurse does

The nurse checks the line and treatment, secures the connection and dressing, and starts the pump. Before leaving, you should have the disconnection location and time, contact numbers including out of hours, and the written spill instructions. Ask to be shown what normal pump progress looks like. You do not need to calculate a rate.

Carrying it, sleeping and washing

Use the supplied carrier and the position recommended for your pump. Keep tubing free from pulling and kinks. Ask how to place the pump safely for sleep and how to protect the line and dressing when washing. Do not immerse it or swim. Avoid direct heat and cold. An elastomeric pump has a balloon that shrinks slowly and may have no alarm; an electronic pump has different instructions. Follow the instructions for the device you actually receive.

Disconnection is not port removal

Keep the appointment even if the pump looks empty, unless the team changes the plan. Staff check the pump and line and arrange safe disposal. After flushing and other line care as required, the port needle may be removed and a small dressing applied. The implanted port stays in place until a separate removal procedure is arranged. A PICC remains in place and needs scheduled dressing and flushing care.

Leakage, disconnection or unexpected pump progress

Call the team immediately if the pump leaks, is damaged, finishes unexpectedly early, appears not to run, or the line disconnects. Follow the emergency or spill steps you have been specifically taught, including use of a clamp if instructed. Do not reconnect, change settings or disconnect the pump yourself. Keep other people away from leaked medicine. If medicine touches skin, rinse it off and wash with soap and water, then seek advice.

Pain, swelling or feeling unwell

Contact oncology immediately for pain or swelling around the line, a wet/loose dressing, redness, fever, shivering or suddenly feeling unwell. A painful swollen arm or neck needs urgent advice. Severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty or collapse: call 112 / 999. Do not wait for pump disconnection or the next clinic visit.

The drawings show general steps, not instructions for inserting or accessing a line. Your hospital confirms its own arrangements and the care instructions for your device.

Make the plan practical

Support around the medicines

One written medicine plan

Ask for one schedule showing prevention medicines, rescue treatment and what to do if symptoms persist. Do not add, double or restart medicines from an old prescription.

Immune effects and maintenance

Pembrolizumab can cause inflammation in healthy organs even after chemotherapy has finished. Keep the alert card and report new symptoms promptly. Steroids or hormone replacement may be needed for specific complications; do not start or stop them without the treating team.

Food, fluids and weight

A dietitian can tailor food texture, supplements and calorie or protein needs. Tell the team early if eating or drinking becomes difficult; do not wait for substantial weight loss. Fluid targets depend on kidney and heart health.

Oral and dental care

Use the mouth-care plan provided by the team and tell them about ulcers, thrush, bleeding or pain. Inform your dentist about cancer treatment. Before any dental procedure, confirm timing with the oncology team.

Swallowing and communication

A speech and language therapist can assess swallowing and advise exercises or safer food and drink textures. Follow the individual assessment; a general guide cannot decide which textures are safe for you.

Fertility, pregnancy and medicines

Discuss fertility and pregnancy precautions before treatment and the medicine-specific duration of contraception afterwards. Ask about breastfeeding. Bring a full medicine, supplement and allergy list; check before adding over-the-counter or herbal products.

Treatment reference · 04

Between visits: symptoms and medicines

Use the agreed home plan and contact pathway.

Symptoms have no fixed calendar

Some effects appear soon after treatment and others build over weeks. Immune effects may begin during maintenance or after the last pembrolizumab dose. Do not wait for the next appointment if a symptom is new, severe or worsening.

Sources: nccp · hse

A medicine plan you can use

Keep prevention, rescue and regular medicines clearly separated on one written list. Record what you have taken and the response, so the team can adjust it. Do not double a missed dose unless your prescriber tells you to.

When self-care is not enough

Call the oncology team promptly for fever, sudden illness, diarrhoea, painful mouth, reduced drinking, new breathlessness or immune symptoms. Treatment reference · 05

Mouth, teeth and jaw care

Dental preparation and gentle daily care reduce avoidable problems.

Before treatment

Arrange dental assessment and oral care with the team. If radiotherapy is planned separately, the dental and radiation teams decide any necessary treatment and healing time before it starts.

Sources: oral

During treatment

Follow the prescribed brushing, fluoride, rinse and pain-relief advice. Report ulcers, infection, new bleeding, difficulty wearing dentures or worsening jaw opening. Avoid starting a new mouthwash without checking if the mouth is sore.

Sources: oral

If you have had head and neck radiation

Earlier head and neck radiation can affect saliva, teeth and healing long afterwards. Tell any dentist about it before invasive treatment. If you have not had radiation, follow the oral-care advice appropriate to your current treatment.

Sources: oral

Eating, drinking and maintaining strength

Recognising an intake problem

Record weight and report food or drink becoming harder to manage. Pain, taste change, dry mouth or nausea can each need a different response; a dietitian can adapt the plan to you.

If ordinary food is not enough

The team may suggest food fortification, supplements or tube feeding when intake or swallowing safety requires it. A tube is a support option, not a judgement about how well you have tried to eat. Training and follow-up are needed.

Fluid needs are individual

Ask for a realistic drinking plan that accounts for kidney/heart problems and any feeding tube. Persistent vomiting, dizziness, markedly reduced urine or inability to drink needs urgent contact.

Diarrhoea needs the correct response

Fluorouracil and pembrolizumab can both cause diarrhoea through different mechanisms. Contact the team early rather than assuming it is ordinary chemotherapy diarrhoea; use rescue medicine only under the written plan.

Understanding the evidence

KEYNOTE-048 studied first-line treatment for recurrent or metastatic squamous head and neck cancer.

The original 2019 report supported pembrolizumab with platinum and fluorouracil in this setting. The chemotherapy group included cisplatin or carboplatin; it does not provide a separate randomised survival estimate for this specific platinum choice.

Sources: nccp · key048

Prepare for the conversation

Questions worth asking

What is this treatment trying to achieve for me?

Pembrolizumab and chemotherapy aim to control head and neck cancer that has spread or returned and cannot be removed. Your team explains the expected benefit, uncertainty and alternatives for you.

How does the schedule change?

Every 21 days: pembrolizumab and carboplatin on Day 1; fluorouracil over Days 1–4. Up to 6 combination cycles, then pembrolizumab alone while appropriate. Every dose depends on safety and benefit review.

Which tests are required?

Blood count, kidney and liver function, glucose and thyroid tests; hepatitis B surface antigen/core antibody and hepatitis C; tumour PD-L1 CPS and DPD assessment before fluorouracil. Review previous immune problems, transplants, medicines and infection. An isotope GFR measurement is preferred when assessing kidney function for carboplatin dosing; further kidney testing is arranged when indicated. Audiology is considered when indicated. Blood count, kidney/liver tests and glucose each cycle; thyroid tests every 3–6 weeks and sooner if symptoms require. Ask about bowel, breathing, skin, hormone and other immune symptoms before every dose. A normal blood test does not exclude immune toxicity.

When should I call?

Call the oncology team promptly for fever, sudden illness, diarrhoea, painful mouth, reduced drinking, new breathlessness or immune symptoms. Chest pain during or after fluorouracil needs urgent assessment. Severe chest pain, breathing difficulty, collapse or a seizure is an emergency. Early severe fluorouracil symptoms can occur despite DPD testing.

What if a treatment is held?

The team records the reason and recovery or tests needed before restarting. Do not count an omitted dose as completed or restart from an old plan.

Have all my prescribed steroids and other medicines that suppress the immune system been reviewed?

Bring a complete medicine list, including treatments from other clinics. Your oncology team checks whether steroids or other medicines that suppress the immune system affect the treatment plan. Do not stop prescribed medicines yourself.

Try explaining the plan back: Explain which treatment phase you are in, what happens next, which tests are due and which symptoms would make you call today. Ask the team to clarify anything you cannot explain.
